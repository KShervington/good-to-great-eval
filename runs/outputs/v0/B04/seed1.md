# Brightwell Housewares: Good to Great

## One-screen verdict

**Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** *Brightwell makes the few everyday home products in each of its core categories that households trust most and retailers can't drop, and it earns more profit on each of them than anyone else.*

**Weakest flywheel link:** turning brand strength into **price**. Sales growth came almost entirely from volume. The largest customer's share rose from 11% to 13% to 14%. Lower-priced rivals sell in the same aisles. The brand may be famous without being able to command a premium.

**Top three stop-doing items:**
1. Drop the public goal of 15% compounded annual growth in sales and EPS.
2. Stop entering a new category every 12–18 months.
3. Stop launching roughly one product a day. Cap launches and tie each one to retiring an existing SKU.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** The 15% goal is not being met organically. Sales grew about 8% a year over the last two years. At that pace Brightwell reaches about **$2.9B** in five years, while the target needs about **$3.9B**. The ways to close a ~$1B gap are acquisitions, new categories, more SKUs and concessions to the biggest customer, and each of those erodes greatness. Year −1's 29% earnings jump followed a year of nearly flat earnings (+0.9%). That pattern fits resin relief better than a structural gain `[ASSUMPTION]`.

**Missing numbers that would change this answer:**
- Order-fill and on-time rates.
- SKU count and profit by SKU and by line, especially the tail.
- Price realization and terms with the largest customer.
- Resin cost sensitivity and hedging.
- Category share trends against low-price rivals.
- Profitability of the toy and leisure subsidiaries.

Greatness potential: 30/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

The great Brightwell is smaller in scope and bigger in each thing it does. It competes in a deliberately limited set of home categories where it holds the #1 brand. In each one it sells fewer, longer-lived "hero" products that are clearly better designed and more durable than low-priced copies. It measures itself on profit per SKU and on being the most reliable supplier its retailers have, not on launch counts or a compounding sales target. Plants run fewer changeovers, orders ship complete and on time, and retailers give it shelf space and price respect because its categories make them money. Its earnings hold up through resin cycles because its pricing does. Its success no longer depends on the turnaround CEO's machine or on a single discount chain. Growth will probably run below 15%, but it will be steadier and better quality.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

| Circle | Candidates considered | Kept | Explicitly *not* best-in-world at |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Best in the world at** | (a) volume of new-product innovation; (b) designing durable, useful molded-plastic products for everyday household chores under a trusted brand; (c) being the category leader for mass retailers in core housewares; (d) toys and leisure; (e) institutional and janitorial products | **(b) + (c)**: the trusted standard product in core home categories, backed by category leadership at retail | Toys and leisure (acquired, not built); lowest price; launch volume as an end in itself; new categories on a schedule; international consumer markets as a near-term engine `[ASSUMPTION]` |

| **Deeply passionate about** | Product invention ("eleven ideas from one museum visit"); the brand; disciplined execution (the turnaround legacy) | **Making useful everyday things work better.** This redirects the existing passion from *newness* to *usefulness* | Novelty for its own sake |

**Why profit per SKU:** the binding constraints appear to be manufacturing and logistics complexity and retailer shelf space. Profit per SKU penalizes proliferation directly and rewards hero products. Its weakness is that it can be gamed by over-pruning, so it is paired with share and fill metrics in the scorecard.

**Confirmation tests:**
- **Explains past wins?** Partly. The turnaround worked by *cutting* four of eight divisions and focusing. The brand leads in core categories. The launch machine was built afterwards and so far is untested against the denominator.
- **Rules things out?** Yes: new categories on a schedule, out-of-core acquisitions, the product-a-day rate and dated international targets.
- **Uncomfortable?** Very. It contradicts three of the four stated public objectives.
- **Evidence that would confirm it:** the SKU profitability map shows profit concentrated in a minority of SKUs in core categories. Fill rates improve as the tail is cut. Gross margin holds when resin rises again.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   │                ▼
   │    2. Higher sales and economic profit per SKU   ◄── denominator

   │                │
   │                ▼

   │       (shelf space, assortment influence, price respect)
   │                │
   │                ▼

   │       and pass through resin cost swings   ◄── WEAKEST LINK
   │                │
   │                ▼
   └─── 6. Cash reinvested in design, tooling and quality for the next hero products
```

**The loop closes:** cash from item 6 funds item 1. **PASS on closure.** It is CONDITIONAL on link 4→5, which the evidence currently fails to support, and on link 3, where there is no data.

**Current push (next one or two turns):**
1. **Cut the tail.** Build the SKU profitability map and retire the bottom slice. This lifts item 2 and eases item 3.
2. **Prove service before asking for price.** Measure and fix order-fill. Then use category data with retailers to defend price and set up resin pass-through.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop promising 15% compounded growth in sales and EPS.** Replace it with economic profit per SKU, ROIC and margin stability through the resin cycle.
2. **Stop entering a new category every 12–18 months.** Enter a new category only when it passes all three circles, with no calendar.
3. **Stop launching about one product a day.** Set an annual launch cap with a one-in, one-out rule (or two-out while the tail is pruned).
4. **Stop using "one-third of sales from products under five years old" as a goal.** If met, it implies about $650M from about 1,800 launches, or roughly **$0.36M of sales per new product** `[ASSUMPTION: derived from stated rate and target]`. That is a complexity factory.
5. **Stop accepting self-graded success** ("nine in ten meet targets"). Every launch gets a full-cost P&L covering tooling, inventory, changeovers and fill impact, reviewed after 18 months by finance, not by the sponsor.
6. **Stop acquiring outside core home categories.** Run a strategic review of the toy and leisure subsidiaries.
7. **Stop chasing a dated international share target.**
8. **Stop treating the "Most Admired" ranking as evidence.** It grades past results and reputation, not current fill rates or SKU economics.
9. **Stop managing to the streaks** (42 years of record sales, 40 quarters of earnings growth). Year −2 earnings rose only 0.9%, so the streak is fragile and creates pressure to protect it rather than face reality.

## 7. Core ideology and the BHAG

**Core values** (proposed, `[ASSUMPTION]`, to be confirmed by leadership):
1. **Useful and durable over new.** A product earns its place by lasting.
2. **Quality is never traded for volume.**
3. **Be the supplier retailers can count on.** Complete, on time, every time.
4. **Face the numbers.** Every product carries a true-cost P&L.

**Core purpose:** make everyday household work simpler and more orderly with products that last.

**BHAG (20-year horizon):** *In every category Brightwell competes in, be #1 in consumer preference and #1 in profit per SKU, and be named the best supplier by every major retailer.*

**Clock-building mechanisms:**
- SKU governance rule (one-in, one-out) built into the planning calendar.
- Board-level monthly metrics: order-fill, price realization, profit per SKU and customer concentration.
- Executive pay tied to ROIC and profit per SKU, not sales growth.
- An independent lead director with an annual "challenge the strategy" session.
- A succession bench of at least two internal CEO-ready candidates, reviewed annually.
- Finance-led post-launch reviews at 18 months.
