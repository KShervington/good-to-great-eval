# Good to Great: Brightwell Housewares, Inc.

## One-screen verdict

**Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):**
*Brightwell makes the everyday plastic and rubber products American households choose by name, designed to last and always in stock, and it grows only where that name earns a premium on every pound of resin.*

**Weakest flywheel link:** turning consumer pull into **price realization with retailers**. Growth has come from volume, not price. The largest customer's share is rising every year (11% → 13% → 14%). Competitors are cheaper. No evidence yet shows the brand can pass through cost.

**Top 3 stop-doing:**
1. Stop promising 15% compound growth in sales, earnings and EPS.
2. Stop entering a new category every 12–18 months, and freeze acquisitions outside core housewares.
3. Stop measuring innovation by count (one new product a day, one-third of sales from new products).

**Most urgent brutal fact:** The company has publicly promised **15% a year** while its sales grow about **8–9%**.
- Roughly **a third** of the last two years' sales growth came from **one discount chain**.
- Earnings move with **resin**, which the company doesn't control. Earnings rose 0.9% in Year −2 as resin rose, then 29% in Year −1 as resin eased.
- The gap between the promise and the engine is what usually drives an undisciplined pursuit of more.

**Missing numbers that would change this answer:**
- Order-fill and on-time rates
- SKU count and profit by line, including cost-to-serve
- Resin hedging and pass-through terms
- Category market share
- Net price realization with the largest customer
- Total shareholder return against the market

Greatness potential: 25/100

*Evidence note: this run had no search, memory or past-run access. The research and baseline-lookup steps were skipped. Everything below comes from the dossier alone, and inferences are marked `[ASSUMPTION]`. Seven of the eleven gates are CONDITIONAL, which is more than half. They share two root causes: (1) growth and newness targets that are outrunning the economic engine, and (2) a blind spot on operating and unit economics (fill rate, SKU profit, resin exposure).*

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Brightwell is **narrower and harder-working** than today's company.
- It owns the categories where its name earns a real premium over lower-priced molded plastics: kitchen and food storage, home organization and storage, trash and cleaning, and their institutional counterparts.
- It ships complete and on time, every time, so retailers treat it as their most dependable vendor, not just their best-known one.
- Each new product has to beat a profit-per-pound hurdle and retire a weaker SKU before it gets a mold.
- It manages resin as a known cycle, through indexing, hedging and cost-out, so a commodity swing doesn't decide its earnings.
- It grows at whatever rate the engine supports, likely high single digits `[ASSUMPTION]`, and compounds through margin and reliability, not through category sprawl, toys, or international expansion pursued for its own sake.
- It is great because the turnaround's discipline outlasted the turnaround CEO, not because of a magazine ranking.

## 3. The flywheel (Gate 8: CONDITIONAL)

```
   ┌─► 1. Fewer, better, longer-lasting products in core categories
   │        (every launch retires a weaker SKU; must clear per-pound hurdle)
   │                         │
   │                         ▼
   │   2. Consumers ask for Brightwell by name (pull, durability reputation)
   │                         │
   │                         ▼
   │   3. Retailers give winners shelf space AND accept a price premium  ◄── WEAKEST LINK
   │        (because it sells through and ships complete and on time)
   │                         │
   │                         ▼
   │   4. Higher gross profit per pound of resin  (the denominator)
   │                         │
   │                         ▼
   │   5. Reinvest in molding productivity, design, service reliability,
   │        and resin risk management
   └─────────────────────────┘
```

**Current push (next one to two turns):**
1. **Know the unit economics.** Build profit and cost-to-serve per SKU and per pound of resin, then prune the tail.
2. **Make reliability a non-negotiable.** Measure, publish internally, and guarantee fill rate to the top customers. This is the lever that strengthens link 3.

**Doom-loop check:** early signs are present, though not yet a full doom loop.
- **Grasping for growth:** the 15% target is above the recent run rate.
- **New directions stacked up:** a new category every 12–18 months plus an international push.
- **Acquisitions outside the core:** toys and leisure.
- **Velocity treated as momentum:** a product a day.

**CONDITIONAL.** Condition: the loop closes on paper, but link 3 has no evidence behind it. It becomes PASS with documented price realization at the top five customers and a measured fill rate at or above target.

## 5. Stop-doing list (Gate 6: CONDITIONAL)

1. **Stop promising 15% compound growth** in sales, earnings and EPS. Replace the promise with hedgehog metrics.
2. **Stop entering a new category every 12–18 months.** New categories need a hedgehog case, not a calendar slot.
3. **Stop counting launches as innovation.** Retire the "one-third of sales from products under five years old" target and the product-a-day cadence as goals.
4. **Stop adding SKUs net.** One in, at least one out.
5. **Stop acquiring outside core molded housewares.** Review the toy and leisure holdings.
6. **Stop chasing international share for its own sake.**
7. **Stop competing on price for commodity items** against low-cost molders. Cede or exit them.
8. **Stop letting the largest customer's growth stand in for consumer pull.** Watch growth excluding that customer, currently about 6–7%.
9. **Stop treating the record streaks** (42 years of record sales, 40 straight quarters of earnings growth) **as something to protect.** A streak is not a strategy.

**Non-negotiable disciplines (fanatical consistency):**
- Ship complete and on time.
- Every product is built to last.
- Every launch clears the profit-per-pound hurdle.
- Bad numbers reach the board monthly.

**Framework (freedom within boundaries):**
- **Boundaries:** core categories only, molded plastic and rubber, a per-pound profit hurdle, a fill-rate floor, and one-in-one-out for SKUs.
- **Freedom:** inside those boundaries, product teams choose designs, materials engineering and channel tactics.

**Bureaucracy check:** if approval layers multiply to police SKU sprawl, the targets are the problem, not the people. Fix the incentives first.

**CONDITIONAL.** Condition: today's culture rewards volume and newness. It becomes PASS when the board formally replaces the 15% and category-cadence targets.
