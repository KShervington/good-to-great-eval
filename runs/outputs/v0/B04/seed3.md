# Brightwell Housewares, Inc.: The Great Version

## Verdict (one screen)

**Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** *Brightwell makes the everyday household plastic products that American homes trust to last, and it competes only where that trust earns a premium per pound of resin.*

**Weakest flywheel link:** brand trust turning into **price**. The last two years of growth came from volume, with little from price. Meanwhile the largest customer grew from 11% to 14% of sales, and lower-priced rivals are in the same categories. If the brand can't command a premium, the flywheel becomes a volume treadmill.

**Top three things to stop:**
1. **Stop promising 15% compounded growth.** Sales are growing about 8%. The gap will be filled with something worse.
2. **Stop the launch and category quotas.** That means one product a day, a new category every 12–18 months, and one-third of sales from products under five years old.
3. **Stop buying companies to fill the growth gap.**

**Most urgent brutal fact:** growth has roughly halved, from about 15% a year over the past 13 years to about 8% in each of the last two years, but the 15% target hasn't changed. At 15%, sales would reach about $3.9B in five years. At 8%, they would reach about $2.9B. A roughly **$1B gap** creates pressure for gap-filling acquisitions, rushed categories and price concessions. Year −1's 29% earnings jump on 8% sales growth looks like easing resin prices, not a stronger engine.

**Missing numbers that would change this answer:** order-fill and on-time trends; SKU count and profit by line; Brightwell's price premium over lower-priced rivals; margin on the largest customer versus the average; resin hedge position.

Greatness potential: 35/100

> **Evidence note:** No search, memory or prior runs were available, so the competitor, resin and retailer research steps were skipped. Every fact here comes from the dossier, and every inference is marked `[ASSUMPTION]`. Collins's caveat applies: these are disciplined hypotheses to test, not guarantees.

> **Verdict pattern:** 6 of 11 gates are CONDITIONAL, which is more than half. Two shared root problems explain most of them:
> 1. **A growth commitment larger than the engine can deliver** (15% target against about 8% actual).
> 2. **Missing operating truth.** Fill rate, SKU profitability and price premium are not measured or reported.

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Brightwell grows more slowly in revenue, and more steadily, than its targets currently demand. In return, it earns more per pound of resin every year through the resin cycle. It carries fewer SKUs, and each one earns its place on a profit-per-pound hurdle. Every launch replaces something weaker. Retailers, including the one that now buys 14% of sales, keep Brightwell because shoppers ask for it by name and because its orders arrive complete and on time. Brightwell does not keep its shelf space by giving up price. Its designers aim at durability and real household problems, not at launch counts. It enters new categories only when the three-circle test says it can lead, and it leaves categories where it can't. Its success is measured by through-cycle margin and service, not by record streaks or magazine rankings. The machine runs the same way whoever is CEO.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence:** *Brightwell makes the everyday household plastic products that American homes trust to last, and it competes only where that trust earns a premium per pound of resin.*

| Circle | Candidates | Kept | Ruled out (cannot be best at) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Best in the world at** | (a) launching new products at high volume; (b) durable, well-designed molded household goods under a trusted brand; (c) low-cost plastics manufacturing; (d) toys and leisure; (e) international housewares | **(b)**, with the "world" defined narrowly as U.S. mass-channel housewares where it already leads | (a) is a quota, not a capability, and launches are easy to copy. (c) belongs to lower-priced rivals. (e) has no evidence. (d) is unproven and under review. |
| **Economic engine** | profit per SKU; profit per customer; profit per shelf foot; **profit per pound of resin** | **Profit per pound of resin processed** `[ASSUMPTION]` | Revenue growth itself is not an engine. |
| **Passion** | novelty and invention (current form); making useful, lasting things | **Designing products that solve real household problems and last** `[ASSUMPTION]` | Passion for novelty as an end in itself. The museum anecdote suggests ideas are abundant, but abundance is not the scarce input. |

**Why this denominator:** resin is the main input, and it is a commodity Brightwell doesn't control. Profit per pound captures both things that matter at once: whether the brand earns a premium, and whether the factories and portfolio use resin productively. It also exposes products that are really commodity plastic with a logo on them. **Alternate:** profit per active SKU, if resin volumes can't be measured cleanly.

**Confirmation tests:**
- *Does it explain past wins?* Partly. The turnaround cut the company back to core divisions and built leading brands.
- *Does it rule things out?* Yes: quota categories, commodity SKUs, price-led volume and gap-filling acquisitions.
- *Is it uncomfortable?* Yes. It means accepting slower reported revenue growth in public.
- **Evidence that would confirm it:**
  - The top quartile of lines by profit per pound turns out to be brand-led, durability-led lines.
  - Brightwell holds a measurable price premium over lower-priced rivals in its core categories `[ASSUMPTION: about 10% or more]`.
  - The premium survives a resin upswing.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   ┌─► 1. Design fewer, more durable products for real household problems
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │   2. Products perform for years → household trust in the brand
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │   3. Shoppers ask for Brightwell by name → retailers need it in the category
   │            │
   │            ▼  ◄── WEAKEST LINK (unproven: growth is volume, not price)
   │   4. Full-price velocity + premium → PROFIT PER POUND OF RESIN
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │   5. Reinvest in design, molds, and reliable order-fill
   │            │
   │            ▼
   └── 6. Reliable service + category leadership → retailer partnership and shelf
              (category insight feeds back to step 1)
```

**Current push (next one or two turns):**
1. **Measure link 4.** Find the real price premium and profit per pound by line and by customer.
2. **Prune to strengthen links 1 and 5.** Cut the SKUs below the hurdle, then move the freed design, mold and logistics capacity onto the top lines and onto fill rate.

**Doom-loop check:** Brightwell is not in a doom loop yet, but the preconditions are present:
- **Growth targets above the engine's capacity.** The company needs about 15% and is producing about 8%.
- **Programs that push volume.** One launch a day, a category every 12–18 months, and an international quota.
- **Acquisitions as a growth tool.** There are three of them, including one two years ago.
- **Signs of streak-protection.** Forty consecutive quarters of earnings growth would have had to survive Year −2's earnings, which were up only 0.9% for the full year. The streak held, if it held at all, by a hair.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop promising 15% compounded growth** in sales, earnings and EPS. Replace it with the scorecard in Section 8.
2. **Stop entering a new category every 12–18 months on schedule.** Enter only after a three-circle test.
3. **Stop treating launch count as success.** That includes "one a day," "nine of ten meet targets" and the one-third-from-new-products quota.
4. **Stop gap-filling acquisitions.** Pause them until the SKU and resin review is done. Any later deal must pass the Hedgehog test with the board.
5. **Stop buying volume from the largest customer with price.** Set a profit-per-pound floor per account and a concentration watch level `[ASSUMPTION: 15%]`.
6. **Stop carrying SKUs below the profit-per-pound hurdle.** For every new product, retire an existing one.
7. **Stop forcing international share by a date.** Expand abroad only where the brand premium travels.
8. **Stop protecting record streaks** (42 years of record sales, 40 quarters of earnings growth) as goals in themselves.

## 7. Core ideology and the BHAG

- **Core values** `[ASSUMPTION, to be validated with leadership]`:
  1. Products that last.
  2. Useful design over novelty.
  3. Keep promises to customers: complete and on time.
  4. Bad news travels fast.
- **Core purpose:** make everyday home life simpler with products people can count on.
- **BHAG (25-year horizon):** be the brand households reach for first whenever something has to last. That means #1 in consumer preference in every core category in North America and in two other major regions, each entered only after the brand premium is proven there.
- **Mechanisms that keep this going without the current CEO:**
  - The SKU hurdle rule, plus one-in-one-out for new products.
  - A red-flag dashboard reviewed by the board: fill rate, profit per pound, customer concentration.
  - Pricing floors per account.
  - An annual blameless review of failed launches, and of launches that "met targets" but lost money per pound.
  - Two internal successor candidates, rotated through operations and commercial roles.
