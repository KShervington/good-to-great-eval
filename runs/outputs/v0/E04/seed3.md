# Good to Great: Northfield-Brandt Corporation

## Verdict

**Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** *Northfield-Brandt turns its paper-fiber know-how into consumer brands that create or lead their absorbent personal-care categories, and it judges every dollar by profit per consumer brand.*

**Weakest link in the flywheel:** reinvestment. Profit from the leading brands flows out to an airline and to commodity mills instead of back into R&D, advertising and plants for those brands. Meanwhile a larger rival out-spends the company on R&D and advertising.

**Top three stop-doing items:**
1. Stop funding the airline's $120M expansion. Separate the airline so it can grow on its own capital.
2. Stop reinvesting in newsprint, commodity printing paper and tobacco papers. Exit them.
3. Stop competing with Rival P dollar for dollar everywhere. Concentrate spending where the company leads or can lead.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** a 20-year CEO is 64, and his president was appointed two months ago. The handoff is happening just as three hard-to-reverse capital decisions are open: airline expansion, the paper businesses, and the pace of investment in Europe.

**Missing numbers that would change the answer:**
- Profit and return on capital by class of business. If paper or aviation out-earns consumer on capital employed, the Hedgehog Concept needs rework.
- Current market shares by category. Facial tissue data is from the mid-1980s.
- Returns on the European plants.
- Airline profit and capital needs.
- Profit per brand.

`Greatness potential: 62/100`

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Northfield-Brandt finishes the narrowing its CEO began 20 years ago, when he sold the commodity mills. It becomes a pure consumer and personal-care company. The airline is set free to grow on its own capital, and the commodity paper mills go to owners who want commodity businesses. Every remaining brand is #1 or a close #2 in its category, and capital goes brand by brand, based on profit per consumer brand. Every few years it creates or redefines a category from its absorbent-materials know-how, as it did with facial tissue, the premium diaper, training pants and adult incontinence. It doesn't try to out-spend Rival P across the whole board. Instead it out-focuses Rival P one category at a time. It grows in Europe category by category and country by country, against milestones. It hands leadership to a successor who keeps pushing the same flywheel instead of launching a new strategy.

## 3. The flywheel

```

 │        (infancy → toddler → women's care → aging)
 │                 │
 │                 ▼
 │   2. Category-creating product, protected by materials patents
 │                 │
 │                 ▼
 │   3. #1 or close #2 brand position
 │                 │
 │                 ▼
 │   4. Higher PROFIT PER CONSUMER BRAND
 │      (scale, pricing, shelf space)          ◄── weakest link sits
 │                 │                               between 4 and 5:
 │                 ▼                               profit leaks to the
 │   5. Reinvestment ONLY in leading brands:       airline and mills
 │      R&D, advertising, plants (incl. Europe)
 │                 │
 │                 ▼
 │   6. Deeper moat: patents, cost, retailer and consumer trust
 └─────────────────┘
```

The loop closes because profit per brand (step 4) funds the insight and invention work that starts the next turn (step 1).

**Current push (next one or two turns):**
1. Decide how to exit the airline and the commodity paper businesses, and commit the freed capital and management attention to step 5 for the leading brands. The diaper and training-pants fight with Rival P comes first.
2. Re-pace Europe so plants follow brand milestones instead of preceding them.

**Doom-loop check:** the company is in a flywheel, not a doom loop. Its six-year market outperformance and the 20-year consistent pivot are the evidence. There are, however, three doom-loop seeds:
- **The airline.** It is a business built from an asset (the executives' planes) rather than from the strategy. It survived a fatal crash and heavy losses, and it is now expanding.
- **Heavy European plant investment with no visible return data.** This is a possible "big program" pattern.
- **The temptation of a large acquisition** to close the size gap with Rival P after divestitures generate cash. Flag this now.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. Stop funding the airline's $120M expansion with company capital. Hold at committed contracts and separate the business.
2. Stop reinvesting in newsprint and commodity printing-paper mills. Exit them.
3. Stop operating tobacco-industry papers. Exit them; they are outside the Hedgehog Concept.
4. Stop entering consumer categories where there is no credible path to #1 or close #2 `[ASSUMPTION: threshold to be set by the board]`.
5. Stop scaling premium-technology products before a consumer use case is proven, which is the anti-viral tissue lesson. Require test-market proof of repeat purchase.
6. Stop matching Rival P's spending everywhere. Concentrate R&D and advertising on the categories the company leads or can lead.
7. Stop building European plants on a fixed schedule. Release capital only when category and country milestones are hit.
8. Stop allocating capital by class of business. Allocate by brand.
9. Stop creating businesses out of internal support functions because the assets happen to exist.

**Framework and freedom:** brand general managers own their brand's P&L and have broad autonomy inside one boundary: absorbent personal care, with a credible path to category leadership. Any capital request above a set threshold must show its effect on profit per brand.

**Bureaucracy check:** remove approval layers that only exist to police non-core units once those units are gone.

**Fanatical consistency (every year, without exception):**
- Fund the leading brands first.
- Keep one or more category-creating projects in the pipeline.
- Review profit per brand every quarter.
- Defend the patent portfolio.
