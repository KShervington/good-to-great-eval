# Good to Great: Tillery Products Company

## One-screen verdict

**Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** *Tillery makes the most useful, longest-lasting rubber and plastic products for the kitchen and home, and puts them on the shelf where households already shop.*

**Weakest flywheel link:** whether the brand can still command a premium. Earnings compounding broke in Year −6 on resin costs, and pricing has not been tested since the minimum-retail-price agreements ended in Year −5.

**Top 3 stop-doing items:**
1. Stop treating "quadruple sales and earnings in 10 years" as the north star. A size target pulls toward acquisitions and sprawl. Measure profit per pound of resin and return on equity instead.
2. Stop competing head-on with Rival T in party-plan sales. Prepare to sell or exit, pending division profit data.
3. Stop entering any category where Tillery does not already own distribution. The recreational-products failure already proved this.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** management cannot yet show which of its five businesses earn their keep. This is happening as resin is volatile, a recession is expected, and the new chief executive has publicly committed to a size goal.

**Missing numbers that would change the answer:**
- profit and capital employed by division;
- housewares margins and market share since the price agreements ended;
- recent return on equity;
- resin as a share of cost of goods;
- depth of the management bench.

If automotive or party plan turn out to be highly profitable, the Hedgehog Concept widens. If housewares margins collapsed after the price agreements ended, the concept itself is in doubt.

`Greatness potential: 35/100`

*Why 35:* the foundation is real: about 20 years of compounding, a national brand, and direct supermarket distribution. But the company is spread across five units with different economics, faces resin and recession exposure, and has an undocumented bench. Its new leader's stated ambition points at size, not at the company's distinctive engine. Becoming great is plausible. Staying great past one leader is the harder half.

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Tillery is a narrower company than today's. It is the household name for durable, well-designed rubber and plastic products for the kitchen and home. It holds its supermarket and retail shelf with a steady flow of new items, and it extends the same products and molding base into restaurants and hotels, where durability is the purchase criterion. It earns more profit on every pound of resin it converts than any other molder, because its brand and design let it charge for usefulness rather than plastic. It does not chase channels it doesn't own or categories where a specialist is entrenched. It does not buy growth outside markets it already serves. It manages resin swings as a permanent fact of life, not a surprise. And it runs on mechanisms (division P&Ls, a product pipeline cadence, a successor bench) that would keep it compounding if the chief executive left tomorrow.

**Why this denominator:** resin is the main input, and it is the variable that has hurt earnings most (Year −6, price controls, the petrochemical shortage). Profit per pound measures how much value the brand and design add on top of a commodity input. It also penalizes products that are just plastic.

**Confirmation tests (working hypothesis):**
- Does housewares (and commercial) show higher profit per pound than automotive, party plan and international? If not, revise.
- Did the historical earnings-doubling record come mainly from housewares? This should be checked against the old division data.
- Have housewares margins held up since the minimum-price agreements ended? If not, the "best at" circle rests on legal pricing protection rather than brand strength. That would be a FAIL and send the run back to Gate 4.
- Is it uncomfortable to commit to? Yes. It implies shrinking or selling roughly 10–20% of the business, which is the right sign.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   ┌──► 1. Design useful, durable new items for kitchen & home
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │    2. Brand trust lets items sell at a premium   ◄── WEAKEST LINK
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │    3. Direct supermarket distribution wins more shelf facings
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │    4. Volume through one molding & resin base → lower cost/lb,
   │       buying scale (extends to commercial/institutional)
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │    5. Higher operating profit per pound of resin (denominator)
   │            │
   │            ▼
   └─── 6. Reinvest in design, tooling, and the new-item pipeline
```

**Current push (next one or two turns):**
1. **Prove link 2.** Measure the housewares price premium and margin since Year −5. Build a resin pass-through and procurement plan so that the premium isn't eaten by the next resin spike.
2. **Free capital for link 6.** Decide on party plan and automotive. Redirect the proceeds and management attention into the housewares and commercial new-product pipeline.

**Doom-loop check:** several warning signs are present.
- **A new direction under a new leader.** A new outsider CEO who calls the company stodgy invites one.
- **A big public number.** The 4× target pushes toward acquisitions or big programs to jump-start growth.
- **Open strategic questions on acquisitions and divisions.** These invite reaction before understanding, because no division P&L exists yet.
- **No inconsistency yet.** The dossier shows none in the core housewares line, which is good. The aim is to keep it that way.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop** using "4× sales and earnings in 10 years" as the governing goal. Replace it with profit per pound of resin and ROE. Keep the ambition, change the yardstick.
2. **Stop** competing head-on with Rival T in party plan. Prepare a sale or exit and decide by day 90 (pending data).
3. **Stop** entering categories or channels where Tillery has no existing distribution. Make this a written rule.
4. **Stop** considering acquisitions outside markets and channels already served. Keep the prior management's discipline here.
5. **Stop** funding new automotive capacity until its profit per pound is known.
6. **Stop** carrying SKUs below a profit-per-pound floor. Prune the 240-item line annually.
7. **Stop** absorbing resin spikes passively through lagged price changes. Build contractual or formula-based pass-through with retailers, within antitrust limits.
8. **Stop** any pricing practice with retailers that resembles resale price maintenance. Win shelf price on value, under the cease-and-desist order.

**Truth climate:** unknown `[ASSUMPTION]`. The new CEO's "stodgy and complacent" framing risks becoming a story that bad news has to fit. Red-flag mechanisms are needed:

- blameless post-mortems (for example on the recreational line and on the strike);

**Charisma check:** this is a real risk. An outsider with a large-company pedigree, a public numeric goal and a strong opinion of the culture can quietly discourage dissent. The countermeasure is structural: the CEO asks questions and speaks last in reviews.

**Faith statement:** *Tillery will prevail because households trust its name, it already owns the supermarket shelf, and it has compounded for two decades. It needs focus, not reinvention.*

## 7. Core ideology and the BHAG

**Core values** `[ASSUMPTION]`, to be validated with employees:
1. Products that last.
2. Usefulness over novelty.
3. Fair dealing with customers, retailers and regulators.
4. Respect for the people who make the products.
5. Steady compounding over spectacle.

**Core purpose:** make everyday household work easier with products that last.

**BHAG (20-year horizon):** *Make Tillery the first brand shoppers reach for in every aisle that sells durable rubber or plastic household products, in North America and Western Europe.* This replaces the size target with a position target. Size follows from it.

**Clock-building mechanisms:**
- division P&Ls with profit per pound;
- an annual new-product cadence with a target for the share of sales from new items;
- an annual SKU pruning rule;
- a resin council and pass-through policy;
- a written rule for acquisitions and new categories ("served markets and owned channels only");
- a board-reviewed succession plan with two internal candidates;
- a red-flag reporting line.
