# Good to Great: Marlowe Drug Co.

## One-screen verdict

**Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** *Marlowe will be the best and most convenient drugstore in every community it serves, built around the pharmacy, and it will measure itself by profit per customer visit.*

**Weakest flywheel link:** the step from **profit per visit to reinvestment in drugstore density**. Nobody measures profit per visit. The profit that does exist leaks into restaurants, discount stores and side businesses before it can return to the drugstores.

**Top three stop-doing items:**
1. Stop expanding food service. Freeze the 22 planned restaurant units and the new free-standing family-restaurant chain.
2. Stop investing in the 25 discount department stores, and prepare an exit.
3. Stop pursuing "growth across all divisions" as the corporate aim.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** sales grew 130% in nine years ($432.5M → $996.6M), but earnings did not follow. EPS was $1.30 then and $1.15 now. Even with the LIFO effect added back, net margin fell from about 2.2% seven years ago to about 1.4% now. The company is buying volume, not building profit.

**Missing numbers that would change this answer:** profit and return on capital by division; profit per store and per customer visit; regional market share. If restaurants or discount stores earn more on capital than drugstores, the stop-doing list changes. If density does not predict per-store profit, the flywheel changes.

`Greatness potential: 40/100`

*Evidence note: this run had no access to memory, past chats or web research. It uses the dossier alone, so there is no prior baseline and no external market data. Every inference is marked `[ASSUMPTION]`.*

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

The great Marlowe is a drugstore company and nothing else. Each store sits on the most convenient corner in a dense local cluster. Its pharmacy brings customers back several times a month. Health, beauty and house-brand products make each visit more profitable. Every store manager knows the store's profit per customer visit and is judged on it. Capital goes to new and better drugstore sites in markets where Marlowe can be the obvious choice, not to restaurants, discount stores or travel agencies. The founding family's identity, "a pharmacist's store," is turned into mechanisms: a capital-allocation rule, a site standard, a pharmacy-first store format and an independent board. Those mechanisms let the company keep compounding after the current generation steps back.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

| Circle | Candidates considered | Narrowed to |
|---|---|---|
| **Best in the world at** | Discount general merchandise; family restaurants; drug manufacturing; neighborhood drugstores at scale | **Convenient, pharmacy-centered neighborhood drugstores run at scale.** Evidence: the largest U.S. chain, about 2× the nearest competitor's volume, 75 years of operating history, pharmacist roots, and a house-brand lab with more than 500 products. **Cannot be best at:** discount department stores (25 regional stores, the worst hit by the recession, with shrink management calls unacceptable), family restaurants (8% of sales, a different business), travel, coffee roasting or ice cream. |
| **Economic engine denominator** | Profit per store; per square foot; per prescription; **per customer visit** | **Profit per customer visit** `[ASSUMPTION]`. It rewards convenience, because convenience drives frequency. It rewards pharmacy, which drives repeat visits, and it rewards house brands and lower shrink, which raise margin per visit. Profit per store is distorted by openings and the 51 closures. Profit per square foot pushes toward cramming the store, and stores are already getting larger. Test this against profit per store before committing. |
| **Deep passion** | Growth, size, the $1B milestone; the soda-fountain heritage; the drugstore identity | **The drugstore as a community health center.** Management "holds fast to its basic identity as drugstores," the founder was a pharmacist, and the stores are described as "community centers for health and beauty service." |

**Tests of the candidate:**
- *Does it explain past wins?* Yes. The chain grew on drugstores, low prices and house brands.
- *Does it rule things out?* Yes: discount stores, free-standing restaurants, travel, the Mexico stake, and eventually most in-store food.
- *Is it uncomfortable to commit to?* Yes. It means unwinding restaurants that trace back to the 1920s soda fountains, and businesses the family built or bought.

**Confirmation tests (needed within about 12 months):**
1. Drugstore-division return on capital clearly exceeds that of restaurants and discount stores.
2. Profit per visit varies widely across stores, and the variation tracks convenience and pharmacy share. That would show the denominator is something management can act on.
3. Stores in dense clusters out-earn isolated stores per visit.

**Current push (next one or two turns):**
1. Start measuring profit per visit, store by store, and give the number to store managers.
2. Redirect the capital now earmarked for 22 restaurant units and discount stores into drugstore sites in markets where Marlowe already has density, especially Sun Belt markets that Rival E is contesting.

**Doom-loop check (signs are present):**
- Growth in every direction at once: restaurants, discount stores, manufacturing, travel and Mexico.
- Acquisitions that add size, not focus: the travel agency, and 29 Southeast stores bought from another chain, which may or may not fit a density logic `[ASSUMPTION]`.
- New direction alongside old: a brand-new family-restaurant chain launched while margins fall.
- Reaction to results without understanding them. The letter's remedies (shrink, cost, productivity) are operational, and none of them addresses the possibility that the portfolio itself dilutes margin.

**Verdict:** the loop closes, but it is leaking at link 4.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop expanding food service.** Freeze the 22 planned units. Stop building the new free-standing family-restaurant chain, and plan to divest the 27 free-standing restaurants.
2. **Stop investing in discount department stores.** Halt capital spending and prepare a sale of the 25 stores within about 2 years `[ASSUMPTION about buyer availability]`.
3. **Stop stating "growth across all divisions"** as the corporate aim. Replace it with the drugstore Hedgehog.
4. **Stop measuring success by corporate sales.** Don't celebrate the $1B run-rate. Report profit per visit and drugstore return on capital instead.
5. **Sell the travel agency.**
6. **Put no new capital into the Mexico stake.** Hold it passively and plan an orderly exit.
7. **Stop expanding manufacturing capacity** beyond what drugstores and house brands require. The plastic-container plant passes only if it lowers house-brand cost.
8. **Stop treating bigger stores as progress.** Square footage must earn its place in profit per visit.
9. **Stop opening or acquiring stores that don't build density.** Isolated sites and acquisitions done for store count both fail this test.
10. **Review the in-store lunch counters (288)** once there is per-visit data. Phase them out wherever pharmacy or health-and-beauty space earns more per visit. This is heritage, so do it gradually and on evidence, but do it.

## 7. Core ideology and the BHAG

**Core values** `[ASSUMPTION, inferred from heritage]`:
1. A pharmacist's trust: the customer's health comes first.
2. Convenience as a form of service.
3. Thrift and fair prices.
4. Integrity in every prescription and every product.

**Core purpose:** to make it easy for every community to stay healthy.

**BHAG (20–30 years):** a Marlowe drugstore on a convenient corner within easy reach of most American households, earning the industry's best profit per customer visit. This is a hypothesis that follows from the Hedgehog.

**Mechanisms (clock building):**
- A capital-allocation rule: drugstores get first claim, and nothing outside the Hedgehog is funded without board-level return-on-capital proof.
- Profit per visit appears in every store manager's review.
- A written site-selection standard for density and convenience.
- An independent board majority.
- A succession bench that includes non-family executives.
- A pharmacist leadership track.
