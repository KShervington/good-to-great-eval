# Tallypaw: Good to Great

## One-screen verdict

**Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** *Tallypaw is the supplier-neutral ordering and inventory brain for independent vet clinics, built by people who have run one, and it grows by raising gross profit per independent clinic.*

**Weakest flywheel link:** how new clinics arrive. About 38% of ARR comes through VetSource, and most demos depend on Maya personally. Neither is an engine Tallypaw owns.

**Top three stop-doing items:**
1. Stop the dental build. That is two engineers for six months on a non-binding LOI.
2. Stop pursuing corporate chains. Two pilots have produced zero conversions, and chains are the competitors of Tallypaw's customers.
3. Stop investing in reminder features beyond parity. It is a commodity module, and its SMS vendor's prices rose 30%.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** VetSource is building a competing product. It accounts for about $0.9M of ARR (38%), its contract renews in five months, and it has not answered two emails. Meanwhile the plan of record sends engineers to dental, and the board minutes list VetSource under *housekeeping*.

**Missing numbers that would change this answer:**
- **Ownership of VetSource-referred clinics.** Who holds the contract and billing, and what the agreement says about non-solicit, data and post-term rights.
- **New clinics and CAC by channel.** The $1.1M of S&M spend has not been split out.
- **Whether inventory *causes* the lower churn.** The analysis needs controls for clinic size, tenure and channel.
- **Multi-supplier buying and market size.** What share of independent clinics buy from more than one distributor, and how many independent clinics Tallypaw can realistically reach. This settles the "venture-scale" question.

**Overall:** 6 of 11 gates are CONDITIONAL. There are two root problems. First, leadership has not yet chosen a focus: vet, chains and dental are all live at once. Second, Tallypaw depends on distribution it doesn't own: VetSource and Maya's demos.

Greatness potential: 30/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Tallypaw is the back-office brain that lets an independent vet clinic order, stock and spend as well as any corporate chain, across every supplier it buys from. The founders' insight ("nobody else understands how a small clinic actually orders drugs") is built into the product, the onboarding team and the data. Clinics join because other practice managers tell them to, not because a distributor bundles Tallypaw. They adopt inventory in their first month and rarely leave. VetSource is one of several distributor partners, not the landlord. Maya builds the company instead of running its demos. Reminders are a cheap, reliable utility. Revenue grows because gross profit per clinic rises year after year. Its purpose is to keep independent practices independent, and it measures itself on that.

This is the most important claim to test.

**Intersection tests:**
- *Does it explain past wins?* Yes. Inventory is the most-mentioned module, has 71% attach and about half the churn. NPS is 61, word of mouth runs among practice managers, and customers name the ex-vet-tech CS staff in reviews.
- *Does it rule things out?* Yes: dental, chains, and reminder feature work.
- *Is it uncomfortable?* Yes. It kills the CEO's dental idea and the Head of Sales's thesis, and it accepts open tension with the partner behind 38% of ARR.

**Confirmation tests (it stays a hypothesis until these come back):**
1. The inventory-to-retention effect holds after controlling for clinic size, tenure and channel. Owner: Jonah, day 45.
2. Of 20 customer interviews, at least half of the clinics buy from more than one distributor `[ASSUMPTION threshold]`.
3. Gross profit per clinic rises at least 10% over two quarters from inventory-led expansion.
4. Reachable independent clinics × target gross profit per clinic produces a number that supports the company's capital strategy. If it doesn't, change the capital strategy before changing the market. See Brutal Facts.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop the dental build and pilot.** That is roughly 20% of engineering for six months `[ASSUMPTION: ~10 engineers]`, during the VetSource window. It brings no insider edge, and the LOI is non-binding.
2. **Stop the corporate chain pilots and enterprise-only features.** They are 0 for 2, and chains are what independents compete against.
3. **Stop investing in reminder features beyond parity.** Re-bid the SMS vendor or reprice. Reminder-only clinics are a feeder for inventory, not the product.
4. **Stop treating VetSource as housekeeping.** It becomes the first item at every board meeting until it is resolved.
5. **Stop having the CEO run most demos.** Codify her demo into a playbook others can run.
6. **Stop spending S&M without channel attribution.** No new channel spend until CAC by channel is known.
7. **Stop adding roadmap items that don't improve an independent clinic's ordering.** The board asked for this. Deliver a list of five or fewer items now, not after dental.
8. **Stop making multi-year fixed commitments before VetSource is resolved.** Take a short extension on the March lease.

**Non-negotiable disciplines:**
- Every roadmap item names the flywheel step it pushes.
- Every new clinic gets insider-led inventory onboarding within 30 days.
- Single-partner ARR share is reported monthly.
- Blameless post-mortems, Jonah-style, apply company-wide.

**Freedom within the framework:** anything that raises gross profit per independent clinic through ordering and inventory is fair game, and teams choose how. New verticals, chains or segments require a written board-level case that passes the hedgehog tests.

**Bureaucracy check:** At 23 people there is little bureaucracy. The opposite risk is real: decisions run at Maya's speed. Also watch for enterprise-style sales process, imported by the Head of Sales, that doesn't fit a $7.7k ACV.
