# Halvard Strings: Good to Great verdict

**Where it stands:** Halvard is already performing at a great level. Revenue grew 4.2× in 14 years while its market grew about 1.3×. Operating margin has stayed between 24% and 29% for eleven years, ROIC averages 34%, and pro-player adoption rose from 9% to 31%. The question for this session is not how to become great. It is how to avoid starting the decline.

**Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):**
> *Halvard makes the violin-family strings that professional players choose blind, sells them only through the shops and luthiers those players trust, and earns its return from a premium price and players who stay.*

**Most urgent brutal fact:** Growth is drifting down toward the market's 2%. Both items on the board's agenda respond by reversing the decision that made Halvard great. Halvard Six, at a 31% gross margin through mass retail, would be about **24% of Year-3 revenue**. That is almost exactly the **22% mass-channel share the company cut 14 years ago**.

**Weakest link in the flywheel:** Availability at the shop. The independent-shop channel in North America shrank about 3% last year. Cello and bass lead times, on Halvard's strongest instrument, slipped to five weeks this autumn.

**Top 3 stop-doing:**
1. Don't launch Halvard Six as proposed. Decline the 900-store end-cap offer.
2. Don't buy the private-equity group's student brand.
3. Don't give winding capacity to anything outside the core until cello and bass sets hold a two-week lead time through a full autumn peak.

**Missing numbers that could change the answer:**
- Adoption by instrument (violin, viola, bass) and the share of players using full Halvard sets.
- Winding-machine utilization, and whether Six's machines also run cello and bass.
- Six's projected *operating* margin, not just gross margin.
- How independent shops would react to a mass-retail presence.
- The student brand's size and margin.
- Revenue split by region and the concentration of top shops.

Greatness potential: 72/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Halvard looks much like today's Halvard, with three differences. It is more deliberate about why it wins. It is more patient about growth. And it is built so that it no longer depends on Annelie Brandt. It refuses guitar and student volume because those are not its world. It grows 2–4× faster than its market by pushing pro adoption beyond cello into violin, viola and bass, and by turning one-string users into full-set users. It reaches professional and conservatory players in new regions through the same kind of specialist shops. It makes sure that when a player asks a luthier for Halvard, the set is on the wall. The Listening Room, the annual orchestra survey and the minimum-advertised-price (MAP) policy keep doing their work after the current leaders are gone. Ten years from now, the measure of success is that most section players choose Halvard in blind tests. Total string units sold is not the measure.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence:** Halvard makes the violin-family strings that professional players choose blind, sells them only through the shops and luthiers those players trust, and earns its return from a premium price and players who stay.

| Circle | Candidates considered | Chosen | Explicitly *not* best at |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Best in the world at** | (a) winding technology; (b) corrosion durability; (c) player-validated tone for bowed strings; (d) premium brand | **(c)**: strings that pros prefer in blind tests, delivered by in-house winding and the Listening Room working together. Winding is the means. Player preference is the moat. | Guitar (no players, no panel, no channel credibility); student price points; mass-retail logistics; marketplace e-commerce |
| **Economic engine** | profit per set; profit per shop; profit per unit of winding capacity; **profit per loyal player** | **Operating profit per loyal player.** It captures both the 15–25% premium and the 78% three-year retention. `[ASSUMPTION]` Not yet computed; the CFO can derive it from survey and shop-panel data. | Volume economics. | **Deep passion** | acoustics; sound of the instrument; players; winding craft | The sound of bowed instruments, judged by players. **Confirmation tests:**
- *Explains past wins?* Yes. Exiting mass distributors and investing in player testing and winding coincides with gross margin moving from 44% to 61% and adoption from 9% to 31%.
- *Rules things out?* Yes: Halvard Six, the student brand, marketplaces and any untested product.
- *Uncomfortable?* Yes. It walks away from a $19M projection and a ready-made retail deal.
- *Would confirm it over 12–24 months:* pro adoption keeps rising about 1.5 points a year; violin and bass adoption close the gap with cello; profit per loyal player rises; retention holds at or above 78%; growth stays at or above 3× the market.

**On Pieter's argument** ("we only point it at two percent of the string players"): the denominator is wrong. Halvard is not in the business of string players in general. It is in the business of players who choose with their ears in a blind test. His concentration-risk point is fair. The answer to it lies inside the hedgehog: more instruments, full sets, more regions and a stronger channel (see §9).

## 3. The flywheel

```
 [1] Listening Room blind tests
      → strings professional players prefer by ear
              │
              ▼
 [2] Pros adopt: first one string, then the full set
      (31% of section players; 44% of cellists)
              │
              ▼
 [3] Shops & luthiers recommend and stock Halvard;   ◄── WEAKEST LINK
      advanced players follow the pros                    (channel −3%/yr NA;
              │                                            lead times hit 5 wks)
              ▼
 [4] Premium held (MAP, +15–25%), players stay (78% @ 3 yrs)
      = operating profit per loyal player  ← denominator
              │
              ▼
 [5] Reinvest: winding R&D, capacity, panel, people
              │
              └────────────► back to [1]
```

**The loop closes.** Each turn produces more player trust, more shops pulling the product, more margin to reinvest and better strings.

**Current push, the next two turns:**
1. **Strengthen link [3]: availability.** Fix the root cause of the cello/bass lead-time slip and build buffer stock before autumn and around the roof works. Start a shop-partnership program aimed at the independents that are consolidating, so that when a shop closes, its players move to another Halvard-stocking shop rather than to a competitor's display.
2. **Widen link [2]: adoption depth.** Point commercial effort at the instruments where adoption trails cello. `[ASSUMPTION]` Violins hold most section seats, so violin adoption is probably well below 31%. Also push full-set conversion.

**Doom-loop check:** Halvard is not in a doom loop yet, but it shows the classic early signs. Growth slowed from about 11% CAGR to 7–9%. The board is now looking at a big new program and an acquisition, both at once, to restart momentum. Halvard Six doesn't push this flywheel. It grinds against it: it takes capacity from [5], dilutes the premium and MAP at [4], and risks channel conflict at [3].

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop Halvard Six as proposed.** Decline the 900-store end-cap and featured-online deal. If the board insists on answering the guitar question, answer it the Halvard way: a small, capped "bullet" with a panel of guitarists blind-testing prototypes and a specialist channel only, with kill criteria agreed in advance. `[ASSUMPTION]` A plucked niche played by conservatory-trained musicians, such as classical guitar, would be the only adjacency worth that test. It still ranks below every in-core lever.
2. **Don't buy the student brand.** At 1.1× revenue, with unknown margins, from a seller choosing to sell, it would put Halvard back into the mass student channel it left.
3. **Don't give winding capacity to non-core products** until cello and bass hold two-week lead times through a full autumn peak.
4. **Don't let lab data stand in for player judgment.** No product, in any category, ships without blind-test approval by players of that instrument. The corrosion test doesn't qualify.
5. **Never list on general marketplaces or sell below MAP.** This continues an existing discipline; keep it written down.
6. **Stop framing growth as "share of all string players."** Measure share of the professional and advanced players Halvard serves, by instrument.
7. **Don't schedule plant disruption without buffer stock.** `[ASSUMPTION]` Autumn is peak season, so the roof works need a stock build first.

**Truth climate:**
- *Strong.* The Listening Room is a real red-flag mechanism for products: 3 kills in 5 years. The CEO steers credit away from herself. The CFO pre-read names the problems.
- *Gap.* There is no equivalent mechanism for **strategy**.
- *Charisma check.* The CEO's quiet style isn't suppressing bad news. The risk runs the other way. A persuasive growth narrative backed by two board members could fill the space left while she withholds a view.

**Faith statement:** Halvard will keep winning because professional players choose its strings with their ears, blind, and no competitor can buy that with shelf space.
