## One-screen verdict

**Halvard is already great.** It passes all three tests: fourteen years of growth, a 34% average ROIC, and 31% of orchestral string players (44% of cellists). The question for this session is not how to become great. It is how to avoid starting the slide. Both items on the agenda are textbook Stage 2 of *How the Mighty Fall*: undisciplined pursuit of more.

**Hedgehog Concept:** *Halvard makes the strings that professional bowed-string players choose in blind tests, and sells them only through the specialists who serve those players.* The engine is measured as **profit per committed player.**

**Recommendation:** decline Halvard Six as proposed. Decline the student brand. Put the growth energy into the instruments, regions and capacity that sit inside the hedgehog.

**Weakest link in the flywheel:** availability. Cello and bass lead times slipped to five weeks last autumn, in Halvard's strongest segment. That happened while independent shops were consolidating, and Halvard Six asks for a second shift on the same winding machines.

**Top three stop-doing items:**
1. Don't re-enter guitar strings through mass retail at a 31% gross margin. This is the market Halvard deliberately left fourteen years ago.
2. Don't buy a student brand to buy growth.
3. Don't commit winding capacity to anything else until cello and bass hold two-week lead times through peak season.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** the core market grows about 2% a year. All of Halvard's growth comes from taking share and holding price, and growth has slowed from roughly 11–12% a year to 7–9%. The commercial director's worry is real. His answer to it is the wrong one.

**Missing numbers that could change this answer:**
- Halvard's revenue share of the total professional and advanced bowed-string market. This tells you how much headroom is left.
- Share by instrument for violin, viola and bass.
- Current sales outside North America and Europe.
- Winding-machine utilisation.
- How the independent shops would react to Halvard appearing on national-chain end-caps.
- Any blind test of the guitar prototype by professional guitarists. Corrosion lab results don't count.

`Greatness potential: 76/100`

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

In ten years, Halvard is still one plant, still debt-free, still a premium brand with strict price discipline. It is the string that most professional bowed-string players reach for, and not only cellists. Violin, viola and bass sections have closed the gap with the cello's 44%. Halvard is in specialist shops in East Asia as well as North America and Europe `[ASSUMPTION: currently little presence there]`. Its Listening Room panel includes players from every region it sells in. Lead times hold at two weeks all year. Independent shops see Halvard as their most dependable premium line, and that matters more as the channel consolidates. Brandt's successor runs the company on mechanisms rather than on her judgment: the panel's veto, the minimum-price rule, the stop-doing list, and a yearly scorecard. Growth runs at about 6–9% a year with operating margins of 25% or more. The company does not chase a market six times larger, because it knows the larger market is not where it can be best.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence:** *Halvard makes the strings that professional bowed-string players choose in blind tests, and sells them only through the specialists who serve those players.*

| Circle | Candidates considered | Narrowed to |
|---|---|---|
| **Best in the world at** | Winding technology; premium strings generally; corrosion-resistant strings; strings for the violin family as judged blind by professionals | **Strings that professional bowed-string players prefer in blind tests.** The winding is the means. The blind verdict from professionals is the proof. |
| **Cannot be best at** | Mass-market logistics; price-led student strings; guitar strings (no guitar panel, no relationships with guitarists, no guitar channel) | All three are ruled out. |
| **Economic engine (denominator)** | Profit per set; per shop; per orchestra; **per committed player** | **Operating profit per committed player.** Professionals adopt, 78% stay for three years, they accept a 15–25% premium, and advanced players and teachers follow them. `[ASSUMPTION: not currently tracked]` |
| **Passion** | Acoustics engineering; the players; the craft of winding; the blind-test ritual | The CEO and the culture are passionate about **sound as players judge it**. Volume is not what excites them. |

**Does it explain past wins?** Yes. The decision fourteen years ago removed 22% of revenue and is what made the company: margins rose from 9.8% to 27.9%, and orchestral share rose from 9% to 31%.

**Does it rule things out?** Yes. It rules out both agenda items.

**Is it uncomfortable to commit to?** Yes. It means turning down a market six times larger and a retail chain that is actively courting Halvard.

**Confirmation tests over 12 months:**
- Profit per committed player is computed and found to be stable or rising.
- Violin and viola share rises toward the cello figure.
- A pilot in a new region converts professional players at rates similar to Europe.

**On the CEO's question (should the Listening Room test guitar prototypes?):** no. The panel is 40 bowed-string professionals, so it is not qualified to judge guitar strings. To get a credible verdict, Halvard would first have to build a guitar panel, which means building a second company. The fact that the panel can't answer the question tells you the question is outside the hedgehog. Strategic fit is a decision for the board, not for the panel.

**If the board still insists on exploring guitar:** treat it as a small, cheap test, not a full launch. Set kill criteria in writing before anything starts:
- a blind preference win with a panel of professional guitarists;
- specialist channel only, with no national chains;
- gross margin of 55% or more `[ASSUMPTION threshold]`;
- no winding capacity taken from cello or bass;
- a fixed budget and a fixed date.

The expected result is a kill. Say so up front.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   ┌─► 1. Listening Room blind-tests every prototype
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │   2. Strings professionals choose ──► orchestral adoption (31%; cello 44%)
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │   3. Advanced players and teachers follow; specialist shops recommend
   │      (minimum price held, steady shop margins)
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │   4. Committed players: 78% still using after 3 yrs; 15–25% premium holds
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │   5. High PROFIT PER COMMITTED PLAYER (27.9% op. margin, ~34% ROIC)
   │            │
   │            ▼
   └── 6. Reinvest in winding machines, R&D, panel; keep sets in stock
```

**Weakest link:** the step from adoption (2) to in-stock at the shop (3). Cello and bass sets hit five-week lead times last autumn. A professional who can't get a set will try a competitor's, and some won't come back. The shop channel that carries Halvard is shrinking about 3% a year in North America. The roof replacement this summer is another risk to supply.

**Current push (next one or two turns):**
1. Lock in cello and bass availability: a capacity plan, a safety-stock build ahead of the roof work, and two-week lead times through peak season.
2. Close the share gap between violin/viola and the cello.

**Doom-loop check:** the warning signs are clear. Two big moves are arriving at one board session:
- a large new programme in a new market (Halvard Six);
- an acquisition (the student brand).

Both are pitched as answers to slowing growth, even though the flywheel is still turning at 7–9% in a market growing 2%. Neither has been through the Listening Room, and neither fits the channel. Reaching for new directions and buying growth are exactly the doom-loop pattern. The flywheel itself still closes.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Don't launch Halvard Six as proposed.** It would be guitar strings through 900 mass-retail end-caps at a 31% gross margin. If the core grows 8% a year, $19M of guitar revenue would pull blended gross margin from 61% to about 54% by Year 3 `[ASSUMPTION: calculated from dossier figures]`. Halvard would become a supplier to a national chain, and its shops would see that.
2. **Don't buy the student brand** at 1.1× revenue. It is a price-led, mass-distribution business, and a competitor that knows that segment well has decided to sell it. Ask why.
3. **Don't sell through national chains, general online marketplaces, or below the minimum advertised price**, for any product carrying the Halvard name.
4. **Don't put a product on the market without Listening Room approval, and don't enter a category the panel is not qualified to judge.**
5. **Don't commit winding capacity or second shifts to anything outside the core** until cello and bass lead times hold at two weeks or less year-round.
6. **Don't measure success against total string-market size.** Measure it against share of professional and advanced bowed-string players.
7. **Don't buy growth.** Growth comes from turning the flywheel, not from acquisitions or one-off launches.
