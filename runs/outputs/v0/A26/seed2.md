# Good to Great: Brightwater Industries

## One-screen verdict

**Split the company, then narrow the toy business.** As it stands, Brightwater cannot become great. It is really two companies with two leaders, one shared capital pool and a family feud. The great version is a **toy company built on a few long-lived brands**. The pencil business should leave on fair terms and be run by the person who cares most about it.

- **Hedgehog Concept:** *Brightwater turns a small number of classic play patterns and well-loved characters into toy brands that children want year after year, and it judges itself by profit per enduring brand.*
- **Weakest flywheel link:** brand renewal. The owned brands are old and depend on TV advertising, there is no current hit, and debt is starving reinvestment.
- **Top three stop-doing items:**
  1. Stay out of electronic games.
  2. Start no new ventures outside toys.
  3. Launch no one-season novelties, and nothing at all without an independent safety sign-off.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** lenders constrain the company, and the family is split, both at the same moment. Separating pencils may remove the only business described as profitable, and nobody yet knows whether the toy side makes money on its own.
- **Missing numbers that would change the answer:**
  - Profit by segment (toys versus pencils).
  - Debt level, its terms, and how it would be divided in a split.
  - Year −1 results.
  - Profit by brand, and the share of toy sales from brands that are five or more years old.
  - **If toys lose money even after excluding the failed ventures, the answer flips.** The candidate for greatness becomes pencils, and the toy business needs survival work before greatness work.

Greatness potential: 25/100

*Evidence basis:* This session has no memory, past chats or web search. No earlier run exists to use as a baseline, and no market research was possible. Everything below rests on the dossier alone. Inferences are marked `[ASSUMPTION]`. Verdicts are disciplined hypotheses, not guarantees: Collins's method looks backward, and some companies he called great later declined.

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

The great Brightwater is a focused toy company, financially independent of the pencil business, run by one accountable leader under a board with independent directors. Its sales come mostly from a handful of brands that have sold for decades: owned classics like the build-a-face toy, licensed characters, and possibly a revived action-figure brand in a cheaper, lower-plastic format. It puts its advertising and development money behind those few brands every year instead of spreading it across many launches. It does not chase electronics, does not start side businesses, and does not ship anything a parent would later regret buying. Its cash flow is boring and dependable, which lets it pay down debt, restore the dividend, and add one enduring brand at a time. If it disappeared, a generation of children would lose toys their parents also grew up with.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence:** *Brightwater turns a small number of classic play patterns and well-loved characters into toy brands that children want year after year, and it judges itself by profit per enduring brand.*

| Circle | Candidates considered | Kept | Ruled out, and why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Best in the world at** | Electronic games; pencils; inventing a play category; keeping low-price classics alive for decades; turning characters into toy lines | **Keeping a few play patterns and characters alive and selling for decades.** Evidence: a build-a-face toy sold for decades; a 12-inch action figure that created a category and once made up about two-thirds of sales; a comic-strip licence | **Electronics:** no capability, no cash, and a much larger Rival M. **Pencils:** already the country's largest maker, but that excellence belongs to the uncle's business and passion, not the toy company's. **Fads and novelty:** the lawn darts and water gun show both weakness and risk here. **Non-toy services and products:** nursery schools and cookware both failed |
| **Economic engine** | Profit per SKU; per launch; per advertising dollar; per selling season; per brand-year | **Profit per enduring brand**: operating contribution of each brand over its life, tracked yearly | Profit per SKU and per launch reward the proliferation the son is already cutting. Profit per season rewards hit-chasing, the thing that made the company fragile |
| **Passion** | Toys (the family's 50-year identity; the son's work); pencils (the uncle's) | **Toys**, under the son's stewardship `[ASSUMPTION: inferred from his role and his active pruning of the toy line, not from anything he has said]` | Pencils. That passion is real, but it belongs to someone who does not accept the toy leadership. This is the clearest sign the two businesses should be separate |

**Why this denominator:** Classic toys spread their tooling and advertising costs over many years. Hits do not; they bring volatility and inventory risk, which a debt-laden balance sheet cannot absorb. Measuring profit *per enduring brand* rewards longevity and concentration, and it penalises a large catalogue of short-lived products.

**Does it explain past wins?** Yes: the decades-long classic, the category-creating action figure while it lasted, and the profitable licence logic. **Does it rule things out?** Yes: electronics, diversification, novelties and SKU sprawl. **Is it uncomfortable to commit to?** Yes. It means giving up the industry's fastest-growing category and letting the profitable pencil unit go.

**Status: working hypothesis.** Collins's companies often took years to find theirs. It is confirmed if:
- (a) segment data shows the toy business is cash-positive after removing the failed ventures;
- (b) a brand-level P&L shows that a few long-lived brands produce most of the toy contribution;
- (c) retailer reorders for classics are stable from season to season;
- (d) any revived brand sells across at least three seasons, not just one.

## 3. The flywheel

```

        ▼                                              │
 [2] Back them EVERY year with concentrated           │

        │                                              │
        ▼                                              │

     → dependable retailer reorders and shelf space   │
        │                                              │
        ▼                                              │
 [4] Longer brand life spreads tooling and           │
     advertising cost → higher PROFIT PER             │
     ENDURING BRAND                                   │
        │                                              │
        ▼                                              │
 [5] Steady cash pays down debt → covenant room,     │
     dividend path, freedom to invest                 │
        │                                              │
        ▼                                              │

     ONE well-chosen brand at a time
```

**Weakest link: step 6 back to step 1 (renewal).** The owned brands are aging and depend on TV promotion, and debt starves reinvestment. If renewal stalls, step 3 decays.

**Current push (next one or two turns):**
1. Settle the pencil separation so that capital decisions belong to one owner, and allocate the debt deliberately.
2. Concentrate next season's advertising on the top-ranked brands. Run one capped, staged test of reviving the dormant action-figure brand in a lower-plastic, lower-cost format `[ASSUMPTION: smaller or lower-plastic formats address the cost problem that ended the 12-inch figure]`.

**Doom-loop check: past pattern present, current direction improving.**
- The last decade shows the classic doom loop: one hit dominating sales, then nursery schools, then cookware, then novelty products that had to be pulled.
- The son's cuts to products and launches since Year −2 are the first signs of the flywheel pattern.
- The live doom-loop temptation is to "solve" the growth problem by jumping into electronics or making a big acquisition.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop considering electronic games.** No capability, no cash, a much larger competitor. Revisit only if the technology can *accelerate* an existing brand (see the technology triage table in the appendix).
2. **Stop diversifying outside toys.** No services, cookware or subsidy-chasing ventures.
3. **Stop launching one-season novelties and fads.** Every launch must have a credible multi-year path.
4. **Stop shipping without an independent safety sign-off.** The safety gate has a veto over marketing.
5. **Stop running pencils and toys from one capital pool.** Separate them.
6. **Stop SKU sprawl.** Continue the son's cuts. Every line must earn its place on profit per enduring brand.
7. **Stop using the TV show as a disguised advertisement.** Set content standards or exit.
8. **Stop pursuing debt-funded acquisitions or "bet-the-season" launches** until covenants have real headroom.

**Non-negotiable disciplines:**
- Review the brand P&L every quarter.
- Concentrate advertising on the top brands.
- Apply a written launch test: fits the Hedgehog Concept, plans for multiple years, passes the safety gate.
- Report lender covenants to the board monthly.

**Freedom within the framework:** Brand teams have full autonomy over product design, refreshes and promotion *inside* their brand and budget. They have none over entering new categories.

**Bureaucracy check:** The family feud has probably created workaround approvals and duplicated capital fights `[ASSUMPTION]`. Remove them after the split rather than adding rules on top of them.

## 7. Core ideology and the BHAG

**Core values:**
1. Children's safety comes before any launch date.
2. Play value over gimmicks.
3. Brands are looked after, not used up.
4. Honesty with parents, retailers and lenders.
5. Family ownership, professional governance.

**Core purpose:** *To bring lasting play into children's lives, the kind parents remember and pass on.*

**BHAG (20-year horizon):** *Own more decades-old, still-bestselling toy brands than any company in the industry.* `[ASSUMPTION: the industry has no such benchmark yet. It should be defined by counting brands with 20+ years of continuous sales.]`

**Clock-building mechanisms:**
- Independent directors.
- A quarterly brand P&L review.
- A written launch test.
- A safety gate with veto power.
- A documented succession bench.
- Each brand's long-term plan owned by a brand team, not by the chief executive.
