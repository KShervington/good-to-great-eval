# Ashcombe Company: Good to Great

## One-screen verdict

**Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** *Ashcombe makes the world's best shaving systems: handles that win a shaver's loyalty and cartridges they rebuy for years. It grows only by raising profit per shaver per year.*

**Answer to the central question in shaving:** put the money into premium cartridge systems. Keep the disposable as a capped flank whose job is to deny Rival B a foothold and to move users up to systems. Do not fight a price war with it. The option to "do both" with equal force is the comfortable answer, and it is the one that keeps Ashcombe merely good.

**Weakest link in the flywheel:** the step from *shaver acquired* to *shaver locked into recurring cartridges*. Every disposable sold in place of a refill breaks the handle-and-cartridge lock-in and lowers profit on that sale.

**Top three stop-doing items:**
1. Stop fighting the lighter price war with Rival B. Exit or sell the lighter business.
2. Stop defending total razor *unit* share with cheap disposables. Measure profit per shaver instead.
3. Stop diversifying into new categories. Declare a moratorium on acquisitions and launches outside shaving and proven repeat-purchase personal care.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** demand for disposables has outrun the company's expectations. Each one makes less money than the refill it replaces, and Ashcombe launched the product itself.

**Missing numbers that would change the answer:**
- **Profit by line, especially the new deodorant.** If it earns profit per consumer comparable to blades, the Hedgehog widens to repeat-purchase personal care.
- **Disposables' share of U.S. shaving, and its trend.** If disposables are heading toward a majority, the capped-flank stance has to be revisited.
- **ROE and earnings trend against the market.** Needed to test "superior performance".
- **International share of sales.** If it is large, global distribution becomes its own circle.

Greatness potential: 62/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

The great Ashcombe is a shaving company that happens to own a few disciplined repeat-purchase businesses. It is not a diversified consumer-goods company that happens to own razors.

- **Product cadence.** It always has its next, better shaving system in development. It launches each one at a premium and markets it heavily enough to move its huge installed base of shavers up to it. It answers any competitor's blade improvement before the market notices the gap. The stainless-steel episode, which cost about 10 share points for good, does not happen again.
- **Disposables.** It sells a disposable only as an entry ramp and a defensive wall. It never sells one as a substitute for the system.
- **Exits.** It has left businesses where its only possible edge would be low price, chiefly lighters and probably low-end pens. That is the ground Rival B was built for.
- **Cash.** Cash from blades goes back into blade technology, manufacturing scale and brand, not into the next watch or calculator venture.
- **Measures.** It judges itself by profit per shaver per year and by value share, not by how many plastic razors it ships.

**Intersection tests:**
- *Does it explain past wins?* Yes. The twin-blade system, the improved twin-blade system and blades' 71% of pretax profit all sit inside it.
- *Does it explain past failures?* Yes. Watches, calculators, perfume, hair coloring and smoke alarms all sit outside it, and margins shrank during that era.
- *Does it rule things out?* Yes: lighters, price-led disposables, new non-shaving acquisitions and non-shaver small appliances.
- *Is it uncomfortable to commit to?* Yes. It means accepting that unit share may fall while disposables grow. It means walking away from roughly a decade of lighter investment. It means telling a German subsidiary that its non-shaving appliances are not the future.

**Open edge: toiletries.** The new deodorant cost about $118M and won about a quarter of its market. That is real evidence of skill in repeat-purchase personal care. It stays on the bus *under test*. Keep it if its profit per consumer clears the shaving hurdle, but launch no new toiletry categories. If it clears comfortably, the Hedgehog may widen to "premium repeat-purchase grooming". That cannot be decided without profit by line.

**Confirmation tests (next 12–24 months):**
1. Profit per shaver per year rises while disposables are capped.
2. Disposable buyers trade up to systems at a measurable rate. If trade-up is negligible, the "entry ramp" role is false and the disposable is purely cannibalistic.
3. The next premium system carries a price premium without losing value share.

**Current push (the next one or two turns):**
1. **Turn 2:** commit publicly, inside the company, to the next premium system and fund it ahead of disposable expansion. The gaps between launches so far are about six years: the improved system came three years ago, the first about nine.
2. **Turn 3–4:** write a disposable charter. It should set a price floor, rule out promotional wars with Rival B, and add trade-up mechanics such as packaging, offers and in-store placement next to systems. That gives the disposable a job inside the loop instead of letting it leak out of it.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop the lighter price war.** Set a deadline to sell or close the lighter business.
2. **Stop using the disposable to defend total unit share.** Never undercut your own refill on price or promotion.
3. **Stop entering new categories** by acquisition or launch. That means no repeat of watches, calculators, perfume, smoke alarms or hair coloring. Declare a formal moratorium.
4. **Stop investing in non-shaving small appliances** at the German subsidiary.
5. **Stop competing on price in pens.** Run them for cash and put no new money in.
6. **Stop launching new toiletry categories.** Defend existing winners only where profit per consumer clears the hurdle.
7. **Stop reporting razor performance by unit share.** Report profit per shaver and value share first.
8. **Stop treating Rival B as the enemy to beat on every front.** Fight it only where Ashcombe's edge, technology, can win, which is premium shaving.

## 7. Core ideology and the BHAG

**Core values** `[ASSUMPTION: inferred, not stated in the dossier]`:
1. A product that is clearly, demonstrably better.
2. The shaver's daily experience comes first.
3. Discipline: do only what we can be best at.
4. Long-term ownership over quarterly convenience.

**Core purpose:** to give people the best possible shave, every day, everywhere.

**BHAG (20-year horizon):** *Every major shaving system the world adopts for the next 20 years is an Ashcombe system, and Ashcombe earns the majority of the world's blade-and-razor profit.*

**Clock-building mechanisms:**
- **Product cadence rule:** the next shaving system is always funded and in development the day the current one launches.
- **Capital allocation rule:** new money goes only where it raises profit per repeat consumer at or above the shaving hurdle.
- **Competitive-response trigger:** any competitor blade innovation automatically starts a response program.
- **Succession bench:** at least two internal candidates, reviewed every year.
- **Standing red-flag metrics:** disposable mix and value share appear on every board agenda.
