- **Weakest link in the flywheel:** *trade-up*. The plan assumes shavers will pay a premium for a noticeably better cartridge system instead of drifting to cheap disposables. The fact that disposable demand is beating forecasts cuts directly against this assumption.

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

A great Ashcombe is a shaving company that also happens to own a few other businesses, not a consumer-goods conglomerate that happens to own razors. It sets the pace of the world's shaving technology. Every several years it launches a system that shavers can feel is better. It earns its money on the refills that system locks in, and it rolls each launch out across every market it sells in. It concedes the bottom of the market on purpose and does not let a low-price rival pull it into price wars. Cash from shaving funds the next generation of shaving, not the next diversification. Its main measure is profit per shaver-year, not total razor units or company sales. If it disappeared, the world's best shave would go with it.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   │      and scale cost advantage in blade making
   │            │
   │            ▼
   └── 6. Deeper worldwide share of the premium profit pool
```

**Current push (next 1–2 turns):**
1. **Turn 2:** commit the multi-year budget and team for the next-generation system now. Don't wait for disposables to settle the question.
2. **Turn 4:** re-price and re-position the disposable so it stops cannibalizing refills. It should guard the entry point, not replace the system.

**Doom-loop check:**
- **The previous era was a textbook doom loop.** It made new bets in unrelated categories one after another (hair color, perfume, watches, calculators, smoke alarms, fire extinguishers), margins shrank, and the chosen successor left after 15 months.
- **The current CEO is reversing it** through divestitures. That is the right direction.
- **Live doom-loop risk:** "try to do both" in shaving. Pushing the disposable hard while also funding premium systems would mean reacting to Rival B without understanding the profit math.
- **Second live risk:** using the deodorant's success as a reason to fund more $100M+ category launches.

**Gate verdict: PASS.** The loop closes. The weakest link is trade-up (3 → 4), and that open question is logged under Gate 5.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop the lighter price war.** Exit disposable lighters within 12 months by sale or wind-down.
2. **Stop growing the disposable.** No share-chasing promotions and no advertising that steers refill buyers toward it. Price it to protect profit per shaver-year.
3. **Stop reporting total razor unit share as the headline shaving metric.** Replace it with profit per shaver-year and premium dollar share.
4. **Stop entering new categories.** No new $100M+ launches outside shaving, and no extensions of the deodorant into new toiletry categories.
5. **Stop putting growth capital into pens.** Harvest them, and sell when the price is fair.
6. **Stop expanding the German subsidiary's non-shaving appliance lines.**
7. **Stop treating the U.S. antitrust case as part of the strategy.** Settle or contain it. Don't build plans that depend on winning.
8. **Stop matching Rival B on price in any category.** Compete where technology earns a premium, and concede where it doesn't.

**Non-negotiable disciplines:**
- Every capital request answers: *Does this raise profit per shaver-year?*
- A continuous system-innovation pipeline, with the next generation always funded.
- Each launch rolls out worldwide as one coordinated program.

**Framework boundaries (freedom within a framework):** business units have full autonomy over execution, marketing tactics and cost. The center controls capital outside shaving and any change to the Hedgehog.

**Bureaucracy check:** any approval layers added to police the diversified portfolio should come off as that portfolio shrinks.

**Gate verdict: PASS.**
