# Good to Great: Lindqvist Company

## One-screen verdict

**Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** *Lindqvist will be the #1 or #2 one-stop food-and-drug store in every local market it serves, and will measure itself by profit per person living in those markets.*

- **Weakest link in the flywheel:** capital. Replacing a base of mostly small conventional stores with large combination stores costs a great deal, and the price war is squeezing the margins that would pay for it. The money has to come from three places: exiting weak markets, freezing general-merchandise and plant spending, and ending remodels of small stores.
- **Top three stop-doing items:**
  1. Stop putting capital into small conventional stores. Replace them with combination stores or close them.
  2. Stop operating in any market without a credible path to #1 or #2.
  3. Stop treating manufacturing and the general-merchandise "family centers" as growth engines.

- **Missing numbers that would change this answer:**
  - Test-store economics: combination stores versus conventional stores, in the same market.
  - Market share by metro area.
  - Current margins and return on equity versus the industry.
  - Return on capital of the plants.
  - What the consumer research says about one-stop shopping.

  If the combination stores do not beat conventional stores on profit per local population, the Hedgehog Concept is wrong.

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Lindqvist is not the biggest national grocer. It is the dominant store in each market it chooses to be in. Almost every store it runs is a large combination store: a full supermarket, a pharmacy and drugstore, and specialty departments under one roof. The goal is that a household can do its whole weekly shop in one trip. It is #1 or #2 in every market it serves. Where it cannot get there, it leaves early and without drama. Its plants make an honest, trusted store brand and serve the stores, not the other way round. It uses checkout scanning, where it is first in the industry, to turn its long habit of treating purchases as shoppers' "votes" into an item-by-item, store-by-store decision system. It does not try to beat Rival A on price everywhere. It beats Rival A on the whole trip, in fewer markets, with more density in each.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence:** Be the #1 or #2 one-stop food-and-drug store in every market we serve, measured by profit per local population.

| Circle | Candidates considered | Chosen | Explicitly *not* |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Best in the world at** | Vertical integration and manufacturing; lowest prices; national coverage; general merchandise; **combined food-and-drug stores**; consumer research | Large one-stop food-and-drug combination stores, run on shopper data, in markets where it can lead | Lowest price nationally (Rival A is bigger than twice the nearest competitor); general-merchandise discounting; manufacturing as a business in its own right; being everywhere |
| **Economic engine** | Profit per store; per square foot; per customer visit; per plant; **per local population** | **Profit per local population** (profit per household in the trade areas it serves) | Total sales or store count as the main goal |
| **Passion** | Manufacturing excellence; deal-making; **what shoppers actually choose** (the "votes" tradition) | Giving shoppers what their votes say they want, in one trip `[ASSUMPTION: the research culture is still alive inside the company]` | Building monuments, such as "world's largest" plants |

**Why this denominator:** Profit per local population rewards density and share within a market, not raw spread. It explains past exits, since the company has retreated from markets where it was weak. It also turns future exits into a deliberate rule instead of a reaction.

**Tests:**
- *Does it explain past wins?* Partly. The format experiments the company chose (drugstores next door, specialty departments in larger stores) point toward one-stop. The many market exits are consistent with weak local share being unprofitable. `[ASSUMPTION]`
- *Does it rule things out?* Yes. It rules out family centers, a plant-led strategy, small-store remodels, scattered markets and a blanket price war.
- *Is it uncomfortable?* Yes. It means rebuilding most of the store base, possibly leaving the West Coast chain, and demoting a manufacturing legacy that the previous leader built and that just produced the world's largest dairy plant.

**Confirmation evidence:**
1. Combination test stores beat conventional stores in the same market on profit per household in their trade area, over 12–18 months.
2. The metros where Lindqvist is #1 or #2 earn clearly higher returns than its weaker metros.
3. Scanner and research data show households consolidating trips into combination stores.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop remodeling or expanding small conventional stores.** Every store is either replaced with a combination store or put on a closing schedule.
2. **Stop operating in markets with no credible path to #1 or #2** within a set period `[ASSUMPTION: about 5 years]`. The West Coast chain and any remaining weak metros go under explicit review.
3. **Stop investing in the family-center general-merchandise division.** Freeze capital spending now and decide on divesting it within 12 months.
4. **Stop building new plant capacity ahead of store needs.** Manufacturing capital spending only replaces existing capacity until the store program is funded. No more "world's largest" projects.
5. **Stop matching Rival A's deep-discount program across the board.** Compete on price where share is at stake, and win on the whole trip everywhere else.
6. **Stop any product specification that trades honesty for cost.** No added water or fillers, and no hiding behind the minimum legal standard.
7. **Stop running drugstores as a separate business** that wins or loses on its own. Each one becomes part of a combination store.
8. **Stop making growth decisions without market-level profit data.** No new store, exit or acquisition goes forward without the metro analysis.

## 7. Core ideology and the BHAG

**Core values:**
1. The shopper's vote decides. Data comes before opinion.
2. Honest products, with the store-brand promise kept every time.
3. Lead locally or leave.
4. Frugal with capital, generous with the customer.

**Core purpose:** to make a household's whole weekly shop easy, honest and affordable in the neighborhoods we serve.

**BHAG (25 years):** be #1 or #2 in every market we serve, and through that local leadership pass Rival A to become the country's largest grocer. Size is the *result* of local dominance, never the goal in itself.

**Clock-building mechanisms:**
- An annual market review: every metro is classified as lead, fight or exit against the #1/#2 rule.
- A capital-allocation rule: capital goes only to projects that raise profit per local population.
- An independent quality audit that reports to the board.
- Monthly reviews of scanner and research data at store and category level.
- Written succession for the president and every market president.
