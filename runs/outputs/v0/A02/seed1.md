# Good to Great: Corwin Paper Company

## Verdict (one screen)

**Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** *Corwin makes America's best household paper towels and bath tissue at the lowest cost in the industry, and sells them under a few heavily advertised brands that families ask for by name.*

- **Weakest link in the flywheel:** product superiority. Rival P now leads on perceived softness, and Corwin has no answer. Without a better product, the advertising and scale that follow it cannot work.
- **Top three stop-doing items:**
  1. Divest the four non-core acquisitions: technical films, fine papers, furniture and teaching aids.
  2. Stop treating "diversify into growth industries" as the strategy.
  3. Stop running the company by committee or consensus. Appoint one accountable chief executive.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** Rival P, the strongest packaged-goods marketer in the country, has gone national this year with a softer premium tissue and very heavy advertising. Corwin is answering with acquisitions in unrelated industries.
- **Missing numbers that would change this answer:**
  - Current branded share by category and region. If towel and tissue share have already collapsed, the Hedgehog may need to narrow to towels alone.
  - Blind consumer softness tests against Rival P. If the gap cannot be closed technically, the "best in world" circle fails.
  - Profit and loss of each acquisition. A hidden winner would change the stop-doing list.
  - Capital cost of the mill modernization and pollution-control program.

Greatness potential: 25/100

*This is honest, not pessimistic. Corwin still has real assets: brands it invented, a heritage as the low-cost producer, and decades of advertising muscle. But it would have to beat the best consumer marketer in the country on that marketer's chosen ground, while also choosing a leader, funding a large environmental capital bill, and unwinding about $200M of diversification. Very few companies make that turn.*

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Corwin is a focused consumer-products company, not a diversified paper conglomerate. It does a few things in household paper (towels and bath tissue, and possibly facial tissue) better than anyone. It wins blind tests on the attributes consumers actually feel, especially softness and absorbency. It runs the lowest-cost tissue and towel mills in the industry. It reinvests that cost advantage in relentless, consistent brand support, including modern consumer promotion where needed. The non-core businesses have been sold, and the proceeds fund mill modernization and pollution control. One accountable chief executive, chosen for ambition for the company rather than for themselves, runs a team that argues hard about facts and then executes in one direction. The company is great when three things are true: its returns again lead the paper industry, its brands hold or gain share against Rival P, and the system no longer depends on any one leader.

**Sentence:** *Corwin makes America's best household paper towels and bath tissue at the lowest cost in the industry, and sells them under a few heavily advertised brands that families ask for by name.*

**Does it rule things out?** Yes: all four acquisitions, any further diversification, re-entry into private label, and launching consumer products without a demonstrable product advantage.

**Confirmation tests (status: working hypothesis):**

2. National branded share in towels and tissue stops declining, and the north-central region stabilizes.
3. Profit per brand in the core rises after divestiture, even before share growth.
4. The leadership team can state the Hedgehog Concept and say what it rules out, without dissent along the "brands vs. portfolio" line.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   ┌─► 1. Products consumers can FEEL are better
   │      (softness, absorbency, strength) — blind-test proven
   │                     │
   │                     ▼
   │   2. Few brands, heavy and consistent advertising
   │      + competitive promotion where needed
   │                     │
   │                     ▼
   │   3. Brand preference → share and volume
   │                     │
   │                     ▼
   │   4. Full, modern tissue/towel mills → lowest cost per unit
   │                     │
   │                     ▼
   │   5. PROFIT PER BRAND (denominator)
   │                     │
   │                     ▼
   └── 6. Reinvest: product R&D + mill modernization (incl. pollution control)
```

The loop closes: step 6 feeds step 1.

**Weakest link:** Step 1 is currently broken on softness. Step 2 also needs work, because Rival P's advertising budget is likely larger `[ASSUMPTION]` and Corwin refuses coupons.

**Current push (the next two turns):**
1. **Close the softness gap.** Run a dedicated product and process program with a hard blind-test target.
2. **Redirect capital.** Use divestiture proceeds to fund modernization of the tissue and towel mills, so that required pollution-control spending also improves product quality and cost.

**Doom-loop check (failed: Corwin is in a doom loop today):**
- *Reaction without understanding:* it answered a maturing core by buying growth, with no diagnosis of why the core was losing.
- *New direction after new direction:* a 40-year founder-era CEO, then a shared "office of the chairman," then that office dissolved, then a new chairman.
- *Big programs to jump-start momentum:* about $200M of acquisitions in six years, plus a diaper launch without proven superiority.
- *Inconsistency:* management is split between "consumer brands" and "diversified portfolio."

The new chairman's first test is to *not* answer this with another big bold move.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Sell the plastic-coating and technical-films businesses.** They are struggling and have no connection to household brands.
2. **Sell the fine book and printing papers business.** Its profits are falling, and it is commodity-like and not branded for households.
3. **Sell the casual-furniture maker.** There is no plausible link to any circle.
4. **Sell both audio-visual teaching-aid makers.** Same reason.
5. **Stop all acquisitions outside household paper.** Make this a board rule, not a preference.
6. **Stop launching consumer products without proven blind-test superiority.** This covers re-launching the diaper or anything else until it wins on product.
7. **Stop treating "no coupons, ever" as dogma.** Decide promotion policy from regional test data, not from tradition. (This means stopping a *refusal*, not starting a price war.)
8. **Stop committee leadership at the top.** Have one accountable CEO, with debate before decisions and alignment after them.
9. **Stop letting mill capital requests compete on volume.** Rank them by their impact on consumer-product quality and cost.

**Non-negotiable disciplines:**
- Every consumer product beats or matches the best competitor in blind tests before launch and annually afterward.
- Every capital dollar is ranked against the flywheel.
- Branded share by category and region is reported monthly to the CEO and board, with no averaging that hides regional losses.

**Framework boundaries (freedom within a framework):**
- *Fixed:* the category scope (household paper), the few-brands rule, the quality bar, and the capital ranking rule.
- *Free:* how brand teams win in their regions, which mill improvements are pursued, and how R&D attacks softness.

**Bureaucracy check:** the shared "office of the chairman" was structure standing in for a single right person in the top seat. Do not rebuild it.

2. Do a few things better than anyone.
3. Lowest cost as a discipline, not a slogan.
4. Face the facts first.

**BHAG (20-year horizon):** *Be the household paper company American families choose most, with the industry's highest returns, ahead of the best consumer marketer in the country.*

**Clock-building mechanisms (so the company does not depend on one leader):**
- A formal CEO succession process with internal candidates developed over 3–5 years. The 40-year tenure followed by a failed committee shows this clock never existed.

- A board-level capital allocation rule tied to the Hedgehog Concept.
- Monthly regional share red-flag reporting.
- A written stop-doing list reviewed by the board every year.
