# Good to Great: Heart Failure Transitions Program (HFTP), St. Aldric Regional Medical Center

## One-screen verdict

**Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** *HFTP keeps St. Aldric's highest-risk heart-failure patients alive and out of the hospital for 90 days, by untangling their medicines early and reaching them at home when the phone can't.*

**Weakest flywheel link:** getting from "touched in the first 30 days" to "fewer hospital days at 90 days, proven." The program's 30-day readmission advantage over non-enrolled patients is 5.9 points. By day 90 it has shrunk to **1.2 points** (32.7% vs 33.9%). Even that gap comes from a comparison that favors HFTP. No one has yet measured the program's effect fairly.

**Top three stop-doing items:**
1. **Don't take on Hospital at Home now.** Say "not yet, and not this team as lead" until the core program has proof at 90 days.
2. **Stop graduating everyone at day 30.** That date is the penalty clock, not the patient's clock.
3. **Stop presenting the enrolled vs. not-enrolled 30-day gap (15.9% vs 21.8%) as HFTP's effect.** The not-enrolled group includes nursing-facility discharges, patients who can't be reached and patients who declined, so the comparison is confounded by design.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** the most important outcome number HFTP has, 90-day readmissions, appears in a pharmacist's accreditation checklist and not in the memo to the VP. It suggests the program mostly *delays* readmissions rather than preventing them. Surveyors may ask about it, and Finance is about to price the program's value.

**Missing numbers that would change this answer:**
- 90-day readmissions and **deaths** by risk tier, against a fair comparison group
- the breakdown of the 556 non-enrolled patients (declined / unreachable / nursing facility)
- Finance's cost per readmission or bed-day, and whether beds are capacity-constrained
- HF readmission rates from before HFTP launched
- verification of the pharmacist's 90-day data pull

`Greatness potential: 25/100`

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

In its great version, HFTP is the reason St. Aldric's sickest heart-failure patients are still home and alive three months after discharge, and it can prove that against a fair comparison, not just against patients who never had a chance to enroll.

- **Who it serves:** it finds the patients most likely to bounce back, including the ones who can't be reached by phone and the ones who say no at the bedside, and goes to them in person.
- **What it does first:** pharmacists fix the medication tangles that put people like Mr. D. back in the hospital, and they do it in the first week, not at day 10.
- **How long it stays:** patients graduate when they are stable, not when the penalty window closes. Low-risk patients get a light touch, so the team's capacity goes where it changes outcomes.
- **How it shows value:** HFTP reports value in 90-day bed-days avoided per program dollar, a number Finance computed and the VP trusts.
- **What it says no to:** it says no to attractive expansions, including Hospital at Home, until the core is proven. Greatness here is a narrower program with deeper effects.

## 3. The flywheel (Gate 8)

```
 ① Find the highest-risk HF discharges
    (risk score at admission; include the "unreachable" and "declined")
        ↓
 ② Fix medicines by day 7 (pharmacist) + reach them in person (CHW / navigator)
        ↓
 ③ Keep high-risk patients engaged to day 90
    (graduate on stability, not the calendar; low-risk get a light touch)
        ↓
 ④ Fewer 90-day bed-days, no rise in deaths, measured against a fair comparison
        ↓
 ⑤ Finance-validated value per program dollar → reinvest
    (pharmacist coverage, CHW capacity)
        ↓
 ⑥ Outcome data by risk tier shows who benefits → sharper targeting
        └────────────→ back to ①
```

The loop closes. The denominator (bed-days per dollar) sits at step ⑤, where it funds step ①.

**Weakest link: ③ → ④.** Today the protocol ends at day 30, and the 90-day gap is 1.2 points. Without a fair measurement at step ④, step ⑤ runs on anecdotes and a hospital-wide rate HFTP can't claim, and the flywheel has nothing to push against.

**Current push (next one or two turns):**
1. **Measure step ④ honestly**: 90-day readmissions, bed-days and deaths, by risk tier, against a fair comparison, and priced by Finance.
2. **Strengthen ② → ③ for high-risk patients**: pharmacist reconciliation by day 7, and contact through day 90 for the top risk tier.

**Doom-loop check: warning signs present.**
- **A big program to jump-start momentum:** Hospital at Home adds 6 FTE, a new department, a vendor platform, and an external study as the evidence.
- **Pursuit of "more" instead of "better":** a weekend shift to reach a blanket 75% enrollment target.
- **Reacting without understanding:** the VP's value question is being answered with "hope to have something for the budget cycle." The danger is grabbing the flattering 30-day number.
- **No signs of inconsistency so far:** the protocol has been stable and turnover is low. That is good.

**Verdict: `CONDITIONAL`.** The loop closes, but its weakest link has no evidence under it.
- *Condition:* the same as Gate 5.
- *Evidence that would make it PASS:* a fair 90-day comparison plus Finance's valuation.

## 5. Stop-doing list and disciplines (Gate 6)

### Stop doing
1. **Stop pursuing the lead role in Hospital at Home now.** Recommend to the VP and CMO that HFTP serve as a referral and advisory partner, and revisit combining the two after 12 months of 90-day evidence. This is the attractive opportunity the stop-doing list exists for.
2. **Stop the default day-30 graduation** for high-risk patients. Graduate on clinical stability. Low-risk patients can still finish at 30 days or sooner.
3. **Stop presenting enrolled vs. not-enrolled 30-day rates as the program's effect,** in internal memos, to Finance and to the board quality committee.
4. **Stop the blanket 75% enrollment target and the weekend shift** until risk data shows who is being missed. Replace them with a reach target for the high-risk tier.
5. **Stop giving the same six touches to every enrollee.** Tier the intensity.
6. **Stop treating "can't be reached by phone" as a reason not to enroll.** It is a reason to send a CHW.
7. **Stop claiming the hospital-wide rate and the penalty reduction as HFTP results.** Report them as context.
8. **Stop using patient satisfaction (n = 391) as evidence of value.** Keep it as a service measure only.

### Non-negotiable disciplines (fanatical consistency)
- Every high-risk enrollee gets a pharmacist medication reconciliation **by day 7**, covering every prescriber.
- Every enrollee gets a risk tier, documented at enrollment.
- A monthly report on **90-day** readmissions, bed-days and deaths, by tier and against the comparison group, and the worst number goes first.
- A blameless review of a sample of readmissions within 90 days each month: what would have prevented it?

### Framework boundaries (freedom and responsibility)
- **Fixed:** the risk tiers, the day-7 pharmacist standard, 90-day measurement and the blameless reviews.
- **Free:** navigators and CHWs set visit cadence, method (phone, home or clinic) and the graduation date within the tier.

### Bureaucracy check
The fixed six-touch calendar is a rule that substitutes for clinical judgment. With an experienced, low-turnover team, it can be relaxed into tiered judgment.

**Verdict: `PASS`.**
