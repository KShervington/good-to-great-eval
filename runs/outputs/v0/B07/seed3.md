## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Tolland is smaller in footprint, denser in its home region, and far harder to dislodge. It is the first-choice discount store in nearly every small town from Maine to the Chesapeake. It holds each town on everyday low prices, trade-area direct mail, and store managers who own their Monday scorecards. It grows by opening stores in the next town over, paid for from its own store profits. It does not grow by buying other people's broken chains. It carries modest debt. It runs one format. It knows its profit per store by cohort every week. A successor team built inside the company could run it without the founders. When Rival W reaches a Tolland town, it finds an incumbent with lower costs in that trade area, deeper loyalty and better local knowledge, and it struggles to take the town.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop acquiring chains.** Formal moratorium until (a) converted stores reach about 90% of core profit per store for four straight quarters, and (b) debt is under the cap `[ASSUMPTION thresholds]`.
2. **Stop running variety stores and non-core formats.** Exit Florida variety and any acquired variety format.
3. **Stop chasing 12% annual growth.** Withdraw it publicly and replace it with a profit-per-store target. A growth target turns the denominator upside down.
4. **Stop funding expansion with debt.** Board-set leverage cap: under 60% debt-to-net-worth in 12 months and under 40% in 24 months `[ASSUMPTION]`.
5. **Stop holding stores in markets where Tolland can't become the leading discounter.** Prune the 14-state footprint back toward contiguous trade areas.
6. **Stop buying because it's cheap or for tax credits.** Price paid is not a strategy.
7. **Stop converting everything at once.** Sequence conversions by trade area, starting with those nearest the core.
8. **Stop running sales events to prop up converted-store comps.** Keep EDLP everywhere, even when it hurts in the short term.

**Non-negotiable disciplines (done every time):** everyday low prices on everything; the Monday scorecard reviewed in every store, *including converted ones*; trade-area direct mail; and a test that every new store sits next to the network.

**Framework:** inside the format, price policy and scorecard, store managers own merchandising and local decisions. The framework itself, meaning which markets, which formats and how much debt, is set by board-approved rules, not by individual deal judgment.

**Bureaucracy check:** if integration produces layers of approval or control, ask whether the controls exist because acquired-store managers aren't yet trusted. If so, the fix is a people decision, not more rules.

## 7. Core ideology and BHAG

- **Core purpose:** give small-town families the same low prices and choice that big-city shoppers get.
- **Core values:**
  1. Honest everyday low prices, with no games.
  2. Respect for the small-town customer.
  3. Measure everything, weekly, and face what it says.
  4. Thrift: spend only what earns its keep.
  5. Ownership at the store level.
- **BHAG (25 years):** *Every small town from Maine to the Chesapeake shops at Tolland first, and no national discounter takes a single one of those towns from us.*
- **Clock-building mechanisms:**
  - a board-approved acquisition and leverage policy;
  - an adjacency rule for new stores;
  - the cohort-level Monday scorecard;
  - a store-manager development pipeline that feeds the executive ranks;
  - a written succession plan with a named internal successor.
