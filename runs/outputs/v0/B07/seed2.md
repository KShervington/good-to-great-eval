# Tolland Stores, Inc.: Good to Great

## One-screen verdict

**Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** *Tolland is the everyday-low-price discount store that small Northeast towns depend on, and it grows only where it can earn top four-wall profit per store.*

**Weakest flywheel link:** growth is funded by debt and by distressed acquisitions, not by cash from proven stores. Half of sales now come from stores that have never shown a profit under the Tolland model.

**Top three stop-doing items**
1. Stop acquiring chains. Freeze all deals until the converted stores pass a four-wall profit test and debt is back under a set ceiling.
2. Drop the public target of about 12% growth a year. Replace it with a profit-per-store target.
3. Exit variety stores and other non-core formats, in Florida and in the acquired chain, and use the proceeds to pay down debt.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** Tolland raised debt to about 80% of net worth to buy a chain in 14 new states. Within three months it had closed or sold about 130 of that chain's stores. Nobody, possibly including management, yet knows whether the remaining stores earn money in Tolland form. Full-year Year −1 earnings are not known.

**Missing numbers that would change this answer**
- Year −1 earnings and interest coverage
- Debt maturities and covenants
- Four-wall profit of converted stores compared with core stores
- Comparable-store sales for core stores
- Shrinkage
- How far Rival W's stores now are from Tolland's markets
- Succession plan

Greatness potential: 20/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Tolland is smaller in footprint and deeper in density. It is the dominant everyday-low-price discount store in hundreds of small towns from northern New England to the mid-Atlantic. Its stores are clustered tightly enough that distribution, direct mail and store supervision cost less per store than any rival's.

Every store, including every converted one, runs on the Monday scorecard and earns a four-wall profit near the core average. Any store that can't is sold.

Growth pays for itself: new stores open one at a time in towns the company already understands, funded by cash flow and not by debt. There are no variety stores and no scattered states. The company runs on a leadership bench of former store managers that outlasts the founding group. When Rival W arrives, it finds Tolland already the low-cost incumbent in every town worth having.

**Alternative denominator considered:** profit per small-town trade area. It better captures local dominance and density, but it is harder to measure. Revisit it once data on individual stores is reliable.

**Tests**
- *Does it explain past wins?* Yes. Thirty years of profits and stock returns that kept pace with Rival W's came from the core small-town model.
- *Does it rule things out?* Yes: variety stores, roughly 14 new states, the growth target, and the next acquisition.
- *Is it uncomfortable?* Yes. It contradicts the company's last five years of strategy and its own public growth target.

**What would confirm it within 12 months**
- Core stores show positive comparable-store sales.
- Converted stores that sit in or near the core region reach about 75% of core four-wall profit `[ASSUMPTION threshold]`, while distant ones do not.
- Core stores keep their margins in towns where they compete head-to-head with Rival K.

**What would falsify it:** core stores are declining in comparable sales and acquisitions were hiding it. In that case the problem is the core, not the scope, and the Hedgehog Concept has to be rebuilt.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   ┌─► 1. Lowest operating cost
   │      (lean stores, cheap real estate, systems,
   │       dense distribution & direct mail)
   │                 │
   │                 ▼
   │   2. Everyday low prices on everything
   │                 │
   │                 ▼
   │   3. Small-town shoppers come from the whole
   │      surrounding area; Tolland beats Rival K
   │                 │
   │                 ▼
   │   4. HIGH FOUR-WALL PROFIT PER STORE  ◄── denominator
   │                 │
   │                 ▼
   │   5. Cash + buying power with suppliers
   │                 │
   │                 ▼
   └── 6. Self-funded new stores, clustered in
          the existing Northeast region
          (WEAKEST LINK: currently debt + acquisitions)
```

The loop closes: clustering at step 6 lowers distribution, advertising and supervision costs at step 1.

**Current push (next one or two turns)**
1. Prove step 4 in the acquired stores. Put every converted store on the Monday scorecard with a four-wall P&L, then keep, fix or exit it within one or two quarters.
2. Repair step 6. Use cash from divestments and core stores to pay down debt before any new growth.

**Doom-loop check**

| Sign | Present? |
|---|---|
| Big acquisitions to jump-start growth | **Yes.** Sales roughly doubled in one deal, after several earlier deals. |
| A new direction after every result | **Partly.** The company moved from a rural regional chain to an operator in many states and formats within about five years. |
| Reacting without understanding | **Likely `[ASSUMPTION]`.** About 130 stores were closed within three months of purchase, which suggests the deal was priced before the stores were fully understood. |
| Inconsistency | **Yes, in format.** Variety stores and discount stores now run side by side. |

The doom loop is already turning. The flywheel only wins if pushes go back to steps 4 and 6.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop acquiring chains.** No deals until converted stores pass the four-wall test and debt is under the ceiling.
2. **Stop publishing a 12% growth target.** Replace it with four-wall profit per store and comparable-store sales.
3. **Stop operating variety stores**, in Florida and in the acquired chain.
4. **Stop financing growth with debt.** Set a leverage ceiling, for example debt below 40% of net worth `[ASSUMPTION]`, and fund new stores only from cash flow.
5. **Stop converting stores on a calendar.** Convert a store only when it is projected to pass the four-wall test. Otherwise sell it.
6. **Stop operating in states where Tolland cannot build density.** Plan exits from the outlying states among the 14 that came with the acquisition `[ASSUMPTION: some will fail density]`.
7. **Stop reporting results as totals.** Report every acquired cohort store by store, separate from the core.
8. **Stop running promotional sales events** in converted stores "to build traffic." That abandons everyday low prices.

## 7. Core ideology and the BHAG

**Core purpose:** to give small-town families the prices and selection of a big-city store, close to home.

**Core values**
- **Honest everyday low prices:** no fake markdowns.
- **Thrift:** every dollar of cost shows up in the shopper's price.
- **Measured every week:** the facts come before opinions.
- **Respect for the small-town customer and the store team.**

**BHAG (20-year horizon):** be the leading discount store in every Northeast small town worth serving, funded entirely by its own cash flow.

**Mechanisms that make it work without the founders ("clock building")**
- The Monday scorecard covers every store, including four-wall profit and shrinkage.
- A four-wall test decides whether any store opens, converts or stays.
- The board sets a leverage ceiling that requires a supermajority to exceed.
- A succession plan and a leadership bench drawn from store managers.
- Independent directors review every proposed acquisition.

**Decline-risk register (Gate 10)**

| Stage | How Tolland could fall into it | Early warning sign |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Hubris born of success | "We beat Rival K and returned 60×, so we can run anyone's stores." | Deals justified with "we've done this before" rather than with data on individual stores |

| 4. Grasping for salvation | A larger acquisition to "fix" the last one, or a turn to promotional pricing, or a new format | Abandoning everyday low prices, or a new deal announced while integration is still incomplete |

**Shared root causes.** Seven of the eleven gates came out CONDITIONAL, which is more than half. They trace back to two root problems:

- **Growth by acquisition, financed with debt, has outrun proof that the model works in the acquired stores.**
- **The company can't see its own condition.** It has no store-level data on the acquired stores, no comparable-sales or shrinkage data in view, and no succession plan.

Fixing those two problems would clear most of the register.
