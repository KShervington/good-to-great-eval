# Good to Great: Ironbridge Steel Corporation (as of spring, Year 0)

## One-screen verdict

**Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** *Ironbridge will be the flat-rolled steel supplier that demanding North American manufacturers can't do without, made in fewer and newer mills, and judged by cash profit per ton across the whole cycle rather than by tons or rank.*

**Weakest flywheel link:** *Concentrated capital → lower cost per ton.* Nobody has shown that Ironbridge's best mills can get near the landed cost of Japanese and European steel. Without that, the whole loop stalls.

**Top three stop-doing items:**
1. Stop treating price increases as the profit strategy.
2. Stop spreading modernization capital across every plant to defend size and rank.
3. Stop competing with minimills in bars and other simple products.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** The record profits are a one-time windfall from decontrolled prices and inventory building, and they are reversing now. U.S. shipments are expected to fall about 25%, while the wage settlement floor (at least 3% a year) keeps pushing unit labor cost up. The pricing lever Ironbridge has relied on is breaking just as costs are locked to rise.

**Missing numbers that would change this answer:**
- Cost per ton by mill, compared with landed import cost and minimill cost. This decides whether the Hedgehog Concept stands or the honest answer becomes "managed contraction."
- Three-year earnings and return on capital.
- Pension and retiree obligations, which could swamp any plan.
- Labor cost per ton and current headcount.
- Profitability of shipbuilding and of each product line.

Greatness potential: 10/100

*Why so low, even with the plan:* Ironbridge faces three things at once. Rivals with newer equipment have a structural cost advantage. A new low-cost entrant (minimills) is moving up from the bottom of the market. And its cost floor is contractually rising. On top of that, its culture measures itself by size and status. The plan gives it a real path, but the odds of becoming and *staying* great from this starting point are poor.

*Evidence basis:* No search, memory or past-run tools were available in this session, so there was no prior scorecard and no outside market research. Everything below rests on the dossier alone. Inferences are marked `[ASSUMPTION]`.

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

The great Ironbridge is smaller, and it is proud of being smaller. Its capital and its best people are concentrated on one or two flat-rolled mills, the Lake Michigan plant and probably the tidewater plant `[ASSUMPTION, pending the cost benchmark]`. Those mills are run to be the lowest-cost, highest-quality source of sheet and plate for Midwest and East Coast auto, appliance and construction customers. Those customers are close enough that imports pay a freight and delivery penalty, and demanding enough that quality and service matter. Every mill, product line and upstream asset (mines, ore ships, railroads) is judged by one question: does it raise cash profit per ton across the cycle? Anything that fails the test is sold, shut or bought from the market instead. Executive and worker pay rise and fall with profit per ton, not with a guaranteed floor. Leadership talks first about cost, quality and customers, not rank, and it is the first to take a pay cut in a downturn. The company stops asking Washington to rescue it and starts out-producing the mills it currently fears.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence:** *Be the flat-rolled steel supplier that demanding North American manufacturers can't do without, from fewer and newer mills, judged by cash profit per ton across the cycle.*

| Circle | Candidates considered | Narrowed to | Ruled out (cannot be best in the world at) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Best in the world at** | Lowest-cost commodity steel; bars and simple products; shipbuilding; structural shapes and plate; integrated "we do everything"; flat-rolled sheet for nearby demanding customers | **Quality flat-rolled sheet (and possibly heavy plate) delivered fast to nearby North American manufacturers** | Bars (minimills are already cheaper); global lowest-cost commodity steel (newer foreign plants); shipbuilding `[ASSUMPTION: no evidence of advantage]`; being the biggest |
| **Economic engine** | Profit per ton; profit per employee; return per dollar of capital; tons per year | **Cash profit per ton shipped, measured trough-to-trough across the cycle** | Tons shipped and market rank (these drove the volume-chasing expansions) |
| **Passion** | Industrial heritage and size; making things that last (ships, bridges); serving the U.S. manufacturing base | **Making steel that American builders and manufacturers trust** `[ASSUMPTION: no direct evidence of workforce passion in dossier]` | Pride in size and status, which the headquarters tower shows is a live passion and a dangerous one |

**Why this intersection:**
- **Explains past wins:** The company chose to build its newest plant on Lake Michigan and to spend $500M to win Midwest sales. That shows the market already rewards nearby flat-rolled supply.
- **Rules things out:** Bars, shipbuilding, the "modernize everything" path, and integration for its own sake.
- **Uncomfortable to commit to:** Yes. It means accepting that Ironbridge will not be "one of the two largest."

**Confirmation tests (working hypothesis):**
1. A mill-by-mill benchmark shows at least one flat-rolled mill can reach landed import cost within a funded modernization plan.
2. Flat-rolled margin per ton beats bar, structural and ship margins over the last cycle.
3. Key auto and appliance customers say they would pay for, or sign long-term contracts for, quality and delivery reliability.

If test 1 fails, the honest conclusion is that Ironbridge can only be good, and the great version becomes a disciplined contraction that protects the core.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop raising prices as the main profit lever.** Profit must come from cost per ton.
2. **Stop spreading modernization capital across every plant.** No dollars go to a mill without a benchmarked path to competitive cost.
3. **Stop competing in bars and simple products** against minimills.
4. **Stop measuring success by tonnage and rank** ("one of the two largest") in reports, speeches and bonuses.
5. **Stop shipbuilding as a core business**: sell it or run it for cash `[ASSUMPTION pending profitability]`.
6. **Stop owning upstream assets for integration's sake.** Mines, ships and railroads stay only if they beat market cost.
7. **Stop agreeing to wage floors that don't depend on productivity**, starting at the next settlement and through good-faith partnership rather than confrontation.
8. **Stop treating import protection as the strategy.** It can be a tactic that buys time, never the plan.
9. **Stop status spending.** Freeze executive perks and visibly cut executive bonuses before asking anyone else for sacrifice.
10. **Stop "expansion to win sales."** Add capacity only where it raises cash profit per ton.

## 7. Core ideology and BHAG

**Core values:**
1. Steel customers can stake their products on.
2. Safety of every worker.
3. Candor: facts first, rank second.
4. Thrift: every dollar earns its place, starting at the top.
5. Shared fortune: pay rises and falls together with profit per ton.

**Core purpose:** To make the steel that North American builders and manufacturers trust.

**BHAG (20-year horizon):** *By Year 20, Ironbridge earns the highest through-cycle cash profit per ton of any steelmaker in North America, including imports and minimills, and holds that position through two full downturns.*

**Clock-building mechanisms:**
- Profit-per-ton reporting by mill and product, published internally every quarter.
- A written capital-allocation rule: no project unless it raises through-cycle profit per ton at a hedgehog mill.
- Executive and worker pay linked to profit per ton.
- Independent directors and a formal successor bench, with at least two internal candidates and outside comparison.
- A standing red-flag benchmark against imports and minimills.
- Blameless post-mortems on every major capital project.
