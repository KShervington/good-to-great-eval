# Good to Great: Ironbridge Steel Corporation

## One-screen verdict

**Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** *Ironbridge becomes the lowest-cost, most reliable supplier of flat-rolled steel to manufacturers in the U.S. Midwest and Mid-Atlantic, made in its two best plants and judged on through-cycle cash profit per ton of capacity.*

**Weakest link in the flywheel:** *lower cost per ton*. Wages rise by contract at a floor of at least 3% a year, the plants are old and the capital is spread across all of them, and nobody can show where Ironbridge's cost stands against Japanese mills or minimills.

**Top three stop-doing items:**
1. Stop using price increases as the way to make a profit.
2. Stop spreading capital across every plant, and stop adding capacity at a cyclical peak.
3. Stop defending bars and simple products against minimills, and stop building new ships.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** Last year's record profit came from price controls ending and customers stockpiling, not from competitiveness. Demand is now falling fast, with shipments forecast down about 25% and mills at 78–80% of capacity. The cash window to restructure is open now and will close within months.

**Missing numbers that would change this answer:**
- cost per ton by plant against foreign mills and minimills (the most decisive)
- pension and retiree obligations
- return on capital for the last three years
- current headcount and labor cost per ton

If the best plants are already near world cost, the odds go up. If the pension load is very large relative to equity, the room to restructure shrinks sharply.

Greatness potential: 7/100

*Evidence note: this run had no memory, past chats or web search, so no prior baseline and no market research were available. Everything rests on the dossier. Inferences are marked `[ASSUMPTION]`.*

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

The great Ironbridge is a smaller company. It concentrates on flat-rolled sheet and coated products, made mainly at its Lake Michigan plant (next to Midwest auto and appliance customers) and its tidewater plant (cheap waterborne ore, East Coast customers) `[ASSUMPTION: these are its lowest-cost plants, to be confirmed by plant-level cost data]`. It has exited, sold or closed bar lines, new-build shipbuilding and the plants that cannot reach world-benchmark cost. It earns its price through delivery reliability, quality and the shortest lead times in its regions, not through list-price increases. Executives are paid on through-cycle return on capital and on cost per ton against the best mill in the world. Every capital dollar goes to the core plants, and the first one goes to cost reduction. The union is a partner in productivity, not just a counterparty in arbitration. The headquarters is lean. Capacity is sized for the trough, not the peak.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

| Circle | Candidates considered | Narrowed to |
|---|---|---|
| **Best in the world at** | (a) full-line integrated steel for American infrastructure; (b) flat-rolled sheet delivered to regional manufacturers; (c) shipbuilding; (d) structural shapes and plate | **(b)**, narrowed to *delivered* flat-rolled for the U.S. Midwest and Mid-Atlantic. Minimills cannot make sheet yet. Foreign mills carry ocean freight, longer lead times and political quota risk. The Lake Michigan plant was built for exactly this market. It **cannot** be best in the world at bars (minimills win on cost), at full-line breadth, or at shipbuilding. |
| **Economic engine denominator** | profit per ton shipped; profit per employee; profit per customer; **cash profit per ton of capacity, through the cycle** | **Through-cycle cash profit per ton of capacity.** It penalizes idle capacity, which is the integrated producer's chronic disease, and rewards earning profit in troughs, not just peaks. It also makes closing capacity visible as a gain. |
| **Deep passion** | heritage of building America's ships and bridges; pride in steelmaking craft; serving American manufacturing | **Making the steel that American industry builds on** `[ASSUMPTION: the dossier says nothing direct about employee passion]`. There is a tension here: heritage pride points toward ships, bridges and structurals, while the economics point to sheet. The great version has to move that pride over to its customers' factories. |

**Hedgehog sentence:** *Be the lowest-cost, most reliable source of flat-rolled steel for the manufacturers near our two best plants, and judge everything by cash profit per ton of capacity across the whole cycle.*

**Tests:**
- *Explains past wins?* Partly. The Lake Michigan expansion was explicitly about winning Midwest sales. Profitability by product line is unknown.
- *Rules things out?* Yes: bars, shipbuilding, plants that are not cost-competitive, and growth in tonnage for its own sake.
- *Uncomfortable?* Very. It means shrinking an institution that once employed 300,000 people, and closing or selling parts of the original works and older plants.

**What would confirm it within 12 months:**
1. Plant-level data shows the two core plants within reach of benchmark foreign cost delivered to U.S. customers.
2. Flat-rolled margins hold up better than bar and structural margins through this downturn.
3. Key auto and appliance customers will commit to multi-year supply for reliability and lead time.

**What would refute it:**
- The core plants sit structurally far above world cost even after modernization.
- Electric-arc producers show they can roll sheet. That is the existential threat to watch, not a likely near-term event.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   ┌─► 1. Concentrate capital & tonnage in the two best plants
   │                │
   │                ▼
   │   2. Lower cost per ton (continuous casting, process control,
   │      work-rule productivity, closing high-cost capacity)
   │                │
   │                ▼
   │   3. Reliable quality + short lead times for regional
   │      flat-rolled buyers (auto, appliance, containers)
   │                │
   │                ▼
   │   4. Multi-year supply commitments → high utilization even in troughs
   │                │
   │                ▼
   │   5. Higher through-cycle cash profit per ton of capacity  ◄─ denominator
   │                │
   └────────────────┘  (reinvest in the core plants first, never spread)
```

**Current push (next one or two turns):**
1. **Measure and attack cost per ton** (component 2). Build the benchmark by plant, then aim peak-year cash at the highest-return cost reductions in the core plants.
2. **Turn concentration into a decision, not a study** (component 1). Name the capacity, product lines and businesses to exit before the downturn forces fire-sale terms.

**Doom-loop check.** The signs are already present:
- Profit protected through price increases instead of cost reduction (reaction without understanding).
- Four presidents in 15 years (new direction after new direction).
- A $3B modernization program and a $500M expansion spread across a full network (big programs to jump-start momentum).
- No chosen answer to the modernize / protect / cut debate (inconsistency).
- An $18.5M headquarters built two years before a downturn.

Verdict: the loop closes on paper, but the company is running a doom loop today.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop raising prices as the profit strategy.** Price follows cost position, not the other way round.
2. **Stop spreading capital across every plant.** Core plants get capital first. No project goes ahead unless it lowers cost per ton toward the benchmark.
3. **Stop adding capacity at the cyclical peak.** Freeze expansion. Size capacity for the trough.
4. **Stop defending bars and simple products against minimills.** Exit or sell those lines.
5. **Stop new-build shipbuilding.** Sell the yards or wind them down.
6. **Stop treating import protection as the strategy.** Quotas and voluntary restraints buy time; they do not build a position. Keep at most a parity-level trade-policy effort.
7. **Stop status spending.** End headquarters decoration and executive perks, and stop paying bonuses on peak-year profit. Consider leasing out headquarters floors.
8. **Stop rotating presidents.** Commit to one leader and one strategy for at least a full cycle.
9. **Stop running marginal captive mines** where purchased ore is cheaper delivered `[ASSUMPTION: needs the cost comparison]`.

## 7. Core ideology and BHAG

**Core values** (to be tested with employees, not decreed):
1. Safe plants, every shift.
2. Tell the truth about cost.
3. The customer's line never stops for our steel.
4. Earn the price; don't raise it.

**Core purpose:** Make the steel American industry builds on.

**BHAG (20-year horizon):** By Year 20, be the lowest-cost, highest-quality flat-rolled producer in North America, measured against the best mills in the world, not the best in the U.S.

**Clock-building mechanisms:**
- A published cost benchmark, by plant, every quarter.
- A capital-allocation rule: core plants first, cost-per-ton hurdle on every project.
- Executive pay tied to through-cycle return on capital, with clawback on peak-year bonuses.
- Joint union–management productivity committees at each core plant.
- A succession plan with at least two internal candidates and one outside benchmark search.
