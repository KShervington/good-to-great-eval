# Good to Great: Meridian Surgical Corporation

## Verdict

**Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** *Meridian turns surgeons into stapling surgeons, procedure by procedure, and earns its living on every cartridge they keep using.*

**Weakest flywheel link:** a surgeon who has been *trained* does not necessarily become a surgeon who *keeps using* the product. The company lost about $1M eight years ago because instruments were sold and then not used. The current sales model pays commission only and lets weak reps go quickly, so it still rewards placing instruments more than keeping surgeons using them.

**Top three stop-doing items:**
1. Stop paying sales commission on instrument placement. Pay on sustained cartridge pull-through.
2. Stop adding unrelated instruments just to spread marketing cost.
3. Stop judging reps on windows shorter than the time it takes a surgeon to adopt the product.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** the company has no visible plan for the day a large suture maker enters stapling. That maker would already own the surgeon relationship, the hospital contract and the incumbent technique. We don't know how long the patents last, and that is the single biggest unknown.

**Missing numbers that would change this answer:**
- Patent expiry dates and any large competitor's intent to enter. These could move the score by ±20 points.
- Recent sales and margins, split between instruments and cartridges.
- The share of trained surgeons still stapling at 6 and 12 months.
- Cash position.

Greatness potential: 35/100

*Why 35:* the base is genuinely good: a near-monopoly, a razor-and-blades model and a proven training engine. What holds the score down is concentration on one technology, a founder-centred and high-pressure management system with no visible succession, and an unknown patent horizon facing deep-pocketed incumbents. "Stay great" is the hard part of this definition.

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Meridian is the company that made mechanical tissue closure the standard of care, and it still owns that standard after its patents expire and large competitors arrive. Its moat is not the stapler design. The moat is a clinical-education machine that no competitor can quickly copy: medically fluent reps, residency and OR-staff curricula, and surgeon-usage data. That machine converts surgeons one procedure at a time and keeps them using the product. Revenue comes from cartridges, so every incentive in the company points at surgeons *using* staples, not at instruments sitting in hospital closets. It runs on financial and clinical leaders who can tell the founder no. It would keep compounding if the founder left tomorrow.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop paying commission on instrument placement.** Pay on cartridge reorders sustained over 6–12 months.
2. **Stop developing instruments outside tissue closure and stapling** when their only job is to share marketing cost.
3. **Stop letting reps go on windows shorter than the adoption cycle.** Fire for values or effort, not for a slow first quarter at a new hospital.
4. **Stop selling instruments into hospitals without a committed training plan** for surgeons and OR staff.
5. **Stop routing education-dependent procedures through distributors** who cannot teach.
6. **Stop any quarter-end loading of the distributor channel** beyond real cartridge demand `[ASSUMPTION: whether this happens is unknown; the pressure culture makes it a risk]`.
7. **Stop running product and clinical decisions through the founder alone.**

**Framework and freedom:** the framework is "every action raises profit per active stapling surgeon." Inside it, reps choose how to train surgeons at their hospitals, and engineers choose which closure procedures to target next.

**Bureaucracy check:** fast firing and tight sales pressure may be compensating for hiring the wrong people, which happened with the medics. Better selection would let the company loosen those controls.

**Fanatical consistency:** every new instrument ships with a training curriculum, and no surgeon is counted as won until they are active at 90 days.

## 7. Core ideology and BHAG

**Core values:**
1. The patient outcome comes before the sale.
2. Teach before you sell.
3. Tell the truth about the numbers and the clinical results.
4. Inventive craftsmanship.

**Core purpose:** to make surgery faster, safer and less bloody by changing how tissue is closed.

**BHAG (20-year horizon):** stapling becomes the default closure technique taught to every surgical resident in the developed world, and most of those residents learn it on a Meridian curriculum.

**Clock-building mechanisms:**
- A dashboard of surgeon usage.
- A residency curriculum program.
- An independent CFO and audit committee.
- A head of clinical affairs.
- A named successor track.
- Rep pay tied to retention.
