# Good to Great: Meridian Surgical Corporation

## One-screen verdict

**Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** *Meridian is the best in the world at turning surgeons into routine stapling users, and it earns its money every time one of those surgeons chooses to staple.*

**Weakest flywheel link:** the step from *trained surgeon* to *surgeon who staples routinely*. The company has already lost about $1M once because instruments were bought and not used. Today nobody measures this link, and reps are paid commission-only on sales rather than on usage `[ASSUMPTION about comp basis]`.

**Top three stop-doing items:**
1. Stop paying reps on instrument sales. Pay on cartridge pull-through from active surgeons.
2. Stop placing instruments with surgeons and OR teams that haven't been trained.
3. Stop adding non-stapling instruments just to spread marketing costs.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** Meridian has a near-monopoly in a fast-growing, proven, disposables-driven market. That is exactly what draws in a large suture maker that already owns the OR relationships. The company doesn't know (or the dossier doesn't show) how long its patents protect it, or who is coming.

**Missing numbers that would change this answer:**
- Patent coverage and expiry dates.
- Recent sales, margins and cash.
- Cartridge revenue per active surgeon, and the share of placed instruments still in use.
- Any large health-care company's plans in stapling.
- Quality and recall record.

If patents are short and a big entrant is close, the plan stays the same but becomes much more urgent. If margins are thin or cash is weak, Gate 1 could fall to FAIL.

Greatness potential: 30/100

*Method note: this run had no search, memory or past-chat access. No prior baseline was found, and no market, patent or competitor research was possible. Everything rests on the dossier. Inferences are marked `[ASSUMPTION]`.*

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Meridian is not "a stapler company." It is the institution that changed how American surgeons close and join tissue. It trains surgeons and OR teams better than anyone, and it knows each surgeon's usage the way a good physician knows a patient's chart. It earns on every stapled procedure, not on every instrument sold. Its reps are medically fluent, stay for years, and are paid when surgeons actually staple. Teaching hospitals train residents on its instruments, so new surgeons arrive already fluent in stapling. When a large competitor with a cheaper cartridge and a bigger catalog arrives, surgeons stay, because switching means retraining and Meridian is in the OR whenever they need help. The company runs on systems, not just on its founder: a training academy, a usage-data system, a quality system and a successor.

## 3. The flywheel

```
 ┌─► 1. Medically trained reps train surgeons + OR teams (animal lab, OR support)
 │        │
 │        ▼
 │   2. Surgeons staple routinely (ACTIVE SURGEONS)   ◄── weakest link
 │        │
 │        ▼
 │   3. Peers and residents see it in teaching hospitals → referrals into training
 │        │
 │        ▼
 │   4. Cartridge volume → GROSS PROFIT PER ACTIVE SURGEON
 │        │
 │        ▼
 │   5. Funds more trained reps + instrument refinements for more procedures
 └────────┘
```

**Current push (next one or two turns):**
1. Measure link 2: build an active-surgeon census and a cohort usage report.
2. Pay for link 2: tie rep pay to cartridge pull-through from active surgeons, with a ramp so the $8,000 training investment can pay back.

**Doom-loop check:**
- *Three adoption models in about five years (nurses → army medics → trained salespeople).* These look like direction changes, but each was a response to evidence, and the current model has held for about five years. This was learning, not thrashing. **OK.**
- *Developing other instruments to spread marketing costs.* This is an early sign of "more" being chased for its own sake. **Watch.**
- *Commission-only pay plus fast firing.* This drives churn, which throws away $8,000 per departed rep and the surgeon relationships that rep built. Inconsistency at the most important link. **Risk.**

Verdict: the loop closes. **PASS.**

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop paying commission on instrument sales.** Pay on active-surgeon cartridge pull-through.
2. **Stop placing instruments before training.** No first case until the surgeon and OR team are certified.
3. **Stop developing non-stapling instruments just to spread marketing costs.** New products must ride the same training call and the same surgeon.
4. **Stop expanding independent-distributor coverage** in accounts that need clinical conversion.
5. **Stop firing reps on short-window quotas** before their $8,000 training can pay back. Judge them on surgeon activation over 12 months or more.
6. **Stop measuring the business by total sales.** Lead with active surgeons and gross profit per active surgeon.
7. **Stop making product decisions with only the founder in the room.** Every design gets a surgeon review.

Skin staples are under test. They use the same technology, but the user and buying call may differ `[ASSUMPTION]`. Keep them only if they share the training and sales motion.

**Non-negotiable disciplines:**
- Every rep is certified (240 hours plus animal lab).
- Every account is trained before use.
- Every cartridge lot can be traced.
- Active-surgeon cohorts are reviewed every quarter.

**Framework:** reps have full freedom in *how* they convert surgeons. They have none on training-first or quality.

## 7. Core ideology and BHAG

- **Core purpose:** make surgery faster, safer and less bloody.
- **Core values:**
  1. The surgeon's skill and the patient's outcome come first.
  2. Teach before you sell.
  3. Every cartridge works, every time.
  4. Face the facts out loud.
- **BHAG (about 20 years):** stapling becomes the standard way American surgeons close and join tissue in the procedures where it's indicated, taught in every residency program on Meridian instruments.
- **Clock-building mechanisms:**
  - A Meridian training academy with written curricula.
  - A surgeon-usage data system.
  - A quality system.
  - A clinical advisory board.
  - A named successor and a board-run succession plan.
