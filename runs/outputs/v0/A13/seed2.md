# Good to Great: Kestrel Industries

## One-screen verdict

**Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** *Kestrel makes simple steel products at the lowest cost per ton in the industry, because every person in its small, lean, rural plants is paid for the tons their team produces.*

**Weakest link in the flywheel:** the link from output-based pay to keeping the best people. In a recession, short weeks cut take-home pay sharply. If the best crews leave, the productivity edge leaves with them, and that edge is the only part of the model competitors can't buy from an equipment supplier.

**Top 3 stop-doing items:**
1. No stock-funded acquisitions or diversification outside steel and joists. The company has already lived through that doom loop once.
2. No new mill until the Texas start-up runs at standard. Don't stack start-ups during a recession.
3. Add no management layers or HQ staff. Four layers and two senior officers is the ceiling, not a temporary state.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** the Texas mill adds capacity this year just as recession cuts construction and joist demand, and as imports are offered below U.S. prices. Utilization will fall, and the incentive system will cut pay at the same moment.

**Missing numbers that would change the answer:**
- Cost per ton against integrated mills and other minimills. This would confirm the Hedgehog Concept or break it.
- Sales, earnings and return on capital for the last two years.
- Profit per ton through the current downturn.
- Employee count and turnover.
- Current joist and bar market share.

Greatness potential: 45/100

*Why 45:* The culture, cost position and self-financing logic are unusually strong for a company this size. A loop that already closed once lifts the odds well above the low base rate for small firms. Three things hold the number down. The technology is copyable. The cost advantage is not yet measured. The recession is about to test the weakest link.

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Kestrel is the lowest-cost steel producer in America and stays that way through every cycle. Its advantage comes from how its people work, not from the equipment they use. It runs a network of small, nonunion, rural plants, each with a general manager who has real authority. Pay rises and falls with team output, and pain is shared from the top down: officers take the deepest cuts and nobody is laid off. Profits per ton fund each new mill without diluting shareholders. Each new mill starts up faster than the last because of a repeatable start-up team. It wins any product where cost decides the sale. It moves up into products now reserved for integrated mills only when it can make them at lower cost than they can, not because those markets are big. If Kestrel vanished, buyers of bars, light shapes and joists would lose the price floor that disciplines the whole industry.

**Does it rule things out?** Yes. It rules out flat-rolled, specialty grades, diversification and adding management layers.

**Confirmation tests:**

2. Profit per ton stays positive through the current recession.
3. Each new mill reaches its standard output faster than the last.

## 3. The flywheel

```
 │            │

 │            │
 │            ▼

 │            │
 │            ▼
 │   4. Win price-driven bar, light-shape and joist business
 │      from integrated mills and imports; joists keep mills loaded
 │            │
 │            ▼
 │   5. High utilization ──► PROFIT PER TON (denominator)
 │            │
 │            ▼
 │   6. Cash funds (a) big bonuses that attract the best rural workers
 │      and (b) the next mill, built and started by a proven team
 │            │
 └────────────┘  more plants and more teams feed the same culture
```

**Current push (next 1–2 turns):**
- **Turn A: get the Texas mill to standard output without a repeat of the first caster fiasco.** This feeds links 3 and 5.
- **Turn B: protect link 2 through the recession.** Keep the standards honest, cut from the top first, and keep the crews. Hold the next expansion until the current push is complete.

**Doom-loop check:**
- *History:* the company's predecessor ran the classic doom loop. It bought businesses with stock, chased new directions and collapsed into default. The cultural memory of that is an asset.
- *Current warning signs:*
  - Three mills in about six years, with the third starting into a recession.
  - The open debate about "new downstream products" could become new-direction-of-the-year.
  - Industry talk of import protection could tempt a grasp for salvation.
- *Verdict:* no active doom loop, but two live temptations.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop all stock-funded acquisitions and any diversification outside steel and joists.** That is the predecessor's failure mode.
2. **Stop treating flat-rolled sheet and specialty grades as a growth option.** Revisit only if a route emerges that makes them at a lower cost than integrated mills.
3. **Stop overlapping mill start-ups.** No new mill commitment until Texas runs at standard and the recession's depth is known.
4. **Stop adding layers, titles or HQ staff.** Keep the 2,000-square-foot office as a symbol and a constraint.
5. **Stop building any plan that depends on import protection.** A lowest-cost producer should win without it. Kestrel can still state a position, but its strategy must not need one.
6. **Stop pursuing downstream products that don't use Kestrel steel at a cost advantage.** Joists pass this test. Anything else must prove it.
7. **Stop locating plants where the culture can't take root,** meaning urban or legacy-labor sites `[ASSUMPTION: rationale for the rural siting]`.

**Non-negotiable disciplines (fanatical consistency):**
- Daily tons-against-standard, visible to every team.
- No layoffs. Pain is shared through hours, and officers' pay is cut first and deepest `[ASSUMPTION: this ordering is a recommendation, not a known current policy]`.
- Same hard hat, same benefits.
- Every capital project must pay back on profit per ton.

**Framework and freedom:** HQ sets the four things listed below. Plant general managers own everything else: hiring, crew organization, local customers and maintenance.
- Product scope.
- Capital approval.
- The pay formula.
- Safety rules.

**Bureaucracy check:** no obvious compensating rules today. That makes this a guard, not a fix.

## 7. Core ideology and the BHAG

**Core values:**
1. Pay for performance: you share in what you produce.
2. Egalitarianism: no class distinctions between management and the floor.
3. Shared sacrifice: no layoffs, and the top takes the cuts first.
4. Every worker goes home safe. This is added after the fire, and output never outranks it.
5. Frugality: every dollar of overhead must earn its place.

**Core purpose:** *To prove that ordinary people in small towns, treated fairly and paid for what they produce, can out-make anyone in steel.*

**BHAG (20–25 years):** *Become the most profitable steel company in America on profit per ton, and compete in markets now reserved for the integrated giants, without ever giving up being the low-cost producer.*

**Mechanisms (clock building, not time telling):**
- The incentive formula is written down and protected from ad-hoc changes.
- The no-layoff policy is formally adopted by the board.
- Plant general manager autonomy is codified.
- A mill start-up playbook and dedicated team exist.
- A capital rule: payback measured on profit per ton.
- A successor pipeline drawn from plant general managers.
- A cap on the number of management layers.
