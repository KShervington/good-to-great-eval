# Good to Great: Kestrel Industries

## One-screen verdict

**Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** *Kestrel turns scrap into simple steel, and steel into joists, at the lowest cost per ton in America, because its people are paid as owners of output and the company spends almost nothing on hierarchy.* The denominator is **profit per ton of finished steel**.

**Weakest flywheel link:** *Volume → pay.* Low cost, high bonuses and the no-layoff promise all assume full mills. This year the recession cuts construction demand and the Texas mill starts up into it. The first mill's caster took more than a year to stabilize. If Texas repeats that during a downturn, the flywheel turns backwards.

**Top three stop-doing items:**
1. No new mill until the Texas mill runs at rated output.
2. No diversification beyond scrap-based steel and the products built from it. That includes "downstream" products that don't consume Kestrel's own steel, and anything bought with stock.
3. No attempt at flat-rolled or high-grade steel until a proven low-cost, scrap-based route exists.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** The minimill equipment is not proprietary, and competitors are copying it. Kestrel's advantage is therefore its operating culture and nothing else. That culture is now under two stresses at once: short-week pay cuts and a fatal fire that killed four employees.

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Kestrel is the lowest-cost, highest-return steel company in the country, though not the biggest. It is a network of rural, scrap-fed minimills, each paired with joist plants that take a steady share of its output. Each plant runs as its own profit center under a tiny headquarters. The pay system makes workers, in effect, partners in the tonnage they produce. In a downturn everyone loses hours and nobody loses their job, so the workforce stays loyal through the cycle. Profit per ton stays positive through recessions in which integrated rivals lose money. That cash, rather than stock or heavy debt, funds the next mill in the next region. Kestrel refuses work it cannot do at lowest cost, refuses to lean on import protection, and expands its product range only when its own technology makes a new product cheap. The company would remain great after the current CEO leaves, because the system produces plant managers who think like him.

**Weakest link:** the step from [4] to [1]. Demand volume feeds pay. In a recession, tonnage falls, short weeks cut take-home pay, and the people who power link [2] start looking around.

**Current push, for the next one or two turns:**
- **Stabilize the Texas mill first.** Put the best operators and engineers on it. This is the biggest *opportunity*, so it gets the best people.
- **Hold profit per ton through the downturn.** Track cost per ton plant by plant every month. Don't chase volume below variable cost.
- **Defend the promise in the open.** Explain plainly why everyone is on short weeks and nobody is laid off, and show how the profit-sharing pool comes back when tonnage does.

**Doom-loop check:** **PASS, with a history flag.** The company's past *was* a doom loop: stock-funded acquisitions into nuclear, chemicals and electronics, followed by default. The present is different. Three mills in six years follow one consistent pattern rather than a series of new directions. The live risk is the "downstream products" debate, which could become a fresh jump-start program if the recession hurts.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop** starting new mills until Texas runs at rated output.
2. **Stop** any acquisition paid for in stock, and any business outside scrap-based steel and products made from Kestrel's own steel.
3. **Stop** treating downstream products as a growth fix. Each must pass the profit-per-ton and own-steel tests.
4. **Stop** reaching for flat-rolled, specialty or high-grade steel before a proven low-cost route exists. Watch the technology, but don't spend ahead of it.
5. **Stop** relying on import protection as part of the plan. If Kestrel takes a public position, the plan must still work without protection.
6. **Stop** buying volume at prices below variable cost to keep hours up.
7. **Stop** any practice that lets output targets override safety. Safety stops should never cost a team its bonus `[ASSUMPTION: current standards do not exclude safety stops]`.
8. **Stop** adding headquarters staff or management layers. Four layers is the ceiling.

**Non-negotiable disciplines:**
- Output-based team pay.
- No layoffs, with shared short weeks instead.
- One status for everyone.
- Rural sites.
- Monthly cost per ton and profit per ton for each plant.
- Safety above output.

**Framework boundaries:** headquarters sets capital allocation, the pay formulas, safety rules and the Hedgehog Concept. Plants decide everything else.

**Bureaucracy check:** nothing to flag. The company has few rules and few layers. Keep it that way.
