# Kestrel Stores, Inc.: Good to Great run

## One-screen verdict

**Kestrel is good but no longer great, and it shows early signs of decline.** Over 14 years it compounded from $1B to $12.6B. A rival now beats it on every scoreboard that matters, using the selling model Kestrel rejected. Leadership's public answer has been "we're in great shape."

- **Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** *Kestrel is the place Americans go for expert help choosing and setting up complex home technology. It delivers that help through the industry's best-run superstore system and measures itself by profit per customer transaction.*
- **Weakest flywheel link:** "Expert advice → customers pay more for it." Rival B's success with self-service is direct evidence against this link. If category data doesn't support it, the Hedgehog Concept fails.
- **Top 3 stop-doing:**
  1. Cancel the six stand-alone appliance stores and plan a staged exit from appliances.
  2. Fully separate the used-car business from electronics management and capital.
  3. Drop the 800-store goal. Fund remodels of existing stores before opening new ones.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** Rival B leads in sales, earnings, profit per store and national share. It got there by abandoning commissioned selling, which Kestrel still uses.
- **Missing numbers that would change the answer:**
  - Electronics profit per store and ROE trend, set against Rival B.
  - Gross margin and share by category, split into complex and commodity products.
  - Store age and remodel cost.
  - Customer ratings of counselor help.

Greatness potential: 25/100

*Evidence basis: autonomous run. There was no memory or past-chat search and no web research, because the tools weren't available. Every conclusion rests on the dossier alone, and inferences are marked [ASSUMPTION].*

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Kestrel is narrower than today's Kestrel. It stops trying to be the biggest electronics superstore and becomes the one customers trust when a purchase is complicated: home theater, computers, home office, and connected systems. Its best sales counselors work only where advice changes the outcome. Commodity aisles (music, movies, games, accessories) run self-service at low cost. Revenue per customer comes from complete solutions, meaning hardware, setup, installation and support. Its distribution and store-operations systems, already well regarded, keep those solutions in stock and delivered. It no longer owns a used-car company, a disc format or an appliance fight with home-improvement giants. It opens a new store only when the store clears a profit hurdle, and it measures success by profit per transaction and per store, not by store count or revenue targets.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence:** *Kestrel is the place Americans go for expert help choosing and setting up complex home technology. It delivers that help through the industry's best-run superstore system and measures itself by profit per customer transaction.*

| Circle | Candidates considered | Kept | Rejected, and why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Best in world at** | (a) Expert, advised selling of complex electronics. (b) Distribution and store operations. (c) Running big-box retail in any category, cars included. (d) Lowest-price national electronics store. | (a) + (b) | (c) The used-car venture lost money and halted expansion, so there's no evidence it's best-in-world. Pursuing it also turns Kestrel into a conglomerate. (d) Kestrel can't out-cost Rival B or home-improvement chains, and price fights are already underway. |
| **Economic engine** | Profit per store; per square foot; per customer transaction; per sales-counselor hour | **Profit per customer transaction** in complex categories. It must also show up in **profit per store**. | Profit per store alone is a contest Rival B is already winning on its own terms. The advice model can only win where each transaction carries more value. |
| **Passion** | Pride in operations; counselor sales culture; growth and expansion | Operational excellence and counselor craft [ASSUMPTION: the dossier has no evidence about culture or passion] | Expansion as an end in itself. That passion produced the ventures and the 800-store target. |

**Intersection tests**
- *Explains past wins?* Partly. The chain grew on a combination of counselors, selection and strong operations, which matches this concept.
- *Rules things out?* Yes: appliances, used cars, proprietary formats, commodity-category commissions, and store count for its own sake.
- *Uncomfortable?* Very. It gives up about 14% of sales in appliances, splits off a business the CEO built, and rewrites counselor pay.

**Confirmation tests.** It stays a hypothesis until these come back:
1. Category data shows Kestrel's gross margin per transaction in complex categories beats Rival B's, or at least holds while its share holds.
2. In remodel test stores, a counselor-plus-self-service mix raises profit per store compared with control stores.
3. Customer research shows counselor advice is a reason people choose Kestrel.

**If tests 1–3 fail,** the honest conclusion is that the advice model is the wrong bet. Kestrel should then either adopt the winning model, accepting it will be a strong #2 at best, or manage itself as a good, cash-generating #2. It should not pretend to greatness.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   ┌──► 1. Best counselors focused on complex categories
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │    2. Customers trust advice → buy complete solutions
   │       (hardware + setup + install + support)
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │    3. Higher PROFIT PER CUSTOMER TRANSACTION
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │    4. Reinvest: counselor pay/training, remodeled stores,
   │       in-stock systems; self-service lowers commodity cost
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │    5. Better experience → repeat visits and referrals
   │       for the next complex purchase
   └────────────┘
```

- **Weakest link:** step 1 → step 2, which assumes customers will pay for advice. See Gate 5.
- **Current push (next two turns):**
  1. Put the remodel test to work proving step 2. Design the 30–35 remodeled stores as a controlled experiment: counselors on complex categories, self-service on commodity aisles, and pay tied to solution profit instead of unit volume.
  2. Free capital for step 4 by halting appliance stores and the used-car drag.
- **Doom-loop check:** **signs present.**
  - Two large ventures were launched to create growth, at about $233M for the disc format plus used-car losses.
  - The $15B / 800-store target was missed.
  - A new appliance format is being tested while the core is losing share.
  - Management keeps reacting to concerns by quoting core earnings growth.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop building appliance stores.** Cancel the six stand-alone stores. Run a staged exit from the appliance category, which is about 14% of sales and in a price war with home-improvement chains, unless category margins show Kestrel is winning.
2. **Stop running the used-car business from electronics.** Complete a separation (spin-off or sale of the retained stake) so it no longer draws electronics management attention or capital.
3. **Stop chasing store count.** Retire the 800-store goal. Every new store, including this year's planned 25, must clear a profit-per-store hurdle. Remodels of the 571 existing stores come first.
4. **Stop funding new formats and ventures from the core.** That means no more disc-format-style bets. Any new venture needs a board-level hurdle and must be funded separately.
5. **Stop paying volume commission on commodity products.** Music, movies, games and simple accessories go to self-service. Commission pays for solution profit in complex categories.
6. **Stop issuing public 20–25% earnings-growth forecasts.** Stop managing to the tracking-stock price, which went from $14 to $54 in one year.
7. **Stop treating the electronics business as the funding source for diversification.**

## 7. Core ideology and the BHAG

- **Core values [ASSUMPTION, to be validated with employees]:**
  1. Give advice you'd give a friend.
  2. In stock, on time, every time.
  3. Own the outcome, not the transaction.
  4. Respect the customer's money.
- **Core purpose:** *Help people get technology working in their lives.*
- **BHAG (20-year horizon):** *Become the first place most American households turn to for any home-technology decision they can't make alone.*
- **Clock-building mechanisms:**
  - A profit-per-store hurdle for every new store.
  - A capital-allocation rule: no venture is funded from the core without independent board approval.
  - A monthly red-flag dashboard measuring Kestrel against Rival B on profit per store, share and complex-category margin.
  - Counselor certification tied to the complex categories.
  - An independent lead director.
