# Kestrel Stores: Good to Great

## One-screen verdict

**Kestrel is good. It is not great, and it is drifting.** It built a 571-store electronics chain with well-regarded operations, and comparable-store sales grew 8% last year. But Rival B now beats it on sales, earnings, profit per store and share. For years management attention has gone into ventures outside the core.

- **Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** *Kestrel helps households choose, buy and set up complex home electronics, through expert people and the best-run store and distribution system in the category, and it judges every decision by profit per store.*
- **Weakest flywheel link:** expert help → higher profit per store. It is unproven. The rival that dropped commissioned selling earns *more* per store.
- **Top 3 stop-doing items:**
  1. Stop running the used-car business inside the electronics agenda. Separate it fully.
  2. Stop setting goals in revenue and store counts ($15B, 800 stores). Manage to profit per store.
  3. Stop the six stand-alone appliance stores and the appliance price fight with home-improvement chains.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** Kestrel's defining formula no longer wins. Rival B changed its selling model about ten years ago, redesigned its format twice, and now leads on every comparable measure. Meanwhile Kestrel put about $233M into a disc format that failed. When investors raised the distraction, the CEO called the company "in great shape."
- **Missing numbers that would change this answer:**
  - Electronics profit per store against Rival B
  - Electronics ROE/ROIC and gross-margin trend
  - Store age and remodel cost
  - Share and customer satisfaction by category
  - Current used-car profitability

Greatness potential: 20/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Kestrel is a company that does one thing. It is the place households go when a technology purchase is complex enough that they want help getting it right. Expert counselors match people to the right products and set them up. The stores are run and supplied better than anyone's. Kestrel earns more profit per store than any rival, including Rival B. It no longer measures success by store count or headline revenue. It has no side ventures. The used-car business has become an independent company pursuing its own greatness. Any appliance business that survives is there only because it raises profit per store. The selling model stays because controlled tests proved it pays, not because it is tradition. Leadership after the transition runs on facts and red flags, not on past records.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence (working hypothesis):** *Kestrel helps households choose, buy and set up complex home electronics, through expert people and the best-run store and distribution system in the category, and it judges every decision by profit per store.*

| Circle | Candidates | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **Best in the world at** | (a) Store operations and distribution systems | **Keep.** The only "best" the dossier states as fact ("well regarded in the industry"). |
| | (b) Expert-assisted selling of complex electronics | **Keep, as hypothesis.** It is the differentiator against self-service Rival B, but unproven on profit. |

| **Economic engine** | Profit per store, per region, per customer, per counselor, per square foot | **Profit per store** (see below). |
| **Passion** | Counselors' product knowledge; operators' pride in systems `[ASSUMPTION]`; "growth" | Knowledge and operations are real passions. Passion for growth itself is a warning sign, not a circle. |

**Denominator: profit per store.**
- It is the measure on which Rival B demonstrably leads, so the gap becomes visible.
- It shifts capital from opening new stores to fixing existing ones. That matters because the chain is near its own stated ceiling: 571 of 800, with growth slowing from 34 openings to 25.
- It forces honest category calls, such as appliances and floor space.
- *Alternative to test:* profit per region. If distribution density is the real advantage, the regional measure will explain store-level variance better.

**Confirmation tests:**
1. **Explains past wins?** Partly. The superstore era rested on operations plus expert selling, at a time when the products were new and complex `[ASSUMPTION]`.
2. **Rules things out?** Yes: used cars, new formats, appliance price wars, and store count as a goal.
3. **Uncomfortable?** Yes. It means slowing openings, separating a ~$2B business, possibly exiting a ~$1.5B category, and putting the commission model to a test.

**Evidence that would confirm it within 12 months:**
- Remodeled electronics and home-office stores beat matched control stores on profit per store.
- Expert-selling stores beat hybrid or self-service pilots on profit per store, not only on sales.
- Customer research shows that help is a top reason people choose Kestrel.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   ┌─► 1. Expert, well-trained counselors (paid on productivity)
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │   2. Customers get the right product + setup + add-ons; trust rises
   │            │
   │            ▼   ◄── WEAKEST LINK (unproven vs. Rival B)
   │   3. Higher conversion, ticket and repeat visits per store
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │   4. PROFIT PER STORE ↑   (Hedgehog denominator)
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │   5. Reinvest: remodel existing stores, training, regional distribution density
   │            │
   │            ▼
   └── 6. Better stores + in-stock + delivery → attracts the best counselors and more customers
```

**Current push (next two turns):**
1. Make link 2→4 measurable. Run the 30–35 store remodel test with matched controls, measured on profit per store. Add a selling-model test (commission vs. hybrid) in matched stores.
2. Redirect capital from link 5's "new store" spending into remodeling the existing base. The remodel test covers only about 5–6% of the chain (*derived*).

**Doom-loop check:** several signs are present.
- **Jump-start ventures:** DIVX and the used-car rollout (target of 80+ stores, halted).
- **New program after new program:** six stand-alone appliance stores and the remodel test, launched at the same time.
- **Goals not reached and not dropped:** the $15B target (actual $12.6B, about 84%) and the 800-store target.
- **Risk ahead:** copying Rival B's format reactively, without understanding *why* it earns more per store, would be reaction without understanding. Test, then decide.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop funding new ventures outside home-electronics retail.** That includes formats, media plays and new retail concepts. DIVX cost about $233M in development and promotion. Its Year −1 charges (~$130M) equal about 40% of continuing earnings (*derived*).
2. **Stop managing the used-car business as part of Kestrel's agenda.** Pursue full separation with its own board and capital.
3. **Stop the six stand-alone appliance stores.** Stop matching home-improvement chains on appliance price.
4. **Stop setting goals in revenue and store counts.** Retire the $15B and 800-store targets and replace them with profit per store and ROIC.
5. **Stop opening stores that don't clear a profit-per-store hurdle.** Cut the ~25 planned openings to those that pass, and move the capital to remodels `[ASSUMPTION: hurdle set once the baseline exists]`.
6. **Stop defending commissioned selling as identity.** Test it, and keep it only if it wins on profit per store.
7. **Stop answering investor concerns with growth rates.** Answer them with returns and facts.

## 7. Core ideology and the BHAG

**Core purpose:** help people get the right technology into their homes and lives with confidence.

**Core values** `[ASSUMPTION: inferred, to be validated with staff]`:
1. Knowledgeable, honest help. A counselor's job is the right purchase, not the biggest one.
2. Operating excellence in every store, every day.
3. Facts over pride. The data decides, including about our own traditions.
4. Ownership. People own outcomes, not tasks.

**BHAG (10-year):** *Pass Rival B in profit per store within ten years, and never fall behind again.*

**Clock-building mechanisms:**
- A profit-per-store scorecard reviewed by the board every quarter.
- A standing brutal-facts review.
- Blameless autopsies of every major bet.
- A capital-allocation rule that each project must raise profit per store.
- A store-format team that tests continuously.
