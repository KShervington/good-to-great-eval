# Good to Great: Kestrel Stores, Inc.

## One-screen verdict

**Kestrel is good. It was great, and it is now sliding.** It outperformed the market for more than a decade, then spent the last few years on a used-car chain, a $233M disc-format bet and store-count targets while Rival B passed it on sales, earnings, profit per store and share. The record year hides this. Kestrel can be great again only if it gives up trying to be the biggest and narrows to the part of the market where expert help is worth paying for.

- **Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** *Be the store Americans trust when home technology is complicated, earning more profit per square foot from expert-assisted sales than anyone in the category.*
- **Weakest flywheel link:** counselors → customer confidence. No data shows customers experience commissioned counselors as experts rather than as pressure. Rival B's four-year run with self-service suggests many don't.
- **Top 3 stop-doing:**
  1. Stop chasing 800 stores and $15B. Drop store count as a goal.
  2. Stop funding ventures outside electronics. Separate the used-car business fully.
  3. Cancel the six stand-alone appliance stores.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** Rival B has led on sales, earnings, profit per store and national share for about four years, with a different selling model. Management's response has been diversification, not a fix of the core.
- **Missing numbers that would change this answer:**
  - Profit per store and per square foot, with trend, against Rival B.
  - Customer satisfaction and share by category, especially for complex products.
  - Appliance margins.
  - Store age and remodel cost.
  - ROE for the electronics business.

  If Kestrel's share and satisfaction in complex categories are no better than Rival B's, this Hedgehog Concept is wrong.

Greatness potential: 15/100

*Why 15:* a stronger rival with a working model, leadership showing doom-loop behavior, an outgoing CEO staying on as chairman, and no unit economics to confirm the narrow path. The plan improves the odds but does not make greatness likely.

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Kestrel is smaller in ambition and sharper in focus. It stops measuring itself by store count and revenue growth, and stops trying to out-Rival Rival B at self-service volume retail. Its stores are remodeled around the categories where customers need help: home theater, computers and home office. In those categories, trained counselors are paid to get the customer the right system working, not to push units. Installation, setup and service turn one sale into a relationship. Its distribution and store-operations systems, already among the best in the industry, are pointed at one number: profit per square foot. Low-ticket commodity media gets less floor space. Appliances stay only if they earn their square footage against the home-improvement chains, and the stand-alone appliance stores are cancelled. The used-car business runs under its own governance and no longer competes for management attention. If Kestrel disappeared, people who don't want to wire a home theater or set up a home office alone would feel the gap. That is the distinctive-impact test, and today Kestrel probably doesn't pass it.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence:** *Be the store Americans trust when home technology is complicated, earning more profit per square foot from expert-assisted sales than anyone in the category.*

**Denominator:** **profit per square foot of selling space** in the electronics business. The alternative is profit per store. Per square foot was chosen because it judges category mix and remodel decisions directly, and because it can't be improved by opening more stores.

| Circle | Candidates considered | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **Best in world at** | (a) Largest electronics chain | ✗ Rival B already leads. This is a race already lost. |
| | (b) Running big-box superstores of anything | ✗ This is the idea that justified used cars and the disc venture. It explains the drift, not the wins. |
| | (c) Low-price self-service volume | ✗ Rival B's model, and it is ahead there. |
| | (d) Major appliances | ✗ Probably not. Home-improvement chains are attacking on price with structural traffic advantages. Exit or keep depending on margin data. |
| | **(e) Expert-assisted selling of complex, high-ticket home technology, backed by top-tier distribution and store ops** | ✓ **Candidate.** It is the one part of the original formula ("knowledgeable salespeople") that Rival B gave up. Unproven `[ASSUMPTION]`. |
| **Drives the engine** | Revenue growth, store count, profit per store, **profit per sq ft**, profit per customer | Profit per sq ft. It rules out growth by store count and penalizes low-margin floor space. |
| **Passionate about** | Growth and scale (leadership's demonstrated passion); technology; helping customers get it right | `[ASSUMPTION]` Counselors and store teams care about knowing the product and closing the right sale. Leadership's demonstrated passion is for growth, and that has to be redirected. |

**Tests:**
- *Does it explain past wins?* Partly. "Knowledgeable salespeople" was a pillar of the original formula. But data showing which categories drove past profit is missing.
- *Does it rule things out?* Yes: used cars, disc formats, the 800-store target, stand-alone appliance stores, and much of the commodity-media floor space.
- *Is it uncomfortable?* Yes. It concedes the size race to Rival B and puts the heritage appliance category in question.

**Confirmation tests:** what would turn this hypothesis into a PASS.
1. Remodeled expert-format test stores beat matched control stores on profit per square foot, by at least `[ASSUMPTION: 10%]`, within two to three quarters.
2. Kestrel's share in complex categories (home theater, PCs) exceeds its overall national share.
3. Customer satisfaction in complex categories beats Rival B.
4. A redesigned counselor pay plan raises installation and service attach rates without raising returns or complaints.

If tests 2 and 3 fail, Kestrel may only be able to be *good*. Say that plainly at the 90-day re-check.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop targeting store count and revenue size.** Retire the 800-store and $15B goals publicly. The $15B goal needs about 19% growth this year, about 42% from electronics alone, so it will be missed anyway.
2. **Stop funding ventures outside electronics.** No new formats, proprietary technologies or adjacent retail concepts without a board-level Hedgehog review.
3. **Stop treating the used-car business as part of Kestrel's management agenda.** Move to full separation: its own board, its own capital, eventually a spin-off.
4. **Cancel the six stand-alone appliance stores.** Then decide on appliances within the superstores using profit per square foot. Lean toward shrinking or exiting if they can't beat home-improvement chains on economics.
5. **Stop paying counselors on pure volume commission.** Redesign the plan rather than copying Rival B's self-service model.
6. **Stop giving prime floor space to low-ticket commodity media** (music, movies) used as price-war traffic. Price-match; don't price-lead.
7. **Stop defaulting capital to new stores over remodels.** Slow the 25-store plan `[ASSUMPTION: to roughly half]` until the remodel test shows the payback.
8. **Stop issuing aggressive earnings-growth guidance** (20–25%) as the headline measure. Report per-store and per-square-foot metrics instead.
