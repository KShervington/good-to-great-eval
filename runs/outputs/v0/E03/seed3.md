# Marbury Laboratories: Good to Great

## One-screen verdict

**Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** *Marbury wins by holding the #1 or #2 position in essential health-care products that lower the total cost of care (diagnostics, nutrition, hospital products and focused medicines), and by making them at volume with quality no one questions.*

**Weakest link in the flywheel:** quality at scale. Federal regulators have raised quality-control concerns at the diagnostics plants for two years without resolution. Diagnostics is the business Marbury calls itself world leader in, and the whole flywheel depends on buyers trusting the product.

**Top 3 stop-doing items:**
1. Stop considering a pharmacy-benefit-manager purchase or a scale merger as the answer to consolidation.
2. Stop sizing branded-drug R&D to match the largest drugmakers. Fund only the drug arenas where Marbury can be #1 or #2.
3. Stop letting the diagnostics quality findings run as an open regulatory negotiation. Close them, even if it costs output and the earnings streak.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** unresolved quality problems in the flagship diagnostics business, at a company that has already lost reputation and hospital sales once, about 25 years ago, through a contaminated-product recall.

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Marbury is the company that hospitals, governments, HMOs and families turn to first when they need health care that works and costs less. It does not try to be the biggest drugmaker. It owns the #1 or #2 position in diagnostics, medical and infant nutrition, hospital products and a small number of focused drug franchises. It proves to cost-driven buyers, with evidence, that its products lower the total cost of care. It runs plants whose quality regulators hold up as a benchmark rather than a problem, and it funds its future from its own cash flow on an almost debt-free balance sheet. It stays independent while competitors merge, because its engine (profit per employee from high-volume, high-quality, cost-saving products) gets stronger as cost containment tightens. That engine does not depend on pricing power or research scale, which is why it gets stronger.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop evaluating a PBM purchase.** Owning a buyer conflicts with selling to all buyers, and nothing in the dossier suggests Marbury could be best in the world at pharmacy benefits.
2. **Stop considering a scale merger** as the answer to consolidation. Being mid-sized in drugs is a problem only if Marbury insists on competing in blockbuster discovery.
3. **Stop matching larger drugmakers' R&D.** Hold total R&D near today's 10.5% of sales and reallocate it inside the Hedgehog rather than increasing it.
4. **Stop counting on price.** At +0.8%, price is already gone as a growth lever. Plan as if it will be zero or negative.
5. **Stop running diagnostics plants with open quality findings as business as usual.** No new capacity expansion or major launches from affected plants until the findings are closed `[ASSUMPTION: severity unknown]`.
6. **Stop supporting product lines that can't reach #1 or #2** and don't lower the cost of care. Run them for cash, fix them, or sell them once the by-line data exists.
7. **Stop treating the 23-year earnings streak as a constraint.** Don't use buybacks or deferred quality spending to protect it.

## 7. Core ideology and BHAG

**Core values** `[ASSUMPTION: inferred; validate with leadership]`:
1. Quality and patient safety come before volume and before the quarter.
2. Science-led, self-built progress.
3. Value to the health system: lower the cost of care, don't just shift it.
4. Long-term independence and financial prudence.

**Core purpose:** *To improve health by making essential care work better and cost less.*

**BHAG (20-year horizon):** *Within 20 years, be the company health systems around the world rely on first to lower the cost of care: #1 or #2 in every business it operates, with no major quality action against it in the final decade of that period.*

**Clock-building mechanisms:**
- an annual #1/#2 portfolio review using profit per employee by line;
- quality oversight that reports to the board, independent of production;
- a formal succession process with at least two internal candidates ready;
- a health-economics evidence requirement before every major launch;
- written deal criteria: bolt-ons inside the Hedgehog only, no transformational mergers.
