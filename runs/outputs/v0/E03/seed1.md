# Good to Great: Marbury Laboratories

## One-screen verdict

**Marbury is already great by most of the evidence.** It has 23 straight years of higher earnings, a net margin of about 16.6%, return on equity near 38–39%, almost no debt, and world or national leadership in diagnostics, infant formula and one antibiotic class. The question for this run is not how to become great. It is how to **stay great while the industry around it consolidates**.

- **Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** *Be #1 or #2 in the world at health-care products that measurably lower the total cost of care, and earn more profit per employee than anyone else in the industry doing it.*
- **Weakest link in the flywheel:** **quality and trust.** The diagnostics plants have had regulators' quality findings open for two years. Diagnostics is the business where Marbury claims world leadership, and it is the business most exposed to cost-driven hospital buyers.
- **Top three stop-doing items:**
  1. Stop evaluating a transformational merger or a pharmacy-benefit-manager (PBM) purchase "to keep up in scale."
  2. Stop funding broad branded-drug discovery in areas where Marbury cannot be #1 or #2.
  3. Stop expanding output or launching products from the diagnostics plants until every regulator finding is closed.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** the quality problem in diagnostics is unresolved after two years. The company's own history shows the cost: a contaminated-IV recall about 25 years ago damaged reputation and hospital sales for years.
- **Missing numbers that would change the answer:**
  - Profit and market share by product line. These decide which businesses pass the #1/#2 test.
  - The severity of the regulators' findings.
  - The joint venture's sales and profit. If it carries pharma, the case for staying independent changes.
  - Shareholder returns compared with the market. This is the missing proof of "superior performance."

Greatness potential: 70/100

*Evidence note: this run was autonomous and had no search, memory or research tools. No earlier run or external market data was available. Everything rests on the dossier as of early Year 0. Inferences are marked `[ASSUMPTION]`.*

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

In its great version, Marbury is the company that cost-squeezed buyers want to buy from. Those buyers are hospitals paid a fixed amount per diagnosis, HMOs, PBMs, state programs and government formula bidders. They choose Marbury because its products lower the total cost of care, not just the price per unit. It holds the #1 or #2 position in every business it keeps: diagnostics, medical and infant nutrition, hospital products, and a focused set of drugs where it leads (anti-infectives, plus the prostate-cancer franchise through the joint venture). Its manufacturing quality is beyond reproach. It grows through volume rather than price, funds focused research out of its own cash, and declines the industry's merger race without regret. Its succession is boringly smooth, which is a deliberate contrast with the 1980s.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop evaluating a transformational merger or a PBM purchase** to match competitors' scale. Distribution and benefit management are not businesses where Marbury can be best.
2. **Stop funding broad branded-drug discovery** in therapeutic areas where Marbury cannot be #1 or #2. Hold R&D at about 10% of sales, but concentrate it.
3. **Stop adding capacity or launching products from the diagnostics plants** until every regulator finding is closed and verified.
4. **Stop treating the 23-year earnings streak as the goal.** No cuts to quality or focused R&D just to protect the streak.
5. **Stop accepting price-only contracts and bids** (for example, government formula tenders) that fall below a set profit-per-employee floor and have no cost-of-care advantage `[ASSUMPTION: bid profitability unknown]`.
6. **Stop keeping product lines that are neither #1/#2 nor have a credible path there** once the portfolio review identifies them. Decide each case explicitly. Don't drift.
7. **Stop running succession by crisis.** No more surprise exits at the top.
