# Marbury Laboratories: the great version

## One-screen verdict

**Marbury is already good, and on its record it is arguably great.** It has grown earnings for 23 straight years, earns about 39% on equity, carries almost no debt and has funded its growth organically. So this run is about staying great, not becoming great. The main danger is a stumble described in *How the Mighty Fall*, not mediocrity.

- **Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** *Marbury wins by being the world's most trusted maker of high-volume products that lower the total cost of care, led by diagnostics and medical nutrition, and it earns more profit per employee every year even as prices fall.*
- **Weakest link in the flywheel:** trust and quality. Two years of unresolved regulatory quality findings sit in diagnostics, the business Marbury calls itself world leader in. Every other turn of the flywheel depends on buyers trusting what Marbury ships.
- **Top three stop-doing items:**
  1. Don't merge or buy a pharmacy benefit manager to "keep up" with consolidation.
  2. Stop benchmarking branded-drug R&D against the largest drugmakers.
  3. Stop protecting the earnings streak at the expense of quality, maintenance or R&D.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** the diagnostics plant findings have stayed open for two years. Marbury has seen this before: a contamination recall about 25 years ago damaged its reputation and its hospital sales.
- **Missing numbers that would change the answer:**
  - Profit by product line. This decides whether branded pharma belongs inside the Hedgehog Concept or outside it.
  - The severity of the regulators' findings.
  - Total shareholder return against the market. This confirms or denies "great" on Collins's own test.
  - Market share by product.
  - The joint venture's economics.

Greatness potential: 68/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

The great Marbury doesn't try to become the biggest drug company. It becomes the company cost-constrained health systems can't do without. Its diagnostic tests and instruments, medical and infant nutrition, hospital products and niche drugs each make care cheaper or faster, and Marbury can prove that with health-economic evidence. It grows on volume because buyers seek it out, not on price. It turns that volume into rising profit per employee through manufacturing discipline and quality beyond reproach. It funds its own research from those profits, with no debt and no transformative mergers. Every product it ships is something a hospital quality officer would bet their career on. The company runs on mechanisms (quality governance, succession, capital-allocation rules), not on any one chief executive. It stays independent because its Hedgehog Concept doesn't require scale in the areas where consolidators are competing.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop considering a merger or a PBM purchase as a route to scale.** Nothing in the Hedgehog Concept needs drug-distribution ownership or blockbuster-scale marketing.
2. **Stop benchmarking R&D against the largest drugmakers.** Set R&D by the Hedgehog Concept, not by peer spending. There should be no across-the-board "step-up to compete".
3. **Stop letting the 24th consecutive year of earnings growth drive operating decisions.** No deferral of quality fixes, plant maintenance or R&D to protect the streak.
4. **Stop treating regulatory findings as a compliance-department matter.** No new diagnostics launches or capacity expansions from plants with open findings until those findings are closed `[ASSUMPTION: severity unknown; adjust once the findings are in hand]`.
5. **Stop funding broad, primary-care branded-drug programs where Marbury can't be best in the world.** Keep the niches where it leads, such as its antibiotic class, and therapies that cost-constrained buyers specifically value `[ASSUMPTION pending product-line profit]`.
6. **Stop selling commodity hospital lines on price alone.** Review IV solutions, anesthesia and other lines one by one. Keep the ones with a demonstrable cost-of-care or safety advantage, and reprice, rebundle or exit the rest `[ASSUMPTION]`.
7. **Stop bidding for government formula contracts at prices that lower profit per employee**, unless the bid clearly drives downstream volume that pays back.

**Non-negotiable disciplines:**
- Quality is first; it's never traded for volume or earnings.
- Every major project is tested against the Hedgehog Concept.
- The balance sheet stays conservative.
- Each product line proves its cost-of-care value with data.

**Framework boundaries:** business units have full autonomy over product, process and customer tactics inside the Hedgehog Concept and the quality rules. Capital allocation, acquisitions, and any move into new categories need a written Hedgehog test reviewed by the CEO and the board.

**Bureaucracy check:** if quality procedures keep multiplying without closing findings, the plants have a people-and-authority problem, not a rules problem.

## 7. Core ideology and the BHAG

**Core values** `[ASSUMPTION: inferred, to be validated with leadership]`:
1. Quality and patient safety first, with no exceptions.
2. Science-led self-reliance: grow by building, not buying.
3. Long-term thinking over the current quarter.
4. Financial conservatism.
5. Integrity with regulators, customers and payers.

**Core purpose:** *Make better health care more affordable for everyone.*

**BHAG (20-year horizon):** *Within 20 years, Marbury becomes the name health systems worldwide rely on to cut the cost of care, with a Marbury test, nutrition product or therapy part of most patient journeys in the countries it serves.*

**Clock-building mechanisms** (so this doesn't depend on any one CEO):
- A quality governance line from plant floor to board, with authority to stop shipments.
- Formal CEO and president succession planning, with at least two ready internal candidates.
- A written Hedgehog test for every major R&D program and every acquisition.
- A health-economics function attached to every product launch.
- Profit-per-employee reporting by segment, reviewed annually alongside earnings per share.
