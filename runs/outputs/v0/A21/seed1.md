# Good to Great: Harlan Pacific Corporation

## One-screen verdict

**Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** *Be the lowest-cost, highest-return bank in the American West: run it like a business, keep it on home ground, and measure everything by profit per employee.*

- **Weakest flywheel link:** whether **lower cost actually wins profitable customers**, or just shrinks the bank. This is untested, and Rival B's much larger branch network and consumer lead put it at risk.
- **Top 3 stop-doings:**
  1. Stop overseas expansion. Wind down the scattered offices and the West German stake.
  2. Stop new foreign and Latin American lending. Run the book off.
  3. Stop matching Rival B branch for branch. Consolidate weak branches.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** the cost base was built for a world of capped deposit rates. That world is ending, with ceilings phasing out over about three remaining years and money-market accounts already live. Funding costs are rising *now*, and the bank has no strategy for it.
- **Missing numbers that would change the answer:**
  1. **Latin American exposure as a share of capital.** If it is large, this becomes a survival plan, not a greatness plan.
  2. **Cost-to-revenue ratio against peers.** If Harlan is already low-cost, the Hedgehog must be something else.
  3. **Profit by business line.** If real estate is carrying everything, the concentration risk grows sharply.
  4. **The last three years of earnings and return on assets.**

Greatness potential: 35/100

*Why 35:* the opening is real. Deregulation is a generation-scale shift that punishes high-cost banks, and the new CEO built the bank's strongest profit line. Against that, most companies that try this never reach sustained greatness, recent performance is falling, the Latin American exposure is unsized, and the CEO's style is undocumented. Treat this as a hypothesis, not a forecast.

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Harlan Pacific is a **West-only bank** that has stopped trying to be global, stopped trying to out-branch Rival B, and stopped judging itself by size. It treats deregulation as an advantage: as deposit costs rise for everyone, it wins because it wastes less than anyone. Each branch, product and employee has a known profit number. The executive floor cuts its own costs first. Money freed from overseas ventures and surplus branches goes into cheaper delivery, such as ATMs and centralized operations, and into buying in-region banks whose costs it can strip out. It lends where it has proven expertise, West Coast businesses and commercial real estate, under credit and control limits that do not depend on the CEO's own instincts. Great here means **sustained profitability above larger rivals, Rival B included, for decades and across successive CEOs**. It does not mean being the biggest.

**Current push (next one or two turns):**
1. **Build the profit-per-employee and profit-by-line ledger**, then use it to set cost targets. Today no one can say which pieces earn their keep.
2. **Close the international drain and the weakest branches**, and redirect the freed people and capital into West Coast business and real estate lending.

The verdict is that the bank has been in a mild doom loop. The new CEO must avoid answering it with a big acquisition or a flashy venture *before* the cost flywheel has turned.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop overseas expansion.** Set a plan to wind down or sell the Seoul, Hong Kong and Bahamas branches and the Latin American and Asian representative offices `[ASSUMPTION: none earns above the bank average]`.
2. **Exit the West German bank stake.** It has already lost $4M on bad real estate loans.
3. **Stop new lending to Latin American and other foreign borrowers.** Run off the existing book under close watch.
4. **Stop the branch-coverage race with Rival B.** Close or merge underperforming branches.
5. **Stop chasing deregulation-era "new financial ventures"** unless they clearly raise profit per employee in the West.
6. **Stop measuring success by size or rank.** "11th-largest" goes off the scorecard.
7. **Stop executive-floor perks and overhead that don't produce profit.** Leaders cut themselves first.
8. **Stop national large-corporate lending outside the West** where Harlan has no edge `[ASSUMPTION; confirm with line profitability]`.

**Non-negotiable disciplines:**
- Every unit reports profit per employee monthly.
- Every new initiative must name its effect on the denominator.
- Credit limits by sector, with real estate included, are set independently of the CEO.
- Controls and audit are exempt from cost cuts.

**Framework boundaries:**
- *Fixed:* the West as geography, the cost discipline, credit limits, and the controls.
- *Free:* how each regional and line head hits their cost and profit targets, and how they serve their customers.

**Bureaucracy check:** after the cuts, review whether layers of approval exist because some seats hold people who can't be trusted with autonomy. If so, fix the seat rather than add a rule.
