# Wexley Medical: The Great Version

## One-screen verdict

**Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** Wexley wins by being the best in the world at practical, clinician-originated devices that save caregiver time or treatment cost, in narrow hospital niches it can lead. It starts with powered orthopedic instruments and is measured by **profit per focused niche**.

**Weakest link in the flywheel:** *Focused selling.* Wexley has effectively no sales force, so even its best products cannot reach the niches they could lead. The second weak point is that the product pipeline still depends on one inventor, the founder, who is in his early 80s.

**Top 3 stop-doing items:**
1. Stop routing decisions through the founder and family without a budget or goals. Install both this year.
2. Stop drifting toward implants or bone cement to "catch" the hip-replacement trend. That ground belongs to Rival H. Wexley's opening is to supply the operating room around those procedures.
3. Stop treating the IPO as a strategy. No offering until there are at least two years of budgeted, goal-tracked, audited results.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** Seventy percent of sales ride on beds and stretchers, and there is no one effectively selling anything. Revenue depends on reorders and reputation alone. Because profit and margins are unknown, nobody can yet say whether the business is healthy or slowly declining.

**Missing numbers that would change the answer:**
- Gross and net margin by product line, beds/stretchers versus instruments. This decides whether beds fund the transition or drag on it.
- Net income for the last 3–5 years. The only earnings figure available, the record of about $1M, is 22 years old.
- Market share in powered instruments and specialty patient handling.
- The hospital capital-spending outlook.

Greatness potential: 25/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Wexley is a set of small, focused product teams. Each one leads a narrow hospital or operating-room niche, starting with powered orthopedic instruments: cast saws, oscillating and reciprocating bone saws, and the power tools that surgeons need more of as joint replacement grows. Each team works from an annual budget and its own earnings goal. Each has specialist salespeople who know its surgeons and nurses by name, and each feeds a steady stream of clinician-originated ideas through the founder's test: *does it make caregivers more efficient or treatment cheaper?* Beds and patient handling continue where Wexley leads a niche and stop where it is only competing on price. The company no longer depends on one inventor or one family. The new product engine is institutional, the founder's rule is written down, and the company keeps compounding earnings year after year without needing an implant bet, an acquisition spree or an IPO to manufacture momentum.

**Sentence:** *Wexley wins by being the best in the world at practical, clinician-originated devices that save caregiver time or treatment cost, in narrow hospital niches it can lead. It starts with powered orthopedic instruments and is measured by profit per focused niche.*

**Tests:**
- **Explains past wins?** Yes. The turning frame became a category named after the founder. The cast saw and the patented bone saw each solved one problem well.
- **Rules things out?** Yes. It excludes implants, me-too beds, and expansion with no niche to lead.
- **Uncomfortable?** Yes. It implies the line that makes 70% of sales gets *less* new investment unless it proves niche leadership. It also turns away from the most visible trend in orthopedics.

**What would confirm it within 12–24 months:**
- Product-line P&Ls show instruments and specialty handling earn above-average margins.
- Specialist reps in instrument territories grow profit faster than generalist coverage did.
- At least two new clinician-originated products pass the design rule and reach a target share in their niche.

**Verdict: CONDITIONAL.** The "best at" and passion circles are well supported. The economic circle is unproven because no margin data exists by line.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop centralized, unbudgeted decision-making.** Every unit runs on an annual budget and written goals.
2. **Stop any move into implants or bone cement**, including licensing and acquisitions, unless a future Hedgehog review explicitly reverses this.
3. **Stop developing general-purpose beds and stretchers that compete on price.** Keep producing and supporting the existing lines. Invest new money only where Wexley leads a specialty niche.
4. **Stop IPO preparation** until there are at least two years of audited, budget-tracked results.
5. **Stop running one generalist, flat-salary sales force across all lines.** Move to specialist teams by niche, with pay that rewards results [ASSUMPTION: substantial variable component; test in pilot territories first].
6. **Stop adding countries to the international subsidiary** until the domestic model works.
7. **Stop launching any product that fails the founder's rule**, however much a clinician or executive loves it.

## 7. Core ideology and BHAG

**Core purpose:** To make caregivers more effective and patient care less costly.

**Core values:**
1. **Practicality first.** It must save time or cost. This is the founder's rule.
2. **Clinicians are the source.** Ideas come from people who treat patients.
3. **Own the result.** Every team owns a number.
4. **Frugal focus.** Lead a niche or leave it.
5. **Straight talk.** Bad news travels fast and without blame.

**BHAG (25-year horizon):** *Be the company surgeons and nurses reach for first in every niche Wexley enters, worldwide, with a record of uninterrupted earnings growth that no peer of its size can match.*

**Clock-building mechanisms:**
- Annual budget and goal cycle per niche.
- A written design rule and a clinical product council.
- A clinician-inventor program, with royalties or recognition [ASSUMPTION].
- A specialist sales training system.
- A board-approved CEO succession plan.
- Niche GMs who can later run divisions.

**Verdict: PASS.**

  - The founder has never built successors to succeed; one died suddenly, and none was prepared.
- **One behavior change:** For the first 90 days, the CEO makes no structural announcements without first asking the people affected, and publicly credits the team for early wins.

- **Bureaucracy check:** Keep the new budget process lightweight. Do not import big-pharma layers to compensate for doubts about people; fix the people question directly.
- **Fanatical consistency:** Every product passes the design rule. Every unit is reviewed monthly against its budget.
