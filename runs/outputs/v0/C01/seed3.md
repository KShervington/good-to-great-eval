# Good to Great: Wexley Medical Corporation

## One-screen verdict

**Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** *Wexley can be the best in the world at practical, clinician-born orthopedic devices, led by powered surgical instruments, that save caregivers time or cut treatment cost. It sells them through specialized reps who live in the OR and on the ward, and it measures success as profit per sales rep.*

**Weakest flywheel link:** the sales force. Wexley has, in effect, none. Since the move from commission to salary, the path from "good product" to "hospital buys it" is broken.

**Top three stop-doing items:**
1. Stop routing every decision through the founder and family. The new CEO needs written decision rights now.
2. Stop paying a flat-salary sales force with no variable pay tied to results.
3. Stop treating an IPO, or a move into implants and bone cement, as the next big thing. Both are jumps, not pushes on the flywheel.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** the company is effectively steering blind. It has no budget, no goals, no profit data by line, no sales force, and an innovation engine that has depended on one man now in his early 80s. Its last major new product came about 9 years ago.

**Missing numbers that would change the answer:**
- Net income and margin for recent years, split between beds/stretchers and instruments. If instruments earn far more per dollar, the Hedgehog Concept sharpens further toward them. If beds do, beds move to the center.
- A multi-year sales history (is the business growing, flat or shrinking?).
- Market share in powered orthopedic instruments versus beds.
- Hospital capital-spending and reimbursement trends.

Greatness potential: 30/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Wexley is a focused orthopedic device company, not a general hospital-furniture maker. Surgeons and nurses bring it their frustrations. It turns them into practical tools that pass the founder's test: make the caregiver more efficient or make treatment cheaper. Its powered instruments are the surgeon's default choice for cutting bone and casts, and demand for them rises with the growing number of joint-replacement procedures. It does not try to make the implants. Specialized, incentive-paid reps who know each surgeon by name carry the instruments into operating rooms. Specialty patient-handling equipment (turning frames, stretchers) runs as a disciplined second unit with its own profit and loss, funding the flywheel instead of diluting it. Budgets, goals and product-line P&Ls replace founder intuition. The company's identity survives the founder because his design rule has become a formal product gate, not a memory.

**Hedgehog sentence:** best-in-world practical orthopedic devices, led by powered instruments, that save caregiver time or treatment cost, sold by specialized reps, measured as profit per rep.

**Tests:**
- *Explains past wins?* Yes. The turning frame, cast saw and bone saw all started as a surgeon's frustration solved practically.
- *Rules things out?* Yes: implants, cement, generic beds competing on price, unrelated diversification, and expansion for its own sake.
- *Uncomfortable?* Yes. About 70% of sales sit in beds and stretchers, so the growth focus would point at the smaller line. It also means rebuilding a commission-heavy sales force the company just dismantled.

**Confirmation tests (in the next 6–12 months):**
1. Do product-line P&Ls show instruments earning higher margin per dollar and per rep than beds?
2. Do newly hired specialized instrument reps reach breakeven within roughly 12 months `[ASSUMPTION threshold]`?
3. Does a surgeon-sourced idea pipeline produce at least three candidates that pass the founder rule?

**If the tests fail:** if beds prove both more profitable and more defensible, the Hedgehog Concept shifts to specialty patient handling with instruments as the second unit. Re-run Gate 5.

**Current push (next one or two turns):**
1. Rebuild link 3: hire and pay a specialized instrument sales force, starting with the highest-volume orthopedic territories.
2. Restart link 2 without depending on the founder: a structured idea-intake process from surgeons, with the founder as advisor rather than sole source.

**Doom-loop check:** these signs are *present now or likely*:
- An IPO as a fix for leadership and funding problems. This is grasping for a big jump.
- The temptation to chase Rival H into implants. This is reaction without understanding.
- An outsider CEO importing big-company programs all at once. This produces new direction after new direction.
- The salary/commission flip-flop has already happened once.

Signs *not yet seen:* acquisitions to buy momentum.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop centralized founder/family decision-making.** Move to CEO decision rights plus a board governance role.
2. **Stop flat-salary-only selling.** Pay should carry a meaningful variable component tied to territory profit `[ASSUMPTION on exact mix]`.
3. **Stop discussing an IPO as a near-term goal.** Revisit only after about two years of budgeted, audited, growing results.
4. **Stop any move into implants or bone cement.** Instead, serve the procedures they create with instruments.
5. **Stop developing general-purpose beds that compete on price.** Concentrate patient handling on specialty products that pass the founder rule `[ASSUMPTION pending margin data]`.
6. **Stop launching any product that fails the founder rule**, however attractive the market.
7. **Stop expanding international sales forces** until the domestic model is proven.
8. **Stop running without a budget.** No more spending decisions made by instinct alone.
9. **Stop selling every line through one generalist channel.** Instruments and beds have different buyers: surgeons versus purchasing and nursing `[ASSUMPTION]`.

**Truth climate:** this is likely weak. A founder who held every decision, with no reporting system, leaves no channel for bad news. The charisma check therefore applies to the *founder*, not yet to the CEO. Install red-flag mechanisms:
- a monthly P&L review open to all managers;
- reps' lost-sale reports reaching the CEO directly;
- blameless reviews of failed launches.

**Faith statement:** *A 35-year reputation earned by solving real problems for caregivers, a patented instrument line, and a growing number of orthopedic procedures give Wexley the raw material to prevail, if it rebuilds the channel and the systems the family era never needed.*

## 7. Core ideology and the BHAG

**Core values (do not change):**
1. Every product must make caregivers more efficient or reduce the cost of treatment. This is the founder rule.
2. Clinicians are the source of ideas.
3. Owners of results, not followers of instructions.
4. Practical over fashionable.

**Core purpose:** to help caregivers deliver better care with less effort and lower cost.

**BHAG (20 years):** *Become the world's first-choice maker of powered orthopedic surgical instruments, in every major orthopedic operating room on every continent where Wexley sells.* This is ambitious relative to a $17.3M base. Calibrate it after market-share data arrives.

**Clock-building mechanisms:**
- an annual budget and goal-setting cycle;
- the founder rule as a formal product-review gate;
- product-line P&Ls with named owners;
- a sales incentive plan tied to profit per rep;
- surgeon advisory panels for idea intake;
- a documented CEO succession plan. The company has already lost one leader suddenly.

## 9. Next 90 days

1. **Secure written decision rights.** The board and founder delegate operating authority to the CEO, and the founder's new advisory role is announced. *Owner: CEO + board chair.*
2. **Hire a controller or CFO, and build the first budget.** Produce a monthly P&L by product line (beds/stretchers vs. instruments vs. international). *Owner: CEO → CFO.*
3. **Design the sales model.** Hire a VP Sales, approve a variable-incentive comp plan, and staff the first specialized instrument territories. *Owner: CEO → VP Sales.*
4. **Run a product audit and pipeline reset.** Score every product against the founder rule and the Hedgehog Concept, and start a surgeon idea-intake panel with the founder as advisor. *Owner: CEO + engineering lead.*
5. **Assess the people and formally defer the IPO.** Hold one-on-ones with each manager (keep / move seat / off), and have the board minute an IPO decision date of no earlier than about two years out `[ASSUMPTION]`. *Owner: CEO + board.*
