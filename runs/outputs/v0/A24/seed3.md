# Good to Great: Halstead Motors Corporation

## One-screen verdict

**The run stops at Gate 1. Halstead is not yet "good," so greatness work would be premature.** It is a large company with a real core: $13.6B in sales, a national dealer network, a defense franchise, and a new front-wheel-drive car family a year from launch. But its economic engine is running in reverse. It has lost money in four of the last five years, its losses are widening, private capital is unavailable, and it has said it may close by year-end without government aid. The framework assumes a working foundation, and the dossier itself says Halstead's immediate problem is survival.

In the candidate flywheel, the weakest link is **quality → buyer trust**. A new car from a maker known for recalls starts at a disadvantage.
- **Top three stop-doing items:**

  2. Stop trying to field a full line against the two largest makers.
  3. Stop keeping three exit strategies alive at once: merger, court reorganization and independent recovery.

## 1. The great version in one paragraph (not yet reachable)

**What "good" must look like first.** Halstead is funded through its new-car launch with a clear margin. It knows its break-even volume line by line and runs above it. It builds to dealer orders, so inventory sits at normal levels. It launches the front-wheel-drive family without a quality stumble. Its earnings come from operations, not aid.

**What great would mean afterward** (the draft definition from Gate 1, to be tested on the re-run):
1. **Superior performance:** profit per vehicle and return on capital above the two larger domestic rivals for a sustained period, not one good model year.
2. **Distinctive impact:** in the segments it chooses, buyers would miss a Halstead product that no rival offers in the same way.
3. **Lasting endurance:** the results survive the current CEO and the current hit product.

## 3. The flywheel

**Current state, the doom loop:**

```
 Product mix bets on the wrong demand
            ↓
 Unsold inventory (80k units, ~95 days)
            ↓
 Discounting + losses (−$207M in Q2)
            ↓
 No private capital; credibility lost
            ↓
 New products late; whole line can't be renewed
            ↓
 (back to) wrong product mix
```

**Candidate flywheel for the re-run** `[ASSUMPTION]`:

```
 Few platforms, many models
            ↓
 Lower cost per unit (profit per platform ↑)
            ↓
 Build quality + fuel economy buyers trust   ← weakest link
            ↓
 Dealers sell at full price, low inventory
            ↓
 Cash to fund the next platform on time
            ↓
 (back to) few platforms, many models
```

**Current push:** nothing on the candidate flywheel yet. The push is to **break the doom loop at its two cheapest points**: cut production to dealer orders, and protect launch quality.

**Doom-loop signs present:** direction after direction (big cars into the embargo, short of mid-size afterward, wrong again now); a search for rescue through a merger, government aid, and a leader from outside; and a single large product bet. The rescue moves may be necessary. They only work if they are followed by boring, consistent execution.

## 5. Stop-doing list (survival edition)

1. **Stop building cars dealers haven't ordered.** Bring inventory down to target before restoring output.
2. **Stop funding a full line** against much larger rivals. Commit capital first to the front-wheel-drive family, then only to lines the profitability data shows are positive.
3. **Stop running three strategies at once.** Pick independent recovery, with a written fallback trigger (for example, a cash floor or a failed financing date) that switches to reorganization or merger. Every week spent undecided burns cash and credibility.
4. **Stop letting a launch date override quality gates.** A failed launch of the front-wheel-drive car would probably end the company. A late launch might not.
5. **Stop blaming regulation for the decline.** Market share fell from 21% to 9% over 25 years, long before the current rules. Blaming outside forces weakens the case with legislators, who can see the history.
6. **Stop treating publicity as a substitute for operating numbers.** Publicity can earn time, but only the numbers will earn the financing.
