# Good to Great: Tolliver Company

## One-screen verdict

**Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** *Tolliver wins by running the best full-service consumer-electronics superstores, built in dense regional clusters, and it measures itself by profit per region.*

- **Weakest flywheel link:** *Profit per region → capital for the next cluster.* Nobody has shown that superstores earn more than the mid-sized stores. Each superstore is a big bet for a company this size, and debt is unknown. If cluster profits cannot fund the next cluster, the flywheel only turns on borrowed money.
- **Top three stop-doing items:**
  1. Stop buying chains for their tax credits or because they are distressed. Ring-fence the big-city chain and set a deadline for deciding its future.
  2. Stop running four formats in parallel. Open no new mid-sized stores and plan an exit from the West Coast leased departments.
  3. Stop entering a market with one store. Enter with a full cluster or stay out.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** Management cannot currently show which of its four formats makes money. Meanwhile it has made its two largest capital commitments in one year (four superstores and the big-city chain), and a rival backed by a deep-pocketed parent is blitzing new markets.
- **Missing numbers that would change this answer:**
  1. Profit and return on capital by format, especially superstore vs. mid-sized.
  2. Debt levels and current net worth.
  3. Cash burn at the big-city chain, and whether using its tax losses requires keeping it open.
  4. Current-year sales and net income.
  5. Market share outside the national capital area.

  If mid-sized stores turn out to out-earn superstores per dollar of capital, the Hedgehog Concept flips.

Greatness potential: 40/100

> **Evidence note:** This was an autonomous run with no search tools and no earlier run to compare against. No research was done, and the dossier is the only evidence. All inferences are marked `[ASSUMPTION]`. 4 of the 11 gates are CONDITIONAL, and they trace to one root cause: **there is no profitability data by format or by region.**

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

The great Tolliver runs one format, the large full-service electronics superstore. It enters a region with several stores at once and builds there until it holds the leading local share. Concentration gives it advertising, distribution and management advantages that single stores and mail-order rivals cannot match. Every region runs its own P&L. A new cluster is funded only after the existing clusters prove the model, and the company grows region by region at a pace set by how many proven regional managers it has, not by the deals on offer. It has left the leased-department, mail-order, tire, audio-boutique and tax-credit-acquisition businesses for good. Over time it becomes the electronics retailer that each region would miss most if it disappeared.

**Hedgehog sentence:** *Be the best full-service consumer-electronics superstore operator, region by region, and grow profit per region.*

**Tests:**
- *Does it explain past wins?* Partly. The company recovered from the loss after the superstore bet, and its 11% share in the capital area shows that concentrated presence works. That share, however, comes from mid-sized stores.
- *Does it rule things out?* Yes: three of the four current operations.
- *Is it uncomfortable to commit to?* Yes. It means walking away from a $6.6M acquisition logic and from the format behind its best-known share figure.

**What would confirm it within 12 months:**
1. Superstore return on capital beats mid-sized store return on capital.
2. A region with three or more superstores shows a lower advertising cost per dollar of sales than a single superstore.
3. The neighboring-state cluster reaches four-wall profitability.

**What would falsify it:** mid-sized stores out-earn superstores per dollar of capital. In that case the Hedgehog Concept becomes "mid-sized full-service stores, clustered by region," and the denominator still holds.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop acquiring chains** for tax credits, distress pricing or "instant presence." No acquisitions in the next 24 months.
2. **Stop putting capital and top talent into the big-city chain.** Cap its capital and set a 6-month deadline for a decision: sell, close or convert to the cluster model. Work out the tax-loss implications with tax counsel first. `[ASSUMPTION: whether the tax losses require keeping the chain open is unknown]`
3. **Stop opening mid-sized stores.** Keep the profitable existing ones and convert or close the rest as the profit-by-format data comes in.
4. **Stop the leased-department business.** Exit the remaining West Coast arrangement on a planned timeline.
5. **Stop single-store entry into new regions.** Enter with a full cluster or stay out.
6. **Stop trying to match Rival S's advertising spend in its markets.** Compete through density and full service in our own regions.
7. **Stop re-entering exited businesses** (mail order, audio boutiques, tires, general merchandise) and stop format experiments outside the superstore.
8. **Stop approving capital without region-level return forecasts and a review afterwards.**

## 7. Core ideology and the BHAG

- **Core purpose:** help people get the most out of the electronics in their lives, with expert advice and the right product at a fair price.
- **Core values:**
  1. Facts before direction.
  2. Customer service with real product expertise.
  3. Discipline over opportunism.
  4. Promote from within, region by region.
- **BHAG (20-year horizon):** *Be the number-one consumer-electronics retailer in every region we enter, and the largest in the country, built one cluster at a time.*
- **Clock-building mechanisms:**
  - a region P&L template;
  - a cluster-entry playbook (sites, advertising, distribution, staffing);
  - a capital rule: the next region is funded only from proven profit per region;
  - a regional-manager development pipeline;
  - a written succession plan for the CEO and the board chair.
