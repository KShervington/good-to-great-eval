# Good to Great: Tolliver Company

## One-screen verdict

**Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** *Tolliver becomes the dominant electronics-and-appliance superstore in each metro market it chooses, with the widest selection, knowledgeable service, sharp prices and stock on hand, and it grows only as fast as profit per market allows.*

**Weakest flywheel link:** "Market profits fund the next market." No one can currently show that superstores make more money per market than the mid-sized stores. Each superstore also costs a large share of the company's net worth.

**Top 3 stop-doing items:**
1. Stop running four formats in parallel. Freeze new mid-sized openings and new acquisitions until format-level profits are known.
2. Stop letting tax credits drive strategy. The big-city chain gets a fix-or-exit deadline.
3. Stop entering new markets with one or two stores. Enter only where you can build density, and stay out of Rival S's home markets.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** management is allocating its scarcest resource, capital, across formats without visible profit-by-format numbers. Meanwhile a rival backed by a deep-pocketed parent is buying markets with store clusters and heavy advertising.

**Missing numbers that would change this answer:**
- Profit and return on capital by format, especially superstore versus mid-sized.
- Losses at the big-city chain.
- Debt level.
- Net income and ROE for recent years.
- Sales for the last two years.
- Market share outside the capital area.

If the mid-sized stores turn out to be the better engine, the Hedgehog Concept changes.

Greatness potential: 35/100

*Evidence basis:* memory search, past-run lookup and web research were not possible in this session. Everything below comes from the dossier alone, which is dated early Year 0. Inferences are marked `[ASSUMPTION]`.

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Tolliver is one company with one format. It runs clusters of large electronics-and-appliance superstores and dominates each metro market it enters on selection, service, price and immediate availability. It does not run leased departments, it does not buy chains for their tax shelters, and it does not open lone stores in someone else's territory. Every market has its own P&L, and expansion is paced by proven profit per market and by how many trained store leaders it has ready. The founder's son runs it by asking hard questions. A management bench and a set of mechanisms would let it outlast him. Rival S may win markets with money, but Tolliver wins them with density and execution, and it would leave a real gap in those markets if it disappeared.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   ┌──► 1. Choose a metro and commit to density
   │        (multiple superstores, one ad budget)
   │                      │
   │                      ▼
   │   2. Selection + knowledgeable service + sharp price + in stock
   │                      │
   │                      ▼
   │   3. Traffic and volume per store rise
   │                      │
   │                      ▼
   │   4. Better buying terms; ad and overhead cost per store fall
   │                      │
   │                      ▼
   │   5. Reinvest in price and service → share in market rises
   │                      │
   │                      ▼
   └── 6. PROFIT PER MARKET rises → funds next market
          (and makes the tax-loss carryforwards usable)
```

The loop closes: 6 feeds 1. **Weakest link: 6 → 1.** Superstore profitability is unproven in the dossier, and each store is a large capital lump.

**Current push (next 1–2 turns):**
- **Turn A:** measure link 6 for every existing market and format.
- **Turn B:** deepen density in the neighboring state, where four superstores opened last year, before opening any new metro.

**Doom-loop check:**

| Sign | Present? |
|---|---|
| New direction after new direction | **Yes, historically.** The sequence ran licensed departments → acquisitions → audio specialty → mid-sized → superstores. That arc ended in a loss eight years ago. The loss-year cleanup was a correct turn. |
| Acquisition to jump-start momentum | **Yes.** The big-city chain was bought partly for its $36.5M in tax-loss credits and is struggling in the most competitive market in the country. |
| Inconsistency | **Yes.** Four formats run side by side, with no stated expansion route, pace or region. |
| Reaction without understanding | **Risk.** The temptation is to answer Rival S's ad blitz market for market. |

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop running four formats.** Freeze new mid-sized openings until the format comparison is done, then convert or close the losing format.
2. **Stop the tax tail wagging the dog.** No acquisition gets justified mainly by tax credits. The big-city chain gets a 90-day fix-or-exit decision. First check with tax counsel whether keeping the carryforwards depends on continuing that business `[ASSUMPTION]`.
3. **Exit the West Coast leased department.**
4. **Stop single-store market entries.** Enter a metro only with a funded plan to reach density.
5. **Stop fighting Rival S on its home turf, or matching its ad spend market for market.** Win where you already have density.
6. **Stop growing without a stated region and pace.** Publish internally which regions come next, how fast, and on what profit evidence.
7. **Keep stopped:** tires, hardware and housewares, mail-order, audio boutiques, new non-core categories.

**Truth climate:** this is a strength. The CEO leads with questions and presses the board and managers on direction. What's missing is a **red-flag mechanism**: monthly market-level P&L that no one can ignore.

**Charisma check:** there's no sign that bad news is being suppressed. The risk is the reverse: endless debate without commitment.

**Faith statement:** *Tolliver has already survived a near-death year by cutting what didn't work and backing a format customers came to; with the same discipline applied to one format and chosen markets, it will prevail.*
