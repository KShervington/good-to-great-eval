## 1. The great version in one paragraph

A great Marlow is not a national chain. It is the price-setter in a handful of regions where it has so many stores that each advertising dollar works harder than any rival's. Its unit economics show up in a monthly profit-per-market report that everyone from store manager to president reads. It opens a store only when an existing market's profits pay for it. It has tested larger formats in its home region on its own terms, instead of copying Rival T out of fear. It keeps one advantage rooted in its history: helping working families afford big-ticket electronics and appliances, which goes back to its installment-selling start `[ASSUMPTION: that capability still exists]`. It would stay great under a new president or a new owner, because the discipline lives in its mechanisms, not in a person.

## 3. The flywheel

```
        ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────┐
        ▼                                              │
 [1] Dense store cluster in one market                 │
        │                                              │
        ▼                                              │
 [2] Lower ad + distribution cost per store            │
        │                                              │
        ▼                                              │
 [3] Sharper prices & promotions (funded by cost,      │
     not by parent cash)                               │
        │                                              │
        ▼                                              │
 [4] Traffic & local share rise                        │
        │                                              │
        ▼                                              │
 [5] Higher volume → better vendor terms → lower cost  │
        │                                              │
        ▼                                              │
 [6] PROFIT PER MARKET rises ──► funds next store in ──┘
                                 same/adjacent market
```

**Current push (next two turns):**
1. Measure link [6] by building a store- and market-level P&L.
2. Pour new capital only into filling in markets that already show positive profit per market, starting with the home region.

**Weakest link:** [6] back to [1]. Today the parent's capital substitutes for market profits, so the wheel is being pushed from outside rather than turning on its own.

**Doom-loop check:** several signs are present.
- Big programs to force momentum: several stores at once in new markets, plus ad blitzes.
- Reversal of direction: the founder grew by buying chains, the president grows organically.
- Category swings: audio added, small appliances dropped.
- Two leadership or ownership shocks in six years.
- A parent for whom retail is not core.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. Stop opening in new, unfamiliar regions ahead of proven per-market economics.
2. Stop using ad blitzes as an entry tactic where store density can't spread the cost.
3. Stop measuring success by store count and total revenue.
4. Stop funding growth with parent capital that isn't tied to market-level returns.
5. Stop operating subscale outlier markets indefinitely. Fill each one to density or exit it.
6. Stop cutting price where there is no cost advantage behind the cut.
7. Stop reactive category swings (add, drop, re-add) without category-level profit data.
8. Stop copying the rival's superstore format out of fear. Pilot it deliberately in the home region instead.

## 7. Core ideology and the BHAG

- **Core purpose:** Put quality home electronics and appliances within reach of working families. This echoes the installment-selling origin.
- **Core values `[ASSUMPTION]`:**
  - honest low prices
  - relentless cost thrift
  - every market earns its own growth
  - store teams own their market's results
- **BHAG (15-year horizon):** Be #1 in electronics-and-appliance share in every market Marlow operates in, with every new store paid for by its own market's cash flow.
- **Clock-building mechanisms:**
  - A monthly profit-per-market review.
  - A capital rule: no new market until existing markets clear a profit threshold.
  - A red-flag report that any store manager can escalate.
  - A store manager → market lead succession bench.
  - A written owner agreement with the parent on holding period and capital terms.
