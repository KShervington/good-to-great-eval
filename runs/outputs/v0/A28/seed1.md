**Top 3 stop-doing:**

2. Stop using company stock as the default acquisition currency. Use it only when a deal clears a written return-on-capital hurdle.

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Corvane is a narrow, deep company. It builds the smallest, most reliable digital computers and control systems for jobs where a wrong or late answer is unacceptable: fire direction, navigation, space data, and later industrial and commercial process control. It makes its own semiconductor devices because those devices make its systems better, not because it wants to compete with the big merchant chip makers. It wins hard research programs, converts them into production programs, earns a high return on every dollar of capital, and reinvests that return in solid-state process skill and engineers. Acquisitions are occasional, small and on-thesis, paid for mostly in cash and judged by return on capital. It has operators who run divisions without the founder in the room, and a capital-allocation rulebook the board enforces. Defense remains its proving ground, but a growing share of revenue comes from non-defense customers who need the same thing: rugged real-time digital control.

**Why not the capital-allocation candidate?** This is the uncomfortable call. The founders' stated plan is to build a major company mainly by acquisition, and the chairman decides where all the capital goes. A capital-allocation Hedgehog Concept could produce excellent financial results *while he is there*. It fails the skill's third test of greatness, lasting endurance, unless the allocation discipline is written down and institutionalized. So the Hedgehog Concept above keeps capital allocation as a *discipline in service of* the narrow business, not as the business itself.

**Tests:**
- *Explains past wins?* Partly. The artillery computer contract, the navigation and satellite data processors, and the devices division all fit. The broad studies and antenna design fit loosely.
- *Rules things out?* Yes: off-thesis acquisitions, merchant commodity chips, and pure study contracts with no path to production.
- *Uncomfortable to commit to?* Yes. It contradicts the founders' stated wide-acquisition ambition.

**Evidence that would confirm it (12–24 months):** in-hedgehog units earn a clearly higher return on capital than out-of-hedgehog units; at least one more research-to-production conversion; at least one non-defense customer buying a Corvane control system.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. Stop acquiring anything outside digital computing, real-time control and solid-state systems, however cheap or attractive it looks.
2. Stop paying with stock by default. Stock is allowed only for on-thesis deals that clear the return-on-capital hurdle, with the board's acquisition review signing off.
3. Stop treating sales, backlog or headcount growth as success measures. Report organic growth separately from acquired growth.
4. Stop bidding on study contracts that have no plausible path to production, unless they are deliberate, capped research bets.
5. Stop competing as a merchant semiconductor maker against established firms. Make devices for Corvane's own systems first.
6. Stop diversifying away from defense for its own sake. Diversify only where the same real-time control capability applies.
7. Stop routing every capital decision through one person without written criteria the board can check.

**Non-negotiable disciplines:** a monthly return-on-capital-by-unit review; every program tagged research or production, with conversion tracked; a written acquisition charter; a blameless post-mortem on every lost bid and every acquisition at 12 months.

**Framework boundaries:** inside the Hedgehog Concept and the capital rules, division GMs have full autonomy over engineering, hiring and bids. **Bureaucracy check:** if headquarters starts adding approval layers over acquired units, that usually means the wrong unit leaders are in place. Fix the people, not the paperwork.
