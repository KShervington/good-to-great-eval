# Good to Great: Ravenhill Drug Company

## One-screen verdict

**Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** *Ravenhill will be the most convenient drugstore in America, the pharmacy on the nearest good corner, and it will grow profit per customer visit.*

**Weakest flywheel link:** customer visits → profit per visit. Third-party payers are pushing down prescription margins at the same moment prescriptions are becoming the core of the business. Rx rose from 38.2% to 43.4% of sales while gross margin fell from 28.4% to 28.0%.

**Top three stop-doing items:**
1. Stop treating store count (the "3,000 stores" goal) as the goal. Treat it as an output of density and profit per visit.
2. Stop considering chain acquisitions as a way to keep up in size with the merging rival. Buy only stores that would pass your own site test.
3. Stop accepting third-party plans that fall below the minimum-profitability standard. Walk away from them.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** the part of the business that is growing, prescriptions, is the part whose margin outsiders now set. Payers, benefit managers and mail-order suppliers are positioned between Ravenhill and its customers.

**Missing numbers that would change this answer:**
- Profit per customer visit and profit per store, which together confirm or kill the denominator.
- Same-store sales.
- Returns on freestanding stores versus older strip-center stores.
- Profitability of mail service and the managed-care subsidiary.
- Prescription market share.
- Shareholder return compared with the market.

Greatness potential: 72/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Ravenhill is the drugstore people choose without thinking about it, because it is the closest one, the easiest to get in and out of, and it holds their prescription history at every location. It builds dense clusters of freestanding corner stores with drive-through windows. It links every store to one pharmacy record, so any store can fill any prescription. It uses that convenience to win more visits and more profit on each visit: the prescription brings the customer in, and photo and front-end goods add to the basket. It pays for its own growth out of earnings with no debt. It says no to unprofitable payer contracts, to size-for-size acquisitions, and to any business outside the drugstore. It keeps compounding long after the current chairman, because site selection, payer discipline and capital rules are written down and owned by people other than him.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

| Circle | Candidates considered | Selected | Ruled out |

| **Deep passion** | A pharmacist founder's legacy; technology leadership; the family's steady stewardship over nearly a century | **The trusted pharmacy, run as a family institution, made easier to use with technology** `[ASSUMPTION]` | Chasing size for its own sake |

**Why visits:** convenience produces visits. A visit that starts with a prescription can carry high-margin front-end and photo sales. The things Ravenhill already invests in (corner sites, drive-through, one-hour photo, refill anywhere) only make sense if the strategy is to win more visits and make each one worth more.

**Tests:**
- *Does it explain past wins?* Yes. The exits from restaurants, optical, discount stores and Mexico all narrowed the company toward the drugstore. The move to freestanding sites and the refill network both target convenience.
- *Does it rule things out?* Yes. It rules out size-for-size M&A, building the benefit-management business as a separate growth engine, mall and strip locations, and low-margin payer volume.
- *Is it uncomfortable to commit to?* Yes. It means passing on a large acquisition while a rival becomes the biggest chain by store count, and it may mean losing some prescription volume to payers.

**Confirmation tests (working hypothesis):**
1. Within 90 days, profit per visit can be measured, and it is higher in freestanding drive-through stores than in strip stores.
2. Profit per visit is stable or rising even while Rx margins fall, meaning front-end and photo sales attached to each visit offset the margin loss.
3. Stores in dense clusters beat isolated stores on profit per visit.

If test 1 fails, go back to Gate 5 and test profit per store or per pharmacy hour as the denominator instead.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. Stop treating the store-count target as the goal.
2. Stop considering chain acquisitions to match a rival's size. Buy only stores that pass the site test, one by one or in small groups.
3. Stop accepting third-party plans below the minimum-profitability standard.
4. Stop growing benefit management or mail service as standalone businesses. Grow them only where they bring visits back to stores.
5. Stop opening in a new metro area without a committed density plan.
6. Stop leasing or remodeling mall and strip sites that fail the convenience test. Keep closing them, as with last year's 88 closures.
7. Stop running the mini-store test with no end date. Decide this year.
8. Stop competing on price with supermarkets and discounters. Compete on convenience.

**Non-negotiable disciplines (done every time):** every new site is checked against written criteria; every payer contract is checked against the profitability floor; growth is paid for from earnings with no debt; one pharmacy record covers every store.

**Freedom within a framework:**
- *Framework:* the site rules, the payer floor, the capital rules and a single pharmacy system.
- *Autonomy:* store and regional teams decide front-end mix, local service and staffing within those rules.

**Bureaucracy check:** there's no evidence of compensating rules in the dossier. Watch for approval layers that pile up around new-store openings at 200+ per year.
