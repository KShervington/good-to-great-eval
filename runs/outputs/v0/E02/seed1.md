# Ravenhill Drug Company: the great version

## One-screen verdict

**Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** *Ravenhill wins by being the most convenient pharmacy in every market it chooses to serve: a dense network of corner stores where any prescription can be filled or refilled fast. It runs that network to maximize profit per customer visit.*

- **Weakest flywheel link:** visits → profit per visit. Third-party payers make each prescription visit less profitable even as prescriptions grow. Prescriptions rose from 38.2% to 43.4% of sales in two years, and gross margin slipped from 28.4% to 28.0%.
- **Top three stop-doing items:**
  1. Stop treating chain acquisitions as the answer to the rival merger. Buy sites, not chains.
  2. Stop accepting third-party plans that fall below the minimum profitability standard. Walk away instead.
  3. Stop opening or renewing in-line strip and mall stores that can't become corner or drive-through sites.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** a rival merger under review would create a ~4,500-store, $11B+ chain. That would take away Ravenhill's sales lead and more than double its store count. It will raise pressure from inside, from Wall Street and from payers to "get bigger" in a way that departs from the Hedgehog. That rival's stores would average roughly $2.4M in sales each, against Ravenhill's ~$5.0M. Ravenhill's advantage is productivity, not count. Protect that.
- **Missing numbers that would change this answer:**
  - Profit per customer visit, and same-store sales. If per-visit profit is flat or falling, the denominator or the strategy is wrong.
  - Prescription profit by payer.
  - Returns on freestanding stores compared with strip-center stores.
  - Profitability of mail service and the managed-care subsidiary. If these are strongly profitable, the stop-doing scope for them needs revisiting.
  - Shareholder returns compared with the market. Without this, "superior performance" is unproven.

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Ravenhill doesn't try to be the biggest drugstore chain. It is the one that is easiest to use. In every metro it enters, it reaches density quickly, putting a freestanding corner store with a drive-through within a few minutes of most customers. Every store is linked so any prescription can be refilled anywhere, and the workflow system makes pickup fast and predictable. Front-store merchandise and one-hour photo exist to make each visit worth more, not to compete with discounters on price. The company refuses chain acquisitions that would bring in poorly sited stores. It refuses payer contracts that make visits unprofitable. Its pharmacy-benefit and mail operations exist to route prescriptions into the store network, not to become separate businesses. It measures itself by profit per customer visit and density in the markets it serves, not by industry sales rank. And it runs on mechanisms (site criteria, payer standards, succession) that will outlast the current chairman.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │                                                          ▼
 [1] Dense corner sites           [2] Most convenient pharmacy
     with drive-through   ───────►    (close, fast, refill anywhere,
     (self-chosen, owned)             workflow speed, photo)
   ▲                                          │
   │                                          ▼
 [6] Network density gives           [3] More visits per customer and
     payer leverage (plans need          per store (Rx + front store)
     our network on our terms)                │
   ▲                                          ▼
   │                                 [4] PROFIT PER CUSTOMER VISIT
 [5] Self-funded capital: sites   ◄───── (basket, Rx mix, purchasing
     + tech, no debt                      scale, payer standards)
```

The loop closes: [6] makes [1] easier to defend and fund, and draws more prescription traffic into [2].

- **Weakest link:** [3]→[4]. Payers push more volume through each store while taking margin out of each prescription. If profitability per visit falls, the wheel turns faster but makes less money each turn.
- **Current push (next one or two turns):**
  1. **Measure and defend [4].** Instrument profit per visit by store, site type and payer mix. Hold the payer standard even when that means losing a plan.
  2. **Densify before expanding [1].** Fill out existing metros and the three new ones before entering more.
- **Doom-loop check:**
  - Consistency is strong: decades of exits, organic growth and steady technology investment.
  - **Warning signs:**
    - Three new metros entered in one year.
    - A new managed-care direction launching in January.
    - The rival merger inviting an acquisition reflex driven by fear of losing rank. That would be "reaction without understanding."
  - **Arithmetic flag:** the 3,000-store target. At ~88 closings a year, 200–215 gross openings net out to ~115–125 a year. That reaches ~2,700 in five years, not 3,000. Hitting 3,000 needs ~180+ net a year, or ~270 gross openings. That pace could tempt the company into lower-quality sites or acquisitions. **Recommend replacing the store-count target with a density/productivity target.**
  - **Verdict:** PASS.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop considering chain acquisitions** to match the rival's size or bargaining power. Buy individual sites or stores only if they meet corner-site criteria.
2. **Stop serving third-party plans that fall below the minimum profitability standard.** Make it an explicit walk-away rule, not a negotiating stance.
3. **Stop opening, renewing or remodeling in-line strip and mall stores** that can't be relocated to freestanding sites. Relocate or close them.
4. **Stop building the managed-care/PBM subsidiary as a separate business.** Limit its scope to feeding the store network.
5. **Stop entering new metros faster than you can reach density in them.** Set a minimum store-count plan per metro before entering `[ASSUMPTION: threshold to be set from store-economics data]`.
6. **Stop using industry sales rank and store count as headline goals.** Replace the 3,000-store target with profit-per-visit and density goals.
7. **Stop competing with discounters and supermarkets on general-merchandise price and breadth.** Keep only the categories that increase visits or basket size on a convenience trip.
8. **Stop the open-ended mini-store test.** Set a decision date.

**Non-negotiable disciplines:**

- Every new store meets the site criteria.
- Every payer contract meets the profit standard.
- Every store is on the shared pharmacy network and workflow system.
- Self-fund growth: keep long-term debt negligible.

**Framework boundaries (freedom within a framework):**

- **Fixed centrally:** site criteria, payer standards and systems.
- **Store and regional autonomy:** staffing, local assortment within the convenience categories, and service execution.

**Bureaucracy check:** nothing obvious from the dossier. Watch for rules being added to manage a fast-growing part-time workforce, where the better fix is hiring and keeping the right people.
