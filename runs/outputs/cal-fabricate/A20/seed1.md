# Brightwater Drug Corporation: Good to Great

## One-screen verdict

**Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** *Brightwater should be the most convenient, lowest-priced drugstore in the Southeast and Texas, built metro by metro to a density that a distant national chain can't match, and judged by profit per customer visit.*

**Weakest link in the flywheel:** the step from **density to cost advantage to lower prices**. It is unproven, and the acquisition spree works against it. Chains bought in Louisiana, Texas, Georgia and the Mid-Atlantic spread the store base out instead of concentrating it, while capital went into four unrelated businesses.

**Top three stop-doing items:**
1. Stop owning businesses that aren't drugstores. Divest the junior department stores, security services and food-service supply.
2. Stop acquiring to grow. Impose a moratorium on acquisitions until store-level economics exist and every deal passes the denominator test.
3. Stop leaving the founder's role undefined. He chooses either full-time leadership or a non-executive chair with real authority handed to the CEO, and he says so publicly.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** The largest drug chain in the country (about 600 stores, about $1B in sales) has just bought 29 stores in Brightwater's home state. Brightwater is meeting it with capital split across five business lines and a founder who has spent five years running for office.

**Missing numbers that would change the answer:**
- Profit and return on capital by business line.
- Store count and share in each metro, compared with Rival W.
- Sales and profit per store and per customer visit.
- The successor CEO's track record.
- The founder's political plans.
- What share of stores depends on the supermarket partner's leases.


Key numbers: revenue growth of 23.7% a year, operating margin of 17.9%, customer retention of 91.3% and return on capital of 14.6%. Market share is 27.4% and rising.

`Greatness potential: 24/100`

*Why 24:* the core drugstore business shows real signs of good. These include a self-service pioneer, a legal win that freed it to discount, an advantaged site pipeline, and 14 years of public-company growth. The plan gives it a credible path. Still, the base rate for sustained good-to-great transitions is very low. The steward's attention is uncertain, a much larger rival has just entered the home market, and the company's recent habit is to diversify rather than focus. Even with the plan carried out, the odds are below even.

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Brightwater is a pure-play drugstore chain and the default drugstore for families across the Southeast and Texas. It is never the biggest chain nationally, but it is first or second in every metro it chooses to enter. Its stores cluster tightly, many next to the supermarkets where people already shop. That density lets it buy, distribute and advertise more cheaply per store than a rival operating far from its home base, and it passes those savings on as everyday discount prices. The company is managed by a single number, profit per customer visit, and every new store, remodel and acquisition must improve it. Cash from the junior department stores, security, food-service and possibly optical businesses has been put back into infill stores and the pharmacy counter. The founder's fairness-to-employees culture has become systems that no longer depend on his signature or his presence: store-level P&Ls, promotion from within, and pharmacist career paths. The company would stay great whether he serves in public office or not.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   ▼                                                          │
[1] Dense clusters of convenient sites                         │
    (supermarket-adjacent, Southeast + Texas)                  │
   │                                                          │
   ▼                                                          │
[2] Everyday discount prices + self-service convenience        │
   │                                                          │
   ▼                                                          │
[3] More customer visits per store (pharmacy + front store)    │
   │                                                          │
   ▼                                                          │
[4] Higher PROFIT PER CUSTOMER VISIT  ◄── denominator          │
   │                                                          │
   ▼                                                          │
[5] Cash flow funds infill stores in existing metros           │
   │                                                          │
   ▼                                                          │
[6] Density lowers per-store buying, distribution, ad costs ───┘
    (funds lower prices → back to [1]/[2])
```

**Weakest link:** the step from [6] to [2]. Density only creates a cost advantage if stores are actually clustered. The recent acquisitions spread the footprint across five or more regions, and the diversified businesses absorb cash that should go into [5].

**Current push (next one or two turns):**
1. **Measure [4].** Build store-level P&L and visit counts so the loop can be steered at all.
2. **Feed [5] from divestitures.** Redirect proceeds from non-core sales into infill stores in the home state, where Rival W is now competing.

**Doom-loop check: warning signs present.**
- *Acquisitions used to jump-start growth:* six or more acquisitions, across four unrelated fields.
- *New direction after new direction:* candy was bought and later sold, then department stores, security, food-service and optical were added.
- *Leader inattention:* five years of campaigns.

None of these is fatal. Together they fit the doom-loop pattern, and stop-doing items 1 to 3 exist to break it.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop owning the junior department stores.** Divest them.
2. **Stop owning the security and guard company.** Divest it.
3. **Stop owning the food-service distributor.** Divest it.
4. **Stop expanding optical** until it proves it raises profit per visit. Then either fold it into the drugstores or exit.
5. **Stop making acquisitions to grow.** Impose a moratorium. After it lifts, buy only infill chains in core metros that pass the denominator test.
6. **Stop planting flags in new regions.** Review whether the Mid-Atlantic chain belongs in the Hedgehog.
7. **Stop trying to match Rival W nationally.** Fight it metro by metro, on density and price.
8. **Stop leaving the founder's role open-ended.** Define it in writing.
9. **Stop relying on personal gestures to carry the culture.** The birthday cards already stopped at 8,000 employees. Replace them with systems.
10. **Stop opening stores that don't meet a profit-per-visit hurdle,** no matter how attractive the site looks.

The candy company sale three years ago shows Brightwater *can* exit a business. Use that as the precedent.
