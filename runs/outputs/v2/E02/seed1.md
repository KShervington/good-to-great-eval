# Ravenhill Drug Company: Good to Great (Stewardship Run)

**Verdict: Already great, so this is a stewardship run.** Ravenhill has 21 straight years of record sales and earnings, a 19.1% return on equity and no meaningful debt. It has spent two decades exiting everything that isn't a drugstore.

- **Hedgehog Concept (confirmed, not invented):** *The most convenient drugstore in every neighborhood it enters, run for profit per customer visit.*
- **Weakest flywheel link:** turning visits into profit. Payers keep squeezing prescription margins while prescriptions grow to 43.4% of sales.
- **Temptations to refuse:**
  1. A big chain acquisition to answer the rival merger.
  2. Growing the pharmacy-benefit and mail business as a standalone instead of as a feeder to the stores.
  3. Letting the 3,000-store target override site quality.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** gross margin slipped to 28.0% as the sales mix shifts toward lower-margin prescriptions whose prices payers control.
- **Missing numbers that would change this:** profit per visit and per store, returns on freestanding versus strip-center stores, and shareholder return versus the market.
- **Confidence:** moderate-high. It rests on the 21-year record and the discipline of the exits, not on unit economics.

`Greatness potential: 74/100`

---

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Ravenhill is the drugstore you can't avoid passing. Its stores are on freestanding corner sites, clustered densely enough that one is always a short drive away, with a drive-through window. Any prescription can be refilled at any store. Every decision is judged by one number, profit per customer visit, and not by store count, total sales or rank in the industry. Its scale gives it the leverage to make third-party payers meet minimum profitability standards. Its managed-care and mail arms exist to send customers into the stores, not to become a separate business. It funds its own growth, stays out of debt, and keeps compounding through consolidation waves it declines to join, until rivals that bought their size are worth less per store than Ravenhill is.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence (working hypothesis):** *The most convenient drugstore in every neighborhood it enters, run for profit per customer visit.*

| Circle | Candidates considered | Kept | Ruled out |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Best in the world at** | Lowest price; widest assortment; pharmacy benefit management; convenient neighborhood drugstores | **Convenient neighborhood drugstores.** The evidence: refill at any store since the 1980s, the move to corner sites with drive-throughs, one-hour photo | Restaurants, discount department stores, optical, foreign retail (already exited, which explains past wins). | **Economic engine** | Profit per store; profit per square foot; profit per prescription; profit per customer visit | **Profit per customer visit** `[ASSUMPTION: not measured in the dossier]` | Total sales and store count. These are outputs, and the rival merger shows anyone can buy them |
| **Passion** | Retail scale; technology; pharmacy | **Pharmacy-centered convenience.** The company was founded by a pharmacist, and the family has stewarded it for nearly a century `[ASSUMPTION: passion inferred from history and choices, not stated]` | Being "the largest chain" |

**Why profit per visit and not per store:** convenience pays off through *frequency*. Corner sites, drive-throughs and refill-anywhere all add visits. A per-visit measure also rewards the front-store purchase that comes with a prescription, which partly offsets the shrinking prescription margin.

**Supporting derived figure:** Ravenhill does about $5.0M in sales per store ($10.4B ÷ 2,085, which includes some mail sales). The merged rival would do about $2.4M ($11B ÷ 4,500). Productivity per location, not footprint, is the existing edge.

**Confirmation tests (all must hold to move this from hypothesis to confirmed):**
1. Profit per visit is higher, or rising faster, at freestanding drive-through stores than at strip and mall stores.
2. Stores in dense clusters beat isolated stores on profit per visit.
3. Prescription customers make more front-store purchases per visit than non-prescription customers. If they don't, the denominator may need to be profit per store instead.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   ┌──► 1. Convenient sites (freestanding corners, drive-through, dense clusters)
   │              │
   │              ▼
   │    2. More customer visits (refill anywhere, front store, one-hour photo)
   │              │
   │              ▼
   │    3. Prescription volume → leverage with payers and suppliers
   │       (minimum profitability standards)
   │              │
   │              ▼
   │    4. PROFIT PER CUSTOMER VISIT  ◄── weakest link (payer squeeze)
   │              │
   │              ▼
   │    5. Self-funded cash (no debt; ~$310M capex ≈ earnings)
   │              │
   │              ▼
   └─── 6. More stores in denser clusters + pharmacy workflow technology
```

**Current push (next 1–2 turns):**
- **Instrument link 4.** Use the new chainwide scanning and inventory data to measure profit per visit by store and by cohort. Today the company can't see its own denominator.
- **Defend link 3.** Keep enforcing minimum profitability standards with payers. Accept losing volume on contracts that fall below them.

**Doom-loop check: clean.** The strategy has been consistent for years. There are no big jump-start acquisitions (24 stores acquired in five years). Exits ran on fixed deadlines that were kept. The one live doom-loop trigger is *external*: the rival merger invites a reaction without understanding.

**Tech triage (Gate 7)**

| Technology | Classification | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Satellite-linked pharmacy system (refill anywhere) | **Accelerates** | This is the convenience promise in technical form |
| Chainwide scanning + inventory system | **Accelerates** | Only if it is used to compute profit per visit. Otherwise it's parity |
| Next-generation pharmacy workflow | **Accelerates** | Frees pharmacist time and shortens waits. Protects visit quality as volume grows |
| One-hour photo (400+ stores) | **Neutral to accelerating** | A visit driver. Keep it if per-visit data shows it brings extra trips |
| Drive-through windows | **Accelerates** | Convenience built into the store |
| Mail-service pharmacy | **Neutral or parity, at risk of cannibalizing** | Keep up with mail-order competitors, but don't let it pull visits out of stores |

Where Ravenhill could pioneer: (1) store-level profit per visit computed from scanning plus pharmacy data; (2) pharmacy workflow that becomes a measurable wait-time advantage.

## 5. Stop-doing list (stewardship mode: real temptations only)

1. **Don't buy a large chain to match the rival merger.** Ravenhill has never integrated more than 66 stores. If approved, the merger will take the "largest by sales" title ($11B+ vs $10.4B). Don't buy the title back.
2. **Don't build the pharmacy-benefit or mail arm as a standalone business.** It sits on the payer side of the table and can cannibalize store visits.
3. **Don't accept third-party contracts below the minimum profitability standard** for the sake of volume.
4. **Don't let "3,000 stores in five years" decide sites.** The arithmetic doesn't close: 215 openings minus about 88 closures is about 127 net a year, which reaches roughly 2,700, not 3,000 `[ASSUMPTION: closure rate stays near last year's]`. The pressure to close that gap is exactly where weak sites get approved.
5. **Don't roll out the mini-store format** until per-visit data shows it works. Six years and about two dozen units is a test, not a proof.

**Non-negotiable disciplines:** every new store is freestanding with a drive-through where possible. No debt-financed growth. Exits happen on stated deadlines. Every store connects to the refill-anywhere system.
**Freedom within the framework:** regional teams choose sites and assortment freely, inside the per-visit hurdle and the convenience format.
