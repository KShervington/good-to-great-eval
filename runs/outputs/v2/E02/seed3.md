# Ravenhill Drug Company: Good to Great (Stewardship Mode)

## Verdict

**Ravenhill is already great.** It has 21 straight years of record sales and earnings, a 19.1% ROE, almost no debt, and a long record of exiting non-core businesses. The job now is to protect what works, not to reinvent it.

- **Hedgehog Concept:** *The most convenient pharmacy-led drugstore on the corner, measured by profit per customer visit.*
- **Weakest flywheel link:** prescription visits keep rising (43.4% of sales) while third-party payers squeeze profit per prescription. Gross margin fell from 28.4% to 28.0%.
- **Temptations to refuse:**
  1. A large acquisition made to "keep pace" with consolidators.
  2. Growing pharmacy-benefit and mail service into an engine that pulls prescriptions out of the stores.
  3. Payer contracts below the profitability floor.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** a rival merger would create a chain with about 4,500 stores and more than $11B in sales. It would pass Ravenhill on sales and gain leverage over payers.
- **Missing numbers that could change this:** profit per visit, same-store sales, store returns by format, mail and benefit-subsidiary profitability, and shareholder returns relative to the market.
- **Confidence:** moderate-high. It rests on a long earnings record, not on unit economics.

Greatness potential: 76/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Ravenhill stays what it already is, and gets more so. It is the drugstore a customer reaches with the least friction: freestanding corner sites, drive-through windows, and a prescription record that follows the customer to any store. It is funded entirely by its own earnings and grows by building its own sites, one disciplined opening at a time. It measures itself by profit per customer visit, not by store count or sales rank. It answers payer pressure and industry consolidation with a floor on contract profitability and with convenience competitors can't easily copy. It does not answer them with a transformational deal. Whoever follows the current chairman can run it unchanged, because the Hedgehog Concept, the exit discipline and the metrics are written down and built into the organization.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence (working hypothesis):** *Ravenhill is the most convenient pharmacy-led drugstore in every neighborhood it serves, and it judges everything by profit per customer visit.*

| Circle | Candidates considered | Narrowed to |
|---|---|---|
| **Best in the world at** | Pharmacy services; general retail; low price; real estate; pharmacy technology; benefit management | **Convenience of pharmacy access at scale**: corner sites, drive-through, any-store refills over a chainwide network. It is **not** best-in-world at discount department stores, restaurants, optical, foreign retail, or (as far as the evidence shows) mail-order and benefit management. It has already exited the first four. |
| **Economic engine** | Profit per store; per prescription; per square foot; per customer visit | **Profit per customer visit** `[ASSUMPTION]`. The dossier doesn't report it. Prescriptions bring the visit, and front-store items (photo, general merchandise) add margin to it. This explains the moves to corner sites, drive-throughs and one-hour photo. |
| **Passion** | Pharmacy heritage; family stewardship; operational technology | **The pharmacy as a neighborhood institution.** The company was founded by a pharmacist, has three generations of family leadership, and has spent two decades narrowing back to the drugstore `[ASSUMPTION: passion inferred from behavior, not stated]`. |

**Denominator:** profit per customer visit `[ASSUMPTION]`.

**Tests**
- *Does it explain past wins?* Yes. Each exit (restaurants, optical, discount department stores, Mexican retail) removed something unrelated to convenient pharmacy visits. Each recent investment (corner sites, drive-throughs, satellite refills, scanning, photo) increases visits or the profit made on each one.
- *Does it rule things out?* Yes. It rules out size-for-size's-sake acquisitions, low-margin payer volume, and mail-order growth that turns store visits into shipments.
- *Is it uncomfortable to commit to?* Yes. It means accepting that a rival may pass Ravenhill on sales and store count without Ravenhill buying its way back to number one.

**Evidence that would confirm it:** (a) profit per visit is higher at freestanding and drive-through stores than at strip and mall stores; (b) front-store spending per prescription visit is stable or rising; (c) profit per visit holds steady or grows while prescription mix rises.

## 3. The flywheel

```
 ┌─► 1. Convenient sites (freestanding corners, drive-through, own real estate)
 │        │
 │        ▼
 │   2. More customer visits, anchored by prescriptions (refill at any store)
 │        │
 │        ▼
 │   3. Front-store spending per visit (photo, general merchandise)  ◄── WEAKEST LINK:
 │        │                                                            payer pressure on
 │        ▼                                                            profit per prescription
 │   4. PROFIT PER CUSTOMER VISIT (denominator)
 │        │
 │        ▼
 │   5. Record earnings, self-funded capital spending (~$310M/yr, no debt)
 │        │
 │        ▼
 │   6. Technology and new and remodeled stores (workflow system, photo, scanning)
 └────────┘
```

The loop closes: earnings fund sites and technology, which drive visits.

**Current push (next one or two turns):**
1. **Protect link 3→4.** Hold a floor under profit per prescription with payers, and lift front-store spending on prescription visits. One-hour photo in more than 400 stores is the right kind of push.
2. **Turn link 1 on purpose.** Keep opening freestanding drive-through stores at the planned pace, but only where site economics meet the bar. Don't let the 3,000-store number set the pace.

**Doom-loop check:** no current signs. Ravenhill has made few acquisitions (24 stores in 5 years), it has stuck with one direction for two decades, and its exits were deliberate. **Latent triggers:** (a) pressure to answer the consolidation wave with a big deal; (b) the 21-year record streak itself, which can push management toward moves that keep the streak alive instead of moves that make the business stronger.

## 5. Stop-doing list (temptations to refuse)

Stewardship mode lists only the real temptations.

1. **Don't buy a large chain to "keep pace" with consolidators or to win back sales rank.** Acquire only individual stores or small clusters that meet freestanding and convenience criteria and add to profit per visit. Ravenhill's history (largest deal ever: 66 stores) is a strength, not a gap to close.
2. **Don't grow mail service or the benefit network as standalone growth engines.** Keep them at the size needed to stay in payer networks. Where they steer prescriptions away from store visits, they work against the Hedgehog Concept `[ASSUMPTION: their profitability is unknown]`.
3. **Don't sign payer contracts below the minimum profitability standard** for the sake of volume. Walk away and accept lower prescription count before lower profit per prescription.
4. **Don't let the 3,000-store target or the record-earnings streak dictate pace.** Missing a round number is acceptable. Opening weak sites is not.
5. **Don't let the mini-store stay a permanent test.** Make the call.
