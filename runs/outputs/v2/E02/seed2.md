# Ravenhill Drug Company: The Great Version

**Verdict: already great. Stewardship mode.** Ravenhill has had 21 straight record years, a 19.1% ROE, no meaningful debt, and two decades of disciplined exits.

- **Hedgehog:** The most convenient pharmacy-led drugstore in every neighborhood it enters, earning more profit per customer visit than anyone else.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** profit per visit. Payers are cutting prescription margins just as prescriptions grow to 43.4% of sales.
- **Temptations to refuse:** (1) a large chain acquisition to match the rival's ~4,500 stores; (2) growing mail service and the benefit network into a business that pulls prescriptions out of stores; (3) payer contracts below the profit floor.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** gross margin is already slipping (28.4% → 28.0%) under the payer squeeze.
- **Numbers that would change this:** profit per visit, same-store sales, freestanding vs. strip-store returns, shareholder returns vs. the market.
- **Confidence:** moderate-high that it is great today. The profit-per-visit denominator is an unmeasured hypothesis.

`Greatness potential: 72/100`

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Ravenhill stays the drugstore people use because it is the easiest one to reach. It sits on a corner site with a drive-through, and any store can fill your prescription from anywhere in the network. Each customer visit earns more than a visit to any rival, because the pharmacy brings people in often and the front store and photo counter turn those trips into baskets. It funds its own growth, stays debt-free, and adds stores mostly by building them in markets where density makes it more convenient still. It deals with payers from strength: it is a network that plans need, not a volume taker. When rivals consolidate, it doesn't follow. It keeps raising profit per visit while competitors work through integration. The discipline it showed in leaving restaurants, optical and department stores now goes into refusing the deals and side businesses that would dilute the corner drugstore.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   ┌─► 1. Prime corner sites + drive-through + refill-anywhere network
   │              │
   │              ▼
   │   2. More frequent visits (Rx anchor + photo + front store)
   │              │
   │              ▼
   │   3. Higher PROFIT PER CUSTOMER VISIT   ◄── weakest link (payer squeeze)
   │              │
   │              ▼
   │   4. Self-funded cash flow, no debt, 19% ROE
   │              │
   │              ▼
   │   5. 200+ organic stores/yr + tech (workflow system, scanning)
   │              │
   │              ▼
   └── 6. Market density → more convenience AND leverage with payers
```

**Weakest link:** step 3. Prescriptions are the visit anchor, but third-party payers keep cutting their margin. Sales can keep growing while profit per visit erodes underneath.

**Current push (next one or two turns):**
1. **Measure step 3.** Track profit per visit by store type and age, so the flywheel is steered by its denominator and not by store count.
2. **Strengthen step 6.** Favor in-fill density in existing markets over new-metro entry. Use density, plus the pharmacy workflow system's cost per prescription, to hold the payer profit floor.

**Doom-loop check:**
- **Current state:** no doom-loop signs today. The company has spent two decades on consistent organic growth and finished its exits on schedule.
- **Pressure point 1, the rival's ~4,500-store deal.** It invites a reactive acquisition.
- **Pressure point 2, store-count arithmetic.** The plan is 215 openings against ~88 closures, about +127 net a year. That reaches roughly 2,700 stores in five years, not 3,000 `[ASSUMPTION: closures stay near last year's level]`. Closing the gap would tempt the company to buy stores or keep weak ones open. The 3,000 target should be treated as an outcome, not a goal.

## 5. Stop-doing list (temptations to refuse)

These are written in stewardship mode: real temptations only.

1. **Don't buy a large chain to keep up with consolidation.** The largest deal in company history was 66 stores. Acquired strip-center fleets would dilute profit per visit.
2. **Don't build mail service or the benefit network into a business that pulls prescriptions out of stores.**
3. **Don't sign third-party contracts below the minimum-profitability standard** to protect volume.
4. **Don't let the 3,000-store target lower site hurdles or slow the closing of weak stores.**
5. **Don't chase discounters and supermarkets on price.** Compete on convenience.
6. **Don't re-diversify** into restaurants, general retail, optical or foreign markets.
