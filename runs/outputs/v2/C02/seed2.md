# Good to Great: Meridian Surgical Corporation

## Verdict

**Meridian Surgical: good, not yet great.** It holds a founder-built monopoly whose endurance is unproven.

- **Hedgehog (working hypothesis):** Meridian is best in the world at converting surgeons from sutures to mechanical stapling, by designing the instruments and teaching the hands, and it earns on every cartridge an active stapling surgeon uses.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** turning training into a lasting surgeon habit. Instruments have sat unused before, and that cost the company about $1M.
- **Stop doing:** (1) paying reps for instruments placed instead of cartridges used; (2) side products that don't ride the surgeon-training engine; (3) counting sales into distributors as success before surgeons use them.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** the monopoly is a head start, not a moat. Every staple displaces a suture, and suture makers already own the surgeon relationship `[ASSUMPTION]`.
- **Missing numbers that would change this:** recent margins and cash, patent expiry dates, and cartridge reorders per placed instrument.
- **Confidence:** low to moderate. The verdict rests on a dossier with no recent financials.

Greatness potential: 30/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Meridian is the company that made mechanical stapling the normal way to join tissue in abdominal and thoracic surgery. It got there by out-teaching every competitor, not by holding a patent. Its decisive asset is a clinical training engine: medically trained reps, a surgeon advisory board, residency programs and OR-staff instruction. That engine turns placed instruments into daily habits. Its economics run on one number, profit per actively stapling surgeon, and every rep, product and channel is judged against it. When the big suture makers arrive, as they will, they find a generation of surgeons who learned to staple on Meridian instruments, accounts that reorder cartridges on autopilot, and a company whose numbers are trusted because nobody inside it inflates them. It no longer depends on its founder. The founder built a clock: a clinical function, an independent finance function, a training academy and a successor bench.

3. **Uncomfortable.** Changing rep pay from placement to usage will hurt short-term reported sales. If it doesn't hurt, the concept isn't biting.
4. **Engine connects.** Profit per active surgeon should be positive and rising. This is unknown today.

**Current push (next one or two turns):**
1. **Make link 2→3 measurable.** Track active surgeons and cartridge pull-through per placed instrument, account by account. Until this exists, the company can't tell a sale from a habit.
2. **Push link 6→1.** Formalize the two teaching-hospital champions into a residency training program. Surgeons who learn on staplers become the cheapest conversions there are, and the hardest for a competitor to undo.

**Doom-loop check:**
- *Three sales models in about 10 years* (nurses, then army medics, then trained salespeople). Read as disciplined iteration toward what works, not thrashing. Each change kept the core idea of clinical teaching. **Not a doom loop**, but the next change must be driven by data.
- *Other instruments built "to spread marketing costs."* This is the main doom-loop seed: growth by adding products rather than by turning the wheel harder. **Watch.**
- *Commission-only, fire-fast culture.* This risks reaction without understanding: dismissing reps without asking whether territory, training or the product was the cause. **Watch.**
- No acquisitions or big-bang programs are visible. **Pass.**

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop paying reps on instruments placed.** Blend pay with a usage component (cartridge reorders per account) and phase out pure commission on placement.
2. **Stop developing or promoting non-stapling instruments that don't share the teaching engine.** "Spreads marketing cost" is not a sufficient reason to make a product.
3. **Stop counting sales into distributors or hospitals as success before sell-through.** Report surgeon usage alongside shipments. Never stretch a quarter by loading the channel.
4. **Stop letting distributors own clinical training or the surgeon relationship.**
5. **Stop firing trained reps without an autopsy.** Each one costs about $8,000 to train. Ask whether the problem was the territory, the training or the person before deciding.
6. **Stop letting product and clinical decisions rest on the founder alone.** Run them through the Surgical Advisory Board.
7. **Stop measuring success by sales growth alone.** The headline number becomes profit per active stapling surgeon.
8. **Don't enter sutures.** That means competing on the incumbents' ground.

**Truth climate:** this is a risk. In a high-pressure culture with commission-only pay and fast dismissals, people learn not to report bad news. **Charisma check:** a founder who personally invented and rescued the company can silence dissent without meaning to. **Red-flag mechanisms to install:** a monthly usage-vs-shipments report that reaches the CEO unfiltered; blameless reviews of every lost account and every rep departure; and a controller who reports to the board.

**Faith statement:** *In a trained surgeon's hands, stapling saves blood and time. Whoever teaches the most surgeons wins, and Meridian has a decade's head start in teaching.*

## 7. Core ideology and the BHAG

**Core purpose:** Make surgery faster, safer and less bloody by giving surgeons better ways to join tissue.

**Core values:**
1. **Patient outcomes first.** We never place an instrument that won't be used well.
2. **Teach before we sell.**
3. **Truth in the numbers.** We report what surgeons actually use, not what we shipped.
4. **Ingenuity with tissue.** We keep improving the mechanics.

**BHAG (20-year):** *Mechanical stapling becomes the standard way to close tissue in abdominal and thoracic surgery, and every surgical residency teaches it on Meridian instruments.*

**Clock-building mechanisms:** a training academy with a codified curriculum; a Surgical Advisory Board; usage-based rep pay; an independent finance function reporting to the board; a quality and regulatory function; a named successor bench of two or more internal candidates.

### Gate 10 detail: How the Mighty Fall

The two mechanisms most likely to bring Meridian down are detailed in the register above: denial of a competitive and patent cliff, and a pressure culture that turns into pursuing reported numbers at the expense of truthful ones. The remaining stages:

- **Hubris:** a 13-year solo founder who created a category is a classic setup.
- **Grasping for salvation:** if entry happens, the temptation will be a big acquisition or a pivot into sutures. Refuse it and double down on training.
- **Capitulation:** this would happen if cartridge pricing collapses and no new procedures are added.
