# Good to Great: Meridian Surgical Corporation

## One-screen verdict

**Good, not yet great. Run the plan as disciplined growth.**

- **Hedgehog Concept:** Be the best in the world at turning suture surgeons into habitual stapling surgeons, and earn on every stapled procedure.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** the step from training to habitual use. That step is carried by a commission-only sales force that is turned over fast.
- **Top three stop-doing items:**
  1. Counting instrument placements as wins.
  2. Paying reps on sales instead of cartridge use.
  3. Leaving accounts that depend on surgeon adoption with distributors who don't train.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** nobody can currently show whether Meridian makes money per procedure. The dossier has no recent earnings, margins or patent dates.
- **Missing numbers that would change this answer:** profit per stapled procedure, cartridges used per placed instrument, patent expiry dates, rep turnover.
- **Confidence:** low to moderate. It rests on the 13-year history and one documented failure (staplers bought but not used), not on current financials.

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Meridian is the company that made stapled closure the normal way to close tissue in the procedures where stapling beats sutures. It got there one surgeon and one operating room at a time.

- **What it sells:** not instruments, but proficiency. Its medically trained representatives stay with a surgeon until stapling is habit, and the company tracks cartridge use per surgeon the way an airline tracks load factor.
- **How it earns:** profit per stapled procedure is the number it lives by. That profit pays for more trainers, better clinical evidence, and stapler designs for the next procedure category.
- **Who runs it:** a clinical leader sits at the top table, and a successor bench exists.
- **How it treats its people:** sales talent is developed rather than burned.
- **How it treats competitors:** when large suture makers arrive, Meridian doesn't win on price or catalog breadth. It wins because surgeons trust its people and its data, and because switching away would mean retraining.
- **How long it lasts:** it outlives its founder.

## 3. The flywheel

```
 ┌─► 1. Clinically credible training of surgeons + OR staff
 │        (reps with medical training, animal-lab proficiency)
 │                         │
 │                         ▼
 │   2. Surgeon uses stapling routinely in more procedures
 │                         │
 │                         ▼
 │   3. Cartridge consumption ↑ → PROFIT PER STAPLED PROCEDURE ↑
 │                         │
 │                         ▼
 │   4. Profit funds retained reps, training capacity,
 │      clinical evidence, next-procedure stapler designs
 │                         │
 │                         ▼
 │   5. Peer surgeons endorse; evidence + teaching hospitals
 │      make stapling the expected technique
 │                         │
 └─────────────────────────┘  (more surgeons willing to be trained)
```

**Weakest link: step 1 to step 2.** Training turns into habit only if the same credible rep stays with the surgeon long enough. The current setup works against that in two ways:
- Commission-only pay plus fast dismissal means short rep tenure `[ASSUMPTION]`. Each departure throws away about $8,000 of training and breaks the surgeon relationship.
- Commission tied to sales rewards placing instruments, not getting them used. That is the same failure that caused the loss year.

**Current push (next one or two turns):**
1. Re-point rep pay and metrics at cartridge use per account.
2. Measure use by surgeon and by procedure so the flywheel can be seen at all.

**Doom-loop check:**
- The company went through three training models in about five years: nurses, then army medics, then trained salespeople. Each change responded to a specific measured failure (cost, then selling ability), and the current model has held for about five years. That reads as **learning, not doom loop**.
- Residual doom-loop signals: the high-pressure culture and the hire-and-fire cycle create churn that looks like momentum but resets the flywheel's people component every time.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop counting instrument placements as success.** An instrument nobody uses is a cost. The loss year proved it.
2. **Stop paying reps purely on commission from sales.** Pay on cartridge use per account, with a guaranteed ramp period.
3. **Stop the fast hire-and-fire cycle for new reps.** At about $8,000 of training per head, churn destroys capital and surgeon trust. Judge after a defined ramp, not on early sales.
4. **Stop selling adoption-critical staplers through distributors who don't train surgeons.**
5. **Stop adding non-stapling instruments to spread marketing cost.** That made sense in the first three years. Now it pulls trainers' attention away from the flywheel.
6. **Stop running the business without procedure-level economics.** No major investment decision should be made until profit per stapled procedure is known.
7. **Stop competing on price with suture makers.** Compete on outcomes and proficiency.

**Non-negotiable disciplines (fanatical consistency):**
- No placement without a training and usage plan for that account.
- Every rep is certified in the animal lab before seeing surgeons.
- Monthly review of cartridge use per active surgeon.

**Freedom within a framework:**
- **Framework:** stapled tissue closure, sold through trained clinical relationships.
- **Freedom:** reps and regional leads choose how to win their accounts, which surgeons to target and how to sequence training.
- **Bureaucracy check:** fast dismissal works as a crude substitute for selecting the right people. Fix the hiring profile, and the firing reflex becomes unnecessary.

## 7. Core ideology and the BHAG

**Core purpose:** Make surgery safer and faster by giving surgeons better ways to join tissue.

**Core values `[ASSUMPTION]`, to be confirmed with the founder:**
1. Proficiency before sales. A surgeon who isn't confident isn't a customer yet.
2. Clinical credibility. Every claim is backed by evidence or by the animal lab.
3. Relentless mechanical improvement.
4. Ownership of the outcome in the operating room.

**BHAG (20-year horizon):** Stapled closure becomes the standard of care in every major procedure where it outperforms sutures, worldwide.

**Clock-building mechanisms:**
- A rep training academy with certification standards.
- A surgeon advisory board.
- Usage-based metrics and pay.
- A succession plan for CEO and the top five roles.
- A pipeline process for new procedure applications.
