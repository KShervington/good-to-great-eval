# Meridian Surgical Corporation: Good to Great

**Verdict: good, not yet great.** Meridian runs a working consumables monopoly. Its real moat is surgeon training, not the stapler itself.

- **Hedgehog:** Meridian is best in the world at moving operating teams from sutures to stapling and keeping them stapling. It earns on every procedure that uses its cartridges.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** training → active use. The loss year was caused by instruments that went unused. Commission-only pay may still reward placements over use `[ASSUMPTION]`.
- **Stop doing:**
  1. Paying reps on instrument placement.
  2. Placing instruments without a trained OR team.
  3. Building non-stapling instruments just to spread marketing cost.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** the monopoly is temporary. Patent runway and the large suture makers' plans are unknown.
- **Missing numbers that would change this:** patent expiry dates, cartridges per placed instrument, current margins and cash, rep retention.
- **Confidence:** low to moderate. It rests on the company's history and business model, not on current financials.

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Meridian has made stapling the standard way surgeons close internal tissue in abdominal and thoracic surgery.

It wins on two things:
- **The surgeon.** It owns the training path that turns a suturing surgeon into a stapling one. That path starts in residency, runs through animal-lab and in-OR coaching, and continues with procedure-specific support.
- **The cartridge.** Each new instrument opens another procedure to stapling.

It manages by one number: profit per stapled procedure, not instruments shipped. Its reps are educators who are paid when surgeons keep using the product. Its distributors handle logistics, not the relationship. Its organization would keep running if the founder stepped away tomorrow.

When a large suture maker enters, and one eventually will `[ASSUMPTION]`, Meridian still holds the trained surgeons, the residency programs and the clinical know-how. Those are much harder to copy than a stapler.

**Does it explain past wins?** Yes, all three:
- Adoption began only after two teaching-hospital surgeons endorsed the device.
- The nurse-instructor model worked clinically.
- The current model works because reps get 240 hours of medical training plus 40 hours in the animal lab.

**Does it rule things out?** Yes. It rules out non-stapling instrument lines, selling instruments without training, and letting distributors own surgeons.

**Is it uncomfortable to commit to?** Yes. It means paying for education that does not book revenue right away, and changing a sales-pay plan that currently "works."

**Confirmation tests.** This is a working hypothesis. It is confirmed if:
- (a) cartridge reorders per trained surgeon are clearly higher than per untrained account;
- (b) profit per stapled procedure is positive and rising;
- (c) surgeons trained in residency adopt faster than surgeons converted mid-career.

## 3. The flywheel

```
 ┌──► 1. Train surgeons & OR teams (residency, animal lab, in-OR coaching)
 │              │
 │              ▼
 │      2. Active stapling surgeons (reorder cartridges)   ◄── WEAKEST LINK
 │              │
 │              ▼
 │      3. Stapled procedures → PROFIT PER STAPLED PROCEDURE
 │              │
 │              ▼
 │      4. Profit funds more trained reps + procedure-specific instruments
 │              │
 │              ▼
 │      5. Teaching-hospital results & residents trained on staplers
 │              │
 └──────────────┘   (surgeons and hospitals ask to be trained)
```

**The current push** has two parts:
1. **Measure and pay for link 2.** Track active users per account, and tie part of rep pay to cartridge pull-through rather than placements.
2. **Strengthen link 5.** Get stapling into residency programs at teaching hospitals, so new surgeons arrive already trained.

**Doom-loop check: watch, but no fail.**
- *Warning signs.* The company used three sales models in about five years: nurses, then army medics, then trained salespeople. It also diversified into other instruments to spread costs, and it fires poor performers fast.
- *Mitigating factor.* The current model has held for about five years, alongside rapid growth.
- *Main risk.* A competitive shock could trigger another reactive reinvention.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop paying reps purely on instrument placement** `[ASSUMPTION: commission basis includes instruments]`. Pay on cartridge pull-through or active surgeons instead.
2. **Stop placing instruments in hospitals that have no trained surgeon and OR team.** No instrument goes out without training.
3. **Stop developing non-stapling instruments** whose only rationale is spreading marketing cost.
4. **Stop letting independent distributors sell or train.** They ship; Meridian teaches.
5. **Stop treating trained reps as disposable.** Measure ramp time against the $8,000 training cost before deciding to let someone go.
6. **Stop counting instruments sold as a headline growth metric.** Report stapled procedures and active surgeons.
7. **Stop treating skin staples as a co-equal growth bet** `[ASSUMPTION]`. They need less training, so they are where the moat is weakest. Run them for margin, not as the main strategy.

**Core values:**
1. Teach before we sell.

3. Patient outcome over transaction.
4. Own the result, not the task.

**BHAG (about 20 years):** stapling is how every residency program in the country teaches internal tissue closure for major abdominal and thoracic surgery, and most of those surgeons learned it on Meridian instruments.

**Clock-building mechanisms:**
- A certified training curriculum that runs without the founder.
- A rep academy with ramp metrics.
- A product-development process with surgeon advisory input.
- A named successor and a working #2.
