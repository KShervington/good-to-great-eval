## Verdict

**Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** Halvorsen-Pike can be the best in the world at turning cellulose fiber into branded, everyday paper-based consumer products that people ask for by name. It should measure itself by **profit per consumer brand**.

**Weakest flywheel link:** product superiority. The diaper leaks, and the company lacks expertise in synthetic nonwovens.

**Top three stop-doing items:**
1. Spreading capital across every business by tradition.
2. Funding printing-paper mills just to stay at cost parity. Prepare to sell them.
3. The air-charter "profit center."

**Most urgent brutal fact:** the company cannot fund a fight with Rival P and mill modernization at the same time, and the mills are its identity.

**Missing numbers that would change this:** segment return on capital through a full cycle, brand-level market shares, and category growth rates.

**Confidence:** moderate-low. The verdict rests on the dossier's qualitative facts. There are no segment economics.

Greatness potential: 33/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

In its great version, Halvorsen-Pike is a consumer products company that happens to know paper better than anyone. It is no longer a mill company that also sells tissue. Its capital goes where profit per consumer brand is highest. Its leadership team mixes world-class consumer marketers with manufacturing people who now run tissue and absorbent-product plants. Its R&D combines its depth in cellulose with synthetic nonwovens. It launches nothing nationally until the product beats the category leader in blind tests. It has left commodity printing papers, specialty papers and aviation services, and it handled those exits fairly for the towns and people involved. Its facial-tissue brand is the anchor, and its absorbent products compete on performance rather than on advertising weight alone. Over 15 years it outperforms the market instead of trailing it by a third.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence:** *Be the best in the world at turning cellulose fiber into branded, everyday paper-based consumer products that people trust by name, measured by profit per consumer brand.*

|---|---|---|---|

| **Passion** | Making things (manufacturing pride); the mill-town heritage; products that touch daily life | **Making the best paper products people use every day** `[ASSUMPTION: the mill-bred culture's pride in craft can transfer to consumer-product manufacturing]` | **Mill identity for its own sake.** This is the weakest circle (see Brutal Facts). |

**Confirmation tests:**
- **Does it explain past wins?** Yes. The company's one durable leadership position, facial tissue, came from inventing a branded fiber-based consumer product.
- **Does it rule things out?** Yes: printing papers, specialty papers, the air charter plan, and me-too launches.
- **Is it uncomfortable to commit to?** Very. It implies selling the mills, possibly including the namesake mill `[ASSUMPTION: the namesake mill produces printing papers or newsprint]`.

**Evidence that would confirm it:**
- Segment ROC through a cycle shows consumer products beating printing papers.
- A redesigned diaper wins blind leakage tests against Rival P.
- Facial-tissue share holds through Rival P's push into toilet tissue.

**Current push (next two turns):**
1. **Turn 6:** decide on selling the mills, using segment ROC, and pledge the proceeds to the consumer flywheel rather than to acquisitions.
2. **Turn 2:** fix or pull the leaking diaper, and build nonwoven capability so the next diaper beats Rival P's on performance.

**Doom-loop check:** several markers are present today.
- Capital is spread across all businesses by tradition.
- Twenty years of acquisitions in specialty papers to manufacture growth.
- A big diaper launch with no sustained product improvement afterward.
- A finance-originated diversification idea (air charter) appearing from nowhere.

The mill sale itself could look like a "big dramatic program." What separates it from one is that it is a single reallocation driven by the Hedgehog Concept, followed by years of steady pushes, with no acquisition spree funded by the proceeds.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. Allocating capital across all businesses by tradition, with the mills first.
2. Modernizing printing-paper mills to stay at cost parity in a commodity cycle.
3. Turning the air fleet into a charter and maintenance business.
4. Holding or acquiring specialty, envelope and cigarette paper businesses.
5. Shipping or advertising a diaper with known leakage problems. Fix it or pull it.
6. Launching consumer products that only match Rival P. Require a blind-test performance win before any national rollout.
7. Filling consumer-business leadership roles from mill ranks by default.
8. Chasing Rival P into every category it enters. Fight only where fiber-based performance can win. For example, don't follow the tampon rival into tampons unless the product is demonstrably superior.

**Baseline (Gate 1):**

- **Definition of great:**
  1. **Superior performance:** sustained total returns above the market.
