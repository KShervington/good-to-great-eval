## Verdict: Halvard is already great, so protect it

Halvard has fourteen straight years of growth. Its share of orchestral players rose from 9% to 31%, operating margin has stayed at 24–29%, and return on invested capital has averaged about 34%. This run uses **stewardship mode**.

- **Hedgehog:** *Halvard makes the violin-family strings that professional players choose in blind tests, and sells them only through the specialist shops that serve those players.*
- **Weakest flywheel link:** supply through the specialist shops. Independent shops in North America fell about 3% last year, and cello and bass lead times reached five weeks last autumn.
- **Temptations to refuse:** (1) Halvard Six through mass retail; (2) the private-equity group's student brand; (3) any sale below minimum advertised price or on general marketplaces.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** Halvard Six goes back into the channel Halvard left 14 years ago. It would earn about half the gross margin and run on winding capacity that has already slipped.
- **Missing numbers that could change this:** share by instrument, winding-machine utilization, and revenue by region.
- **Confidence:** high on direction, because it rests on a 14-year audited record. Moderate on where future growth comes from.

`Greatness potential: 78/100`

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

The great version of Halvard is mostly the Halvard that exists today, with its supply secured and its succession completed. It stays the string that serious violin, viola, cello and bass players choose when they can't see the label, because the Listening Room keeps a real veto that outlasts any one CEO. It grows by winning more of the instruments it already serves. Cellists are at 44%; the other instruments are at or below the 31% average, and that gap is the runway. It sells through a specialist-shop network that it actively helps survive consolidation. It holds price discipline and accepts single-digit growth at high returns instead of buying growth with lower-margin volume. The decision that made Halvard great was leaving mass-market strings. Staying great mostly means not undoing that decision.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

| **Economic engine** | Profit per set; profit per shop; profit per professional player | **Profit per professional or advanced player who keeps a Halvard string on the instrument.** This combines the 15–25% price premium, 78% three-year retention, and a minimum advertised price that is never broken. | Volume-driven unit economics |
| **Passion** | Acoustics; serving serious players; growth | **Sound, as judged by the people who play for a living** `[ASSUMPTION: inferred from the CEO's background, the panel culture, and her crediting players]` | Market-share-for-its-own-sake |

**Denominator:** profit per professional or advanced player retained.

**Tests of the concept:**
- **Does it explain past wins?** Yes. Exiting student and guitar strings 14 years ago (22% of revenue) came before gross margin rose from 44% to 61% and operating margin from 9.8% to 27.9%.
- **Does it rule things out?** Yes: Halvard Six and the student brand.
- **Is it uncomfortable to commit to?** Yes. It means turning down a projected $19M of revenue.

**Status:** this is a working hypothesis. It is confirmed if share by instrument keeps rising and operating margin stays in the 24–29% band without any growth from outside the specialist channel.

**On the CEO's question (should the Listening Room test guitar prototypes?):** no. The panel is 40 violin-family professionals. Their verdict on guitar strings would carry none of the authority that makes the panel valuable. Building a guitarist panel instead would be the first step in founding a second company. The useful version of her instinct is this: the board answers *whether* Halvard enters a new category by testing it against the Hedgehog. The panel answers *whether a string is good enough* only inside the category where its judgment counts.

## 5. Stop-doing list (temptations to refuse)

Stewardship mode lists only the real temptations, with no minimum count.

1. **Halvard Six as proposed.** It means mass retail, about 31% gross margin, a second shift on core winding machines, and a category where Halvard has no player-validated edge. The 2.3× corrosion result is a lab measure of durability. It is not evidence that guitarists would prefer the string.
2. **The private-equity student brand at about 1.1× revenue.** It re-enters the segment Halvard exited, under another company's brand logic. Ask yourselves why a consolidator wants to sell it.
3. **General online marketplaces, or any sale below minimum advertised price.** This includes "just one" promotion to defend share as shops consolidate.
4. **Committing winding capacity to anything outside the core** before core lead times are reliably two weeks or less.
