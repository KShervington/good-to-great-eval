# Tolliver Company: Good to Great

## Verdict

**Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** Tolliver will be the best operator of electronics-and-appliance superstores in a few dense regional clusters, earning more profit per region from each store it adds.

Greatness potential: 35/100

- **Weakest flywheel link:** profit per region. No profit figures exist for any format, so the economics are unproven.
- **Top three stop-doing items:**
  1. No more acquisitions bought for tax credits or market entry.
  2. No new mid-sized stores; growth capital goes to superstores only.
  3. No single-store entries into markets where Tolliver can't build a cluster.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** Tolliver runs about 100 outlets across four formats and the evidence doesn't show which one makes money. Meanwhile Rival S, funded by a steel company, enters markets several stores at a time.
- **Missing numbers that would change this answer:** net income and ROE by format, debt load, and superstore payback.
- **Confidence:** low to medium. The verdict rests on sales growth, the superstore being the only format the company keeps expanding, and the CEO's habit of asking questions. It does not rest on profit data, because there is none in the evidence.

---

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

The great Tolliver is a one-format company. It runs large electronics-and-appliance superstores, and it enters each region with enough stores to share advertising, a warehouse and a service center. Within a region it adds stores only when doing so raises profit per region. It wins on selection, price, and dependable delivery and service. It pays for each new region mostly from the cash earned in the last one, sheltered for a time by the acquired tax losses. It has walked away from the leased departments, from the acquired big-city chain unless that chain can be converted to the cluster model, and from deal-making as a growth habit. Its managers can tell you profit per region for every region, every month. It keeps outperforming because it does one repeatable thing in more places, not because it keeps reinventing itself.

**Tests:**
- **Does it explain past wins?** Partly. The company recovered from the loss year after it exited sideline businesses and bet on the superstore.
- **Does it rule things out?** Yes: three of the four current operations.
- **Is it uncomfortable to commit to?** Yes. It means slowing growth in outlet count and possibly retreating from the newest acquisition.

**Confirmation tests (12 months):**
1. Superstore four-wall return beats mid-sized stores.
2. Profit per region rises as stores are added to a cluster.
3. Superstores hold their share where Rival S enters.

If test 1 fails, this Hedgehog Concept is wrong. In that case, go back to Gate 5 with the mid-sized format as the candidate.

## 3. The flywheel

```
 ┌─► 1. Enter a region with a planned CLUSTER of superstores
 │        │
 │        ▼
 │   2. Share advertising, warehouse, delivery & service across the cluster
 │        │  (lower cost per store)
 │        ▼
 │   3. Wider selection + lower prices + reliable delivery/service
 │        │
 │        ▼
 │   4. More traffic, higher volume per store, rising regional share
 │        │
 │        ▼
 │   5. ★ Higher PROFIT PER REGION + better supplier buying power
 │        │
 │        ▼
 └── 6. Internally funded next store / next region (tax losses shelter early profits)
```

**Current push (next one or two turns):**
1. Measure link 5. Produce real profit by format and by region.
2. Fill in the neighboring-state region where four superstores opened last year before opening anywhere new.

**Doom-loop check: warning signs are present.**
- Four formats are running side by side.
- The company bought a struggling chain partly for tax reasons, which is a jump-start acquisition.
- It has a long history of entering and then exiting businesses (tires, leased departments, mail order, audio stores, the Midwest).
- Outlets doubled in seven years without a stated choice of route, pace or region.

The loop closes on paper. It does not yet close on evidence.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop buying chains for financial reasons** (tax credits) or to enter markets. Grow by building clusters.
2. **Stop opening mid-sized stores.** Every new capital dollar goes to superstores inside a cluster plan.
3. **Stop single-store entries** into markets where a cluster can't follow within about two years `[ASSUMPTION on window]`.
4. **Stop putting growth capital into the big-city chain.** Decide fix-or-exit within 12 months. Get tax counsel on whether the $36.5M of loss credits depends on keeping those stores open `[ASSUMPTION: it may not]`.
5. **Stop renewing the leased-department arrangement.**
6. **Never re-enter abandoned sidelines:** tires, housewares, mail order, small audio stores.
7. **Stop matching Rival S ad-for-ad outside Tolliver clusters.** Defend density and don't chase coverage.

**Non-negotiable disciplines:**
- A monthly P&L by format and region.
- Every new store justified against a written regional cluster plan.
- In-stock rates, delivery and service measured relentlessly in every store.

**Framework:**
- Headquarters chooses the regions, the format and the capital.
- Store and regional managers have autonomy on staffing, local promotion and service recovery.

**Bureaucracy check:** unknown. Look for rules that exist only because the acquired chain's managers can't be trusted.
