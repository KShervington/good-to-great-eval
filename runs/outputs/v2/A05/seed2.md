## Verdict

**Weakest flywheel link:** whether superstore clusters earn more per region than the other formats. Nobody has proved it.

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

The great Tolliver is a one-format company. It runs large electronics-and-appliance superstores in dense clusters, region by region, and enters a region only when it can become the market leader there. Each cluster shares advertising, a distribution center, and delivery and repair operations, so every new store in a region is cheaper to run than the one before. Customers choose it because it has the widest selection at competitive prices and trained staff who help them buy expensive goods, and because Tolliver delivers, installs and repairs what it sells. Profit from established regions pays for the next region at a pace the balance sheet can carry. Leased departments, the stray mid-sized stores, the bankrupt big-city chain and the habit of buying growth are gone or have been converted. The CEO's habit of asking questions has become a company-wide decision discipline that outlasts the family.

**Sentence:** *Tolliver will be the dominant big-ticket electronics-and-appliance superstore in each region it enters, and it will grow profit per region.*

**Tests:**
- *Does it explain past wins?* Partly. The 11% share in the capital area, and leadership's continued investment in superstores, both point here. The loss-year exits (tires, most leased departments, mail order, audio stores) all moved away from things outside this concept.
- *Does it rule things out?* Yes: three of the four current operations, plus acquisitive growth.
- *Is it uncomfortable to commit to?* Yes. It means walking away from roughly 100 outlets' worth of mixed formats and growing more slowly than an acquisition would allow.

**Confirmation evidence:**
- Superstore operating profit per store and per region beats mid-sized stores at equal capital.
- Superstore payback is short enough to fund self-financed rollout.
- Regional share rises when stores are clustered.

If superstores earn less than mid-sized stores, loop back to this gate.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   ┌──► 1. Open superstores in clusters, one region at a time
   │              │
   │              ▼
   │    2. Regional scale: shared ads, distribution, delivery & repair
   │              │
   │              ▼
   │    3. Lower cost per sale → wider selection, sharp prices, full service
   │              │
   │              ▼
   │    4. More traffic & local share; trained staff convert big-ticket sales
   │              │
   │              ▼
   │    5. Higher PROFIT PER REGION
   │              │
   │              ▼
   └─── 6. Self-funded capital + a refined playbook for the next region
```

**Current push (the next one or two turns):**
1. Build format-level and region-level P&Ls. Then make the neighboring-state superstore cluster a proof region by filling it to density, not adding a new region.
2. Convert or close the stand-alone mid-sized stores in the capital area so the 11% share sits on superstore economics.

**Doom-loop check:** Several warning signs are present.
- Four formats are running at once.
- Last year's acquisition was a jump-start bought partly for tax reasons.
- The company has a long history of entering and exiting businesses: tires, mail order, audio stores, the Midwest.

There are counter-signs. Leadership pruned with discipline after the loss year and stayed with the superstore bet. Overall, the company sits at the fork between the two loops.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop buying chains** for tax credits, turnaround value or instant store count.
2. **Stop growing four formats.** No new mid-sized stores except as fill-ins inside a superstore cluster.
3. **Stop single-store entries** into new markets. Enter a region only with a funded plan to reach cluster density.
4. **Exit the West Coast leased department** on an orderly timetable.
5. **Stop putting new capital into the acquired big-city chain.** Run it to a fix-or-exit deadline (12 months `[ASSUMPTION]`), and structure any exit so the tax-loss credits stay usable (get tax counsel to confirm this).
6. **Stop competing on price alone** against Rival S. Compete on price combined with service.
7. **Stop drifting into non-big-ticket merchandise** that dilutes the superstore's purpose.

**Non-negotiable disciplines:**
- Every store reports its P&L monthly, by format and by region.
- No region opens without a density plan.
- Any capital commitment above a threshold `[ASSUMPTION: about 10% of equity]` needs a board debate with a written dissent.

**Framework boundaries:**
- Head office sets the format, the region choices and the capital.
- Store managers have autonomy over staffing, local promotions and service recovery, within that frame.

**Bureaucracy check:** No evidence is available. Watch for rules that exist only to police weak acquired-chain management.
