## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Keystone no longer bets the company on where interest rates go. It earns its living by pricing and managing mortgage risk better than anyone else. It buys loans and either funds them with matching maturities or hedges them. It guarantees securities only at fees that cover measured credit losses. It treats its near-government borrowing cost as a privilege to protect, not a licence to grow. In every market, including credit squeezes, lenders rely on it as the buyer that never disappears. Lower- and moderate-income borrowers get mortgages through it because it knows their risk well enough to price it, not because it ignores that risk. Profit tracks risk taken, not assets held. Its regulator and auditors see everything. Its scale gives it the best loan-level data in the market, and that data makes its pricing better every year.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence (working hypothesis):** *Keystone will be the best capital-markets manager of residential mortgage risk, measured by profit per unit of mortgage risk, in service of opening home ownership to households the market underserves.*

| Circle | Candidates considered | Kept | Ruled out |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Best in the world at** | Holding mortgages for spread; buying volume; mortgage securitization; managing rate and credit risk through capital markets; multifamily lending | **Funding, hedging, securitizing and guaranteeing residential mortgage risk at scale.** Its funding cost near the government's, its scale and its charter make this defensible. | **Holding unhedged fixed-rate loans for spread** (this is what broke it). **Origination** (the charter forbids it, and lenders are its customers). **Multifamily** (not yet: no loss history). |
| **Economic engine (denominator)** | Profit per loan; profit per dollar of assets; profit per dollar guaranteed; **profit per unit of mortgage risk** | **Profit per unit of mortgage risk** (rate risk plus credit risk, measured against the capital that backs it). | Profit per dollar of assets, which rewards growth and leverage, the exact trap. |
| **Passion** | Housing affordability; financial engineering; public service | **Closing the gap between households that can and cannot afford a home.** The CEO has carried this through three careers. Staff passion is an `[ASSUMPTION]`. | Growth for its own sake. |

**Confirmation tests**
- *Does it explain past wins?* Yes. Keystone's best moments came from capital-markets access, when it was the largest buyer in credit squeezes. Its worst came from the hold-and-fund-short spread book.
- *Does it rule things out?* Yes. It rules out new unmatched fixed-rate holdings, chasing volume, unpriced guarantees and fast multifamily growth.
- *Is it uncomfortable?* Yes. It means a smaller balance sheet, slower reported growth, and spending on risk systems before volume.
- *What would confirm it in 12–24 months?* New business carries a positive risk-adjusted return. Market-value net worth becomes less sensitive to rate shocks. Guarantee fees cover observed losses.

**Weakest link:** step 1. Keystone cannot yet state its market-value net worth or its loss rates by loan type, so step 2's pricing is partly guesswork.

**Current push (next 1–2 turns):** (a) build rate-shock and market-value net worth measurement, then use it to set funding policy for the legacy book; (b) set guarantee fees from loan-level credit data and start collecting that data on every swap-program pool.

**Doom-loop check:** There are early signs, but it is not a doom loop yet. In three years Keystone has made four moves: the $10B sale, ARM purchases, MBS/swap issuance and multifamily lending. The first three connect directly to the Hedgehog. Multifamily is the least connected, so hold it at pilot size. There are no acquisitions or big-bang programs. The danger is momentum-by-new-product instead of momentum-by-discipline.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Funding long fixed-rate mortgages with short-term notes.** No new fixed-rate holdings unless they are match-funded or hedged.
2. **Measuring success by purchase volume, market share or portfolio size.** Measure profit per unit of risk instead.
3. **Guaranteeing securities at fees not grounded in loan-level loss data.**
4. **Growing multifamily beyond a capped pilot** until it has a loss history by property type `[ASSUMPTION: pilot cap set by board]`.
5. **Rationing data to the regulator and the congressional audit office.** Disclose risk metrics fully.
6. **Treating mission purchases as unpriced charity.** Price their risk and fund them openly.
7. **Treating the Treasury backstop as a substitute for capital.**
8. **Lobbying for powers outside the residential secondary market.**
