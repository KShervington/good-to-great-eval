## 1. The great version in one paragraph

The great Calder no longer counts itself as an adding-machine company or as an also-ran in the computer race. It is the company banks think of first when they need to move and record money. Its magnetic inks, checks and bank forms, check sorters, electronic bookkeeping machines and its easy-to-operate large computers all sell to the same customer and work together as one system. They come through one sales relationship, and a bank's ordinary clerks can run them without a room full of programmers. Most revenue recurs through rentals, consumables and service. Margins are back in double digits and stay there, because R&D goes only to problems that financial institutions will pay to have solved. The international organization carries the same system to banks abroad. The company does not depend on the current chairman or the forceful new executive vice-president. It runs on line-level profit discipline, a board that decides, and a bench that has been developed on purpose.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence (working hypothesis):** *Calder is the best in the world at automating how banks and other financial institutions process and record money, and it measures success by profit per institution served.*

| **Passion** | Leading-edge computing; diversification and growth; making the books balance for people who handle money | **Making financial records accurate and effortless for the people who keep them.** The company has served accountants and bookkeepers for 75 years `[ASSUMPTION about the culture]` | "Growth through diversification" is a strategy that management likes, not a passion that people share |

**Confirmation tests:**
- **Explains past wins?** Yes. The accounting machines sold to bookkeepers. The bank automation sold early. The acquired forms and check printers sell to banks.
- **Rules things out?** Yes. It rules out the general computer race, military as a growth pillar, and adding-machine reinvestment.
- **Uncomfortable?** Yes. It means openly conceding the general computer market to Rival I and going head-to-head with Rival N for banks.
- **Evidence that would confirm it, within 6–12 months:** banking-related lines show higher profit and better retention than the rest of the company once the line P&L exists. Large-computer placements in banks outperform placements elsewhere. The bank-automation win rate against Rival N and Rival I is at or above parity.

## 3. The flywheel

```
  (1) Decades of trust with banks & bookkeepers
          + international sales organization
                     │
                     ▼
  (2) Sell an integrated check-to-ledger system
      (ink · forms · sorters · bookkeeping machines · computer)
                     │
                     ▼
  (3) Clerks run it without programmers →
      installs stick; rentals, forms & service recur
                     │
                     ▼
  (4) PROFIT PER FINANCIAL INSTITUTION rises   ◄── denominator
                     │
                     ▼    ◄── WEAKEST LINK today
  (5) Profit funds R&D aimed ONLY at bank problems
                     │
                     ▼
  (6) Better bank-specific products + reputation
      as THE bank-automation company ──────────► back to (1)
```

**Weakest link: from (4) to (5).** Right now, profit (about 2% of sales) is too thin to fund anything. R&D is spread across the whole computer market, and nobody has line-level profit data to show whether banking actually earns more.

**Current push (next one or two turns):**
1. Make link 4 visible by building a line-level P&L and measuring profit per institution.
2. Redirect computer sales and R&D toward financial institutions, so link 5 is spent on the flywheel and not on the general race against Rival I.

**Doom-loop check: warning signs are present.**
- Two chief executives in a row have chosen new directions ("diversify away from adding machines").
- Growth came through acquisitions: the computer maker and the forms printers.
- Management has promised a salvation date without giving one ("profits recover once costs are written off").
- A major strategic decision, the computer partnership, lapsed because the board never acted.

The loop above closes logically. Whether it turns depends on stopping these patterns.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop competing across the whole general-purpose computer market.** Sell computers only into financial and accounting-intensive applications.
2. **Stop acquiring or launching equipment lines** that don't serve the financial-institution customer.
3. **Stop investing in standalone adding machines.** Harvest them for cash.
4. **Stop growing military computers as a separate strategic bet.** Make a run-for-cash or sell decision on data by day 90.
5. **Stop funding R&D programs without a line P&L and a hedgehog test.** Every program must answer: "does this raise profit per institution?"
6. **Stop promising investors a recovery without a timeline.** Replace the promise with dated, line-level margin targets.
7. **Stop letting strategic decisions lapse.** Board decisions get a set deadline and a recorded yes or no.

**Non-negotiable disciplines:**
- A monthly P&L by line and by customer type.
- A written account plan for every major bank customer.
- The hedgehog test applied to every product decision.
- Dissent is recorded before the EVP decides.

**Framework boundaries (freedom within a framework):**
- Divisions choose *how* to win banks: pricing structure, product configuration, country tactics.
- The framework fixes *who* the customer is, *what* gets measured (profit per institution) and *what* gets funded (bank problems only).

**Bureaucracy check:**
- There is no evidence on existing rules. The risk runs the other way: a dominant operating chief can centralize decisions that should sit with capable division heads.
- If heavy approval layers grow up, ask whether they exist because the wrong people are in seats.
