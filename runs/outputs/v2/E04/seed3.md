# Northfield-Brandt Corporation: Good to Great

## Verdict

**Good, close to great. The core move was right; finish the narrowing, then hand it on.**

- **Hedgehog:** Northfield-Brandt invents and leads everyday paper-based personal-care categories that people buy by brand.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** reinvestment. Rival P outspends it on R&D and advertising, while capital leaks into the airline, the commodity mills and open-ended European plants.
- **Top three stop-doing items:**
  1. The $120M airline expansion. Exit aviation while it is profitable.
  2. Newsprint and printing papers. Sell them.
  3. European plant spending ahead of evidence. Stage-gate it on returns.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** a larger, better-funded rival leads the core diaper market, and the company is still splitting capital across three unrelated businesses.
- **Numbers that would change this:** profit and return on capital by segment and by brand, airline profit, European returns, current facial-tissue share.
- **Confidence:** moderate. It rests on category-creation evidence and 20 years of consistent strategy. Segment profits are unseen.

Greatness potential: 68/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Northfield-Brandt is a focused branded personal-care company. Every dollar of capital and every senior hour goes to creating and leading categories: tissue, diapers, training pants, feminine care and adult incontinence. In each of these it holds the #1 brand, or is visibly on the way there. It no longer runs an airline or commodity mills. The cash from those exits funds R&D and brand advertising on a scale that lets it beat Rival P category by category, even though it cannot outspend Rival P overall. Europe gets capital in steps that are earned by share. The company manages profit per consumer brand as its primary number. Above all, it has passed control from a 20-year chief executive to a successor who keeps pushing the same flywheel, with no new grand direction. That handover is the test of greatness it has not yet passed.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   ┌──► 1. Category-creating product
   │      (better absorbency, new use: training pants, adult care)
   │                 ▼
   │    2. #1 brand in that category (share leadership)
   │                 ▼
   │    3. Shelf power + manufacturing scale
   │                 ▼
   │    4. Higher PROFIT PER CONSUMER BRAND  ◄── denominator
   │                 ▼
   └──  5. Reinvest in R&D + brand advertising
          (topped up by cash from exiting mills and aviation)
```

**Current push (next 1–2 turns):**
1. Put the freed capital into component 5, aimed at the diaper and training-pants fight, where Rival P's overall lead is narrowing.
2. Extend the loop into Europe only in categories where component 2, local leadership, is plausible.

**Weakest link:** the step from component 4 to component 5. Rival P's deeper budgets mean reinvestment is the constraint, and the airline expansion and mill reinvestment compete for that same money.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop the $120M airline expansion.** Fulfil only binding commitments and exit aviation (airline plus maintenance services) while it is profitable.
2. **Stop reinvesting in newsprint and printing-paper mills.** Sell them.
3. **Stop treating tobacco and technical papers as growth platforms.** Give them no growth capital and divest on a value timeline.
4. **Stop committing European plant capital ahead of share evidence.** Release each tranche against category-share and return milestones.
5. **Stop launching premium-priced novelties with narrow everyday use.** The anti-viral tissue lost money for three years.
6. **Stop trying to match Rival P's total advertising across every category.** Concentrate spending where the company can lead.
7. **Stop judging units by "is it profitable?" alone.** Ask "is it inside the Hedgehog?"

## Appendix: gate artifacts

**Gate 0, subject card.**
- **Name:** Northfield-Brandt Corporation
- **Type:** Business (public consumer and paper products manufacturer)
- **Steward:** Chairman & CEO, with the board
- **Horizon:** decades
- **Stage:** mature, 20 years into a strategic pivot

**Gate 1, baseline sheet.**
- **Evidence of good:**
  - Net sales of $6.41B and net income of $432M, a net margin of about 6.7%.
  - ROE of about 19%.
  - Six straight years of positive returns, well ahead of the market.
  - Several #1 consumer positions.
- **Three tests of great:**
  - *Superior performance:* yes, over 6 years; a longer record is unknown.
  - *Distinctive impact:* yes. It created categories and leads diapers and incontinence.
  - *Endurance:* **not yet shown.** Everything has happened under one CEO.
- **State:** good, not yet great. The plan is deliberately short and focused on finishing the narrowing; it does not invent a new strategy.

**Gate 2, leadership read.**
- **Strengths:**
  - Extraordinary resolve: he sold the mills and timberland within a year and took on the market leader.
  - Reserved, not a celebrity, so the charisma risk is low.
- **Gaps:**
  - Succession started late.
  - The airline grew on his watch; window-and-mirror evidence is not in the dossier.
- **One behavior change:** personally lead the aviation and paper exits as his final act, and announce a handover date.

**Gate 4, truth climate.**
- **Strengths:** the open "questions under discussion" suggest real debate.
- **Risk:** a "very demanding" CEO may mute bad news `[ASSUMPTION]`.
- **Red-flag mechanisms to add:**
  - A monthly brand P&L with share alerts.
  - Blameless post-mortems on dropped products. The anti-viral tissue is the template case.

**Gate 6, framework.**
- **The framework:** capital goes only to personal-care paper categories, measured by profit per brand.
- **Autonomy inside it:** category teams have freedom in product, marketing and launch timing.
- **Bureaucracy check:** the "every unit a profit center" rule mostly polices non-core businesses. It can be retired after the exits.
- **Fanatical consistency:** every product must earn the repeat purchase.

**Gate 9, mechanisms (clocks, not time-telling).**
- A brand P&L reviewed monthly by the board.
- A capital rule: no spending outside personal care without a board-level exception.
- A category-innovation pipeline with launch gates.
- A standing succession committee.
- Post-mortems on every dropped product.

These are disciplined hypotheses drawn from a retrospective framework, not guarantees. Some companies that met Collins's tests later declined, which is why Gate 10's warnings matter.
