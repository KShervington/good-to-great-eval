# Good to Great: Northfield-Brandt Corporation

## Verdict

**Good, and nearly great in its consumer core. Not yet great as a whole company.** The 20-year move from mills to brands is mostly done. What remains is the hardest part: finishing the exit from what doesn't fit, and making the result outlast its architect.

- **Hedgehog (working hypothesis):** Invent and lead the personal-care categories where absorbent-product innovation beats a bigger rival's spending.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** reinvestment. Capital leaks to an airline and commodity mills while Rival P outspends on research and advertising.
- **Top 3 stop-doing:** (1) the uncommitted part of the $120M airline expansion; (2) reinvesting in newsprint and printing-paper mills; (3) European plant spending without share and return gates.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** the architect is 64, his likely successor has been president for two months, and the unfinished portfolio decisions are about to change hands.
- **Missing numbers that would change this:** profit and return on capital by segment (airline and tobacco papers especially), current category shares, European returns.
- **Confidence:** moderate on direction, which rests on 20 years of consistent moves and six years of beating the market. Low on economics, because there is no segment data.

Greatness potential: 66/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

The great version of Northfield-Brandt is a single-minded personal-care company. It creates categories and then leads them: tissue, diapers and training pants, feminine care, adult incontinence. Every dollar of capital goes either to absorbent-product innovation or to the brands and plants that carry it. The airline and the commodity paper mills have gone to owners who can make them great on their own terms. Their proceeds have gone back into the consumer flywheel, so the company can outlast Rival P's larger budgets rather than try to outspend them. A board-run succession hands the company to leaders who already own its biggest decisions. Europe is built plant by plant, with each one proving share and returns before the next is funded. The company then keeps beating the market under a CEO other than the one who started the transformation. That third test, endurance, is the one it has not yet passed.

## 3. The flywheel

```
 ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
 ▼                                                          │
[1] Absorbent-materials & product-design innovation          │
 │   (patents, consumer insight)                              │
 ▼                                                          │

 │   (facial tissue, waistband, training pants)               │
 ▼                                                          │

 │   (#1 single diaper brand, #1 retail adult incontinence)   │
 ▼                                                          │
[4] PROFIT PER CATEGORY-LEADING BRAND  ← denominator          │
 │   (premium price + scale economics)                        │
 ▼                                                          │
[5] Reinvest ONLY in R&D, brand, IP defense,                 │
     and plants for leadable categories (incl. Europe) ───────┘

 One-time push, not a loop component:
 proceeds from selling the airline and commodity mills → [5]
```

**Weakest link: [5] Reinvestment.** Capital currently splits three ways: a $120M airline expansion, commodity mills that need regular reinvestment, and the consumer business. Rival P's larger budgets mean Northfield-Brandt cannot afford that split.

**Current push (next 1–2 turns):**
1. Redirect capital from the airline and mills into [5].
2. Put European capacity through a stage gate, so each plant serves a category the company can realistically lead.

**Doom-loop check:**
- **Mostly flywheel.** Twenty years of one consistent direction. The dropped anti-viral tissue shows real discipline.
- **Doom-loop trace: the airline.** A finance proposal turned a cost center into a profit center. That led to scheduled service, a fatal crash, heavy losses and a recovery, and now a $120M expansion. This is momentum in an unrelated business, not a turn of this flywheel.
- **Watch item: Europe.** Heavy investment began three years ago and its returns are unknown. It must not become the "big program to jump-start growth" pattern.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop the uncommitted part of the $120M airline expansion.** Honor contracts already signed and run the airline well while it is prepared for sale or spin-off.
2. **Stop reinvesting in newsprint and printing-paper mills** beyond safety and maintenance while they are sold.
3. **Stop adding European capacity ahead of evidence.** Fund the next tranche only when the last one meets its share and return gates.
4. **Stop launching premium niche products that can't lead a category.** The anti-viral tissue is the precedent.
5. **Stop trying to match Rival P's advertising dollar for dollar.** Win with being first and with patents, not with spending.
6. **Stop turning internal functions into profit centers.** That is the pattern that produced the airline.
7. **Stop making portfolio decisions without segment-level profit and return on capital.**
