**Verdict: Good, not yet great. Marlowe is a drugstore company carrying four other businesses.**

- **Hedgehog (working hypothesis):** Be America's best neighborhood drugstore, with pharmacy, health and beauty close to home, and judge every store and dollar by *profit per customer visit* `[ASSUMPTION: denominator]`.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** profit per visit. Net margin fell from 2.2% seven years ago to about 1.5% underlying `[ASSUMPTION, from management's claim]`, and 0.8% reported.
- **Top three stop-doing items:** exit the 25 discount department stores; cancel the 22 planned restaurant units and exit free-standing restaurants; retire the goal of "growth across all divisions."
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** sales rose 2.3× in nine years, but EPS barely moved: $1.95 seven years ago, $2.11 two years ago, $1.15 last year.

- **Confidence:** moderate-low. It rests on five years of consolidated results and management's own statements.

`Greatness potential: 30/100`

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Marlowe is one business, not five. It is a dense national network of neighborhood drugstores, with the pharmacy at the center and health, beauty and everyday needs around it. Its own laboratory supplies house brands that lift the margin on every visit. Capital that now goes to discount stores, family restaurants, a travel agency, and coffee and ice cream plants instead goes into well-sited drugstores, especially in the fast-growing Sun Belt, where regional rivals are building their footholds today. Success is measured by rising profit per customer visit, not by passing $1 billion in sales. Earnings per share compound faster than sales. The company is run by mechanisms that would hold up beyond the founding family: store-level P&L, capital rules, and an independent board that actually sees the numbers.

1. In store-level data, does profit per visit separate the best stores from the 51 that were closed better than profit per store or per square foot does? If profit per store separates them better, switch the denominator.
2. Do drugstores earn a higher return on capital than restaurants and discount stores? If restaurants turn out to be a high-return business, reopen Circle 1 for in-store food.
3. Does house-brand share of sales correlate with profit per visit? If not, the manufacturing arm is a cost, not an edge.

- **Weakest link:** step 4. The margin trend runs the wrong way, and no per-visit data exists to prove the link.
- **Current push (next one or two turns):**
  1. Build the measurement: store-level P&L and visits for all 616 stores.
  2. Shift capital out of discount stores and new restaurants into Sun Belt drugstore sites, before regional rivals lock up the best corners.
- **Doom-loop check: signs are present.**
  - "Growth across all divisions."
  - Sales doubling while EPS stays flat.
  - Acquisitions of a travel agency and of 29 chain stores, bought for size.
  - A new family-restaurant chain.
  - Heavy store churn: 94 opened and 51 closed in two years.

  Together these look like motion bought with capital rather than momentum.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop operating discount department stores.** Divest all 25 in an orderly sale.
2. **Stop building free-standing family restaurants.** Cancel the 22 planned units pending review, and divest the chain.
3. **Stop pursuing "growth across all divisions."** Replace it with one goal: rising profit per customer visit.
4. **Stop celebrating sales milestones** such as $1 billion. Report EPS and per-visit profit first.
5. **Stop buying chains for size.** Acquire only clusters that clear the per-visit hurdle. Re-test the 29 Southeastern stores against it.
6. **Stop opening stores without a payback threshold.** The 51 closures in two years mean siting is too loose.
7. **Sell the travel agency.** Wind down coffee and ice cream unless the retained counters need them.
8. **Stop adding vertical plants** (the plastic-container plant, the second lab) unless they beat buying on cost per unit.
9. **Stop funding the Mexico stake.**

**Non-negotiable disciplines:** every store has a monthly P&L; every capital request is ranked on profit per visit and payback; shrinkage is reported per store every month.

**Framework boundaries:** store managers get freedom over local assortment and service inside the per-visit metric. Headquarters owns siting, house-brand standards, and capital allocation.

**Bureaucracy check:** look for controls that exist because store managers can't be trusted with shrinkage. That is a people problem to fix with the right managers, not with more rules.
