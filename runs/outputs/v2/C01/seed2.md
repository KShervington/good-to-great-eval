# Wexley Medical Corporation: Good to Great

## One-screen verdict

**Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** Wexley makes practical, clinician-designed equipment that makes orthopedic and immobilized-patient care faster and cheaper. Specialists who are paid on results put it into clinicians' hands. The economic denominator is gross profit per field specialist.

**Weakest flywheel link:** getting products to clinicians. There is virtually no effective sales force.

**Top 3 stop-doing:**
1. Stop paying salespeople regardless of results.
2. Stop routing every decision through the founder and family.
3. Stop treating implants, or an IPO, as the answer to Rival H.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** the founder's ideas once drove about 90% of the line. Now there is no idea pipeline, no sales force and no budget to replace him.

**Missing numbers that would change this:** margin by product line (beds vs. instruments), the recent sales trend, and market share by line.

**Confidence:** moderate-low. The verdict rests on sales and structure only. Profitability is unknown.

Greatness potential: 38/100

---

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Wexley is no longer a founder's workshop with a bed business attached. It is a company run as a set of focused product units, each with its own budget, goals and profit accountability. Its engineers take problems directly from surgeons and nurses and turn them into devices that pass the founder's old test: make caregivers more efficient, or cut the cost of treatment. Commissioned specialist representatives who know the operating room and the ward put those devices in clinicians' hands. The surgical procedures that rivals' implants create become demand for Wexley's powered instruments rather than a threat to them. The company's growth comes from compounding gross profit per specialist, not from acquisitions or an IPO. Wexley stays great because the design rule, the budgeting discipline and the clinician feedback loop are built into the company. None of them depends on one person.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence:** *Clinician-designed devices that make orthopedic and patient-handling care faster and cheaper, placed by specialists paid on results.*

| Circle | Candidates considered | Selected (working) | What it rules out |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Best in the world at** | (a) Hospital beds generally; (b) orthopedic implants; (c) practical clinician-originated devices for orthopedic and immobilized-patient care; (d) powered orthopedic instruments | **(c), with (d) as the likely growth spearhead** | Implants and bone cement, where Rival H has a pharma parent and approvals 6 years ahead. Also generic hospital furniture competed for on price. |
| **Economic engine denominator** | Profit per hospital account; profit per product line; profit per employee; **gross profit per field specialist** | **Gross profit per field specialist** `[ASSUMPTION]` | Salaried generalist selling, and low-margin lines that no specialist can sell profitably. |
| **Passion** | Founder's design rule; clinician-originated invention; craftsmanship | **Solving the caregiver's practical problem** (the founder's rule) `[ASSUMPTION that staff still share it]` | Products that exist only because a competitor has one. |

**Tests:**
- *Explains past wins?* Yes. The turning frame, the cast saw and the bone saw all came from a surgeon's frustration, and the trade still uses the founder's name for the turning frame.
- *Rules things out?* Yes. It excludes implants, an undisciplined international build-out, and price-led bed competition.
- *Uncomfortable?* Yes. It may imply that beds, which are 70% of sales, are the cash base but not the future. It also forces a sales-pay reversal the company has already made once.

**Confirmation tests (6–12 months):**
1. Line-level margins show powered instruments and patient-handling specialties at or above the company average.
2. Commissioned specialist reps outperform the salaried baseline on gross profit per rep within two to three quarters.
3. At least two new product concepts come from non-founder clinician input in Year 1.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop paying salespeople regardless of results.** Move to commission or gross-profit-linked pay by specialty.
2. **Stop routing every decision through the founder and family.** Push decisions down to accountable managers with budgets.
3. **Stop running without a budget or goals.** "We'll see how the year goes" ends now.
4. **Stop eyeing implants and bone cement as a response to Rival H** for at least two years. Instead, sell the instruments those procedures require.
5. **Stop expanding overseas sales forces** before the domestic specialist model is proven `[ASSUMPTION: the subsidiary is sub-scale]`.
6. **Stop treating the IPO as a strategy.** It is a funding decision, to be made after two or more quarters of real budget data.
7. **Stop launching products that fail the founder's rule** or fail a margin hurdle, however elegant they are.
8. **Stop competing on price in commodity bed and stretcher segments** if line margins confirm they are commodity `[ASSUMPTION pending data]`.

**Truth climate / charisma check:** decades of centralized control by a revered founder very likely trained managers to wait for answers rather than surface problems `[ASSUMPTION]`. ## 7. Core ideology and the BHAG

- **Core purpose:** make caring for patients faster and less costly.
- **Core values:**
  1. Products start from a clinician's real problem.
  2. Every product earns its place on efficiency or cost.
  3. Managers own results, not tasks.
  4. Face the numbers.
- **BHAG (20-year):** Become the world's leading maker of powered orthopedic instruments and the name clinicians trust for patient handling.
- **Clock-building mechanisms:**
  - An annual budget with unit-level goals.
  - Product units with their own P&L.
  - A formal clinician-to-engineering idea channel.
  - A results-linked sales pay plan.
  - A written design rule as the product gate.
  - A succession bench for every senior seat.
