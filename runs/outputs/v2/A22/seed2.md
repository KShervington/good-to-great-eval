# Good to Great: Western Commonwealth Bank

**Verdict: good but slipping. Run this as a turnaround, not a growth plan.**

- **Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** Be the best-run consumer and middle-market bank in our home state, judged by risk-adjusted profit per employee rather than by size.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** the cost to serve. Overhead is reportedly the highest in the industry, and deregulation is ending cheap deposits at the same time.
- **Top three stop-doing items:** (1) treating asset growth as the goal; (2) new energy, shipping and developing-country lending beyond workouts; (3) new businesses, new states and acquisitions, including building out the brokerage.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** bad loans are appearing in four correlated books at once, just as the low-cost deposit cushion that used to absorb losses is being phased out.
- **Missing numbers that would change this answer:** net income and ROA for the last three years; non-performing loans by sector and country, compared with reserves and capital; profit by business line.
- **Confidence:** low to moderate. It rests on qualitative dossier facts with no earnings data.

`Greatness potential: 18/100`

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

The great Western Commonwealth is smaller in ambition and larger in profit. It is the home state's indispensable bank for households, farms and middle-market companies. It wins on cost per transaction and credit judgment, not on branch count or global reach. It applies its old instinct for innovation (the first successful bank card, early computerization) to driving down the cost of serving a customer. Deposits are priced to the market and loans are priced to risk. The bank knows its profit by line, by branch and by employee every month, and it refuses every opportunity that does not raise that number. Its foreign and specialty lending shrinks to what its home-state clients actually need. Measured by size, it ranks lower than it does today. Measured by risk-adjusted return, it ranks at the top.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

> **"We are the best-run consumer and middle-market bank in our home state, and we measure ourselves by risk-adjusted profit per employee, not by size."**

**Confirmation tests.** If the profit-by-line data does not confirm these, the Hedgehog Concept is wrong:
1. Home-state retail and middle-market lending earn a higher risk-adjusted return than corporate and international lending.
2. Branch-level data shows that technology can cut cost per transaction materially.
3. Home-state deposit and loan relationships hold up after market-rate deposit pricing (money-market deposit accounts) arrives.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   ┌─► 1. Lower cost per transaction
   │      (branch rationalization + ATMs/electronic banking + automation)
   │                     │
   │                     ▼
   │   2. Competitive deposit pricing & better service
   │      (can pay market rates and still earn a spread)
   │                     │
   │                     ▼
   │   3. More primary household & middle-market relationships
   │      in the home state (incl. home-state farms)
   │                     │
   │                     ▼
   │   4. Stable core deposits + fee income + loans
   │      underwritten on known local customers
   │                     │
   │                     ▼
   │   5. Higher risk-adjusted PROFIT PER EMPLOYEE
   │                     │
   └──── 6. Reinvest in technology & credit discipline ◄┘
```

**Current push (the next two turns):**
1. **Turn 1, cost.** Build branch-by-branch profitability. Consolidate the weakest branches and replace them with automated banking.
2. **Turn 6, credit discipline.** Run an independent review of the energy, shipping, agricultural and developing-country books, set reserves to reflect reality, and put risk-adjusted pricing on all new lending.

**Doom-loop check: warning signs are present.**
- The strategy has been "more assets, more branches, more countries, more lines."
- An acquisition (the discount brokerage) is under way while the core is under strain.
- The options on the table include "expand into new businesses and states to grow out of the problems." That is a jump-start-by-program move.
- A CEO transition at a turning point raises the risk of chasing one new direction after another.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop treating total assets, branch count or country count as targets.** Remove them from executive pay.
2. **Stop new energy, shipping and developing-country lending** beyond servicing and working out existing exposure.
3. **Stop acquisitions and entry into new states** for at least 12 months.
4. **Stop expanding the discount brokerage with bank capital or management attention.** It costs little in money (about $55M against roughly $113B in assets); the real cost is focus and the signal it sends.
5. **Stop writing long fixed-rate mortgages without matched funding** (or a hedge).
6. **Stop pricing deposits by tradition.** Reprice deliberately as rate ceilings phase out, product by product.
7. **Stop out-of-state and global agricultural lending growth.** Keep disciplined lending to home-state farms only.
8. **Stop opening branches** until branch-level profitability exists.
