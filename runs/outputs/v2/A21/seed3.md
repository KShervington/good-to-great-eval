# Harlan Pacific Corporation: Good to Great

**Verdict: once good, now sliding. Run this as a turnaround.**
Harlan Pacific is a century-old franchise whose earnings grew about 19% a year in the late 1970s. It is now losing ground with no strategy, just as deregulation ends cheap deposits.

- **Hedgehog (working hypothesis):** Run the bank as a business: the leanest, most credit-disciplined commercial bank in the West, measured by profit per employee.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** cost cuts must not drive away the deposit and middle-market customers who fund the loop.
- **Top three to stop:** (1) overseas expansion, foreign stakes and joint ventures; (2) matching Rival B branch for branch; (3) new deregulation side ventures.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** the cost base was built for regulated, cheap deposits, and the Latin American exposure has not been sized.
- **Numbers that would change this:** profit by business line, cost/revenue against peers, and Latin American loans as a % of equity.
- **Confidence:** moderate to low. It rests on the dossier's history and structure, with no financials.

Greatness potential: 55/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Five to fifteen years out, the great Harlan Pacific has stopped trying to be a smaller Rival B or a junior global bank. It is a western commercial bank run with near-fanatical cost and credit discipline.

- It lends where it knows the ground: home-state and western middle-market companies, real estate, and consumers. It does this through a branch network sized to be profitable rather than impressive.
- Every dollar of overhead has an owner. Every business line has a P&L. Every country exposure has a limit the board can see.
- As deregulation forces all banks to pay market rates for deposits, Harlan's lower cost per unit becomes its margin advantage. That margin funds disciplined growth in its home markets, including in-market acquisitions whose savings it knows how to capture.
- It earns more profit per employee than its large-bank peers and keeps doing so after the current CEO leaves.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   ┌─► 1. Cut overhead & run every line as a P&L
   │              │
   │              ▼
   │   2. Lower cost per unit than peers (cost/revenue ↓)
   │              │
   │              ▼
   │   3. Price deposits & loans competitively AND keep margin
   │      as rate ceilings disappear
   │              │
   │              ▼
   │   4. Higher PROFIT PER EMPLOYEE / ROA  (denominator)
   │              │
   │              ▼
   │   5. Capital to grow in home/western markets
   │      (middle market, real estate, in-market acquisitions
   │       whose costs Harlan knows how to strip)
   │              │
   │              ▼
   │   6. More volume on a lean, fixed base → scale economies
   └──────────────┘
```

**Current push (next 1–2 turns):** components 1 and 2. Build line-by-line profitability, and cut overhead starting at the top. Nothing downstream turns until the cost machine is real and measured.

**Weakest link:** the step from 2 to 3. If cuts hollow out service in branches or among middle-market relationship officers, deposits and customers leave just as competition for funds intensifies. Watch customer and deposit retention alongside costs.

**Doom-loop check:** the past 16 years show doom-loop signs:
- direction after direction (cards, branch build-out, Asia, Latin America, Europe);
- ventures used to jump-start growth (the German stake, the London JV);
- inconsistency (the "time to slow down" pronouncement, with no strategy to follow it).

The new risk is reacting to deregulation by launching ventures because peers are. Acquisitions belong in component 5 only *after* components 1–4 are turning. Used that way, they accelerate the flywheel instead of trying to jump-start it.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. Opening new overseas branches or representative offices.
2. Taking minority stakes in foreign banks, or entering foreign joint ventures.
3. Making new Latin American sovereign or cross-border loans until exposure is sized and limited.
4. Expanding the branch footprint to match Rival B. Close or consolidate branches that don't earn their cost.
5. Launching deregulation-era side ventures (new financial products or businesses) that don't serve the western lending and deposit core.
6. Paying up for deposits to defend market share at the expense of margin.
7. Large-corporate lending at thin spreads outside the West just to book assets `[ASSUMPTION: line profitability unknown]`.
8. Executive perks and overhead symbols that signal "big bank" rather than "lean bank" `[ASSUMPTION: none documented]`. Start with the CEO's office.
