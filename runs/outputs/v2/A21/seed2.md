## 1. The great version in one paragraph

In its great form, Harlan Pacific has stopped trying to be a smaller version of everyone else: a global bank, a statewide consumer giant and a deregulation conglomerate all at once. It is the most efficient large bank in its region. It runs on fewer people, fewer and better branches, and automated back offices. It lends to home-state households, middle-market companies and real estate borrowers it understands, with credit discipline that holds through cycles. It pays market rates for deposits and still earns more per employee than larger rivals, because its overhead is lower rather than because its pricing is better. Its size is a result of this approach, not a goal.

**Intersection tests:**
- **Does it explain past wins?** Mostly. The real estate unit fits it.
- **Do the losses fit it?** Yes. Every loss in the record came from outside the home market (Germany, London, currency moves, Latin America) or from weak controls (the embezzlement).
- **Does it rule things out?** Yes: most of the international network, the branch race and new ventures.
- **Is it uncomfortable to commit to?** Yes. It means giving up the "11th-largest, international" identity.

**What would confirm it:** profit by business line shows that home-market lending and core deposits earn above the bank's average while international earns below it. Profit per employee then rises for three years in a row without a rise in credit losses.

## 3. The flywheel

```
 ┌──► 1. Cut every cost not serving home-market customers
 │            │
 │            ▼
 │     2. Lower cost per $ of loans and deposits
 │            │
 │            ▼
 │     3. Price deposits at market and lend selectively,
 │        only to known home-market borrowers
 │            │
 │            ▼
 │     4. Low credit losses, steady earnings
 │            │
 │            ▼
 │     5. Rising PROFIT PER EMPLOYEE and capital
 │            │
 │            ▼
 └──── 6. Reinvest in automation and focused in-market growth
```

**Current push (next two turns):** Turn 1 is cost: cut executive overhead, rank branches by profit, and freeze international expansion. Turn 2 is measurement: profitability by business line and by office, so that step 3 decisions rest on facts.

**Weakest link:** step 3. Lower cost only creates value if the lending stays disciplined. The CEO's background makes real estate the easy place to put freed-up capital, and that is where concentration risk builds.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. Open no new overseas branches or representative offices. Wind down any that don't serve home-market customers.
2. Take no minority stakes in foreign banks and form no joint ventures abroad. Exit the German stake.
3. Stop competing with Rival B on branch count. Close or consolidate branches that lose money.
4. Turn down the new financial ventures that deregulation opens up unless they raise profit per employee in the home market.
5. Stop large-corporate lending where the bank is a price-taker with no relationship edge. `[ASSUMPTION]`: confirm with line-profitability data.
6. Stop treating asset size and "11th-largest" rank as goals.
7. Cut executive perks and overhead before cutting frontline service. `[ASSUMPTION]`: perks exist.
8. Stop growing Latin American loan exposure.

## Appendix: gate detail

**Gate 0: Fit (PASS).**
- **Name:** Harlan Pacific Corporation
- **Type:** business, a public bank holding company
- **Steward:** the new chairman and CEO
- **Time horizon:** decades
- **Current stage:** a mature, century-old institution in decline, at a leadership transition

**Gate 1: Baseline (CONDITIONAL).**
- **State:** good, not yet great, run as a turnaround.
- **Evidence of good:** more than a century of operation; 11th-largest US bank; about 19% average earnings growth over six years in the 1970s; a statewide franchise; a profitable real estate unit.
- **Definition of great:** sustained profitability above large-bank peers; a hole in the regional market if it disappeared; endurance through credit cycles and CEO successions.
- **Condition for PASS:** the Latin American exposure must not threaten solvency. If it does, Gate 4 contradicts this gate and the baseline has to be redone.

**Gate 2: Level 5 Leadership (CONDITIONAL).**
- **Strength:** built a profitable unit; a long insider who knows the bank.
- **Gap:** his window-and-mirror behavior is unknown, and he was COO through the slide.
- **Behavior change:** publicly own the bank's past mistakes, including his share, and appoint a credit chief empowered to overrule him on real estate.

**Gate 4: Truth climate.**
- Hold blameless post-mortems on the German stake, the London joint venture and the embezzlement.
- Report monthly exposure data straight to the board.
- **Charisma check:** unknown. The abrupt exit of the previous CEO is a reason to test whether bad news travelled upward under him.

**Gate 6: Discipline (PASS).**
- **Framework:** the home market, known borrowers and the profit-per-employee test.
- **Freedom inside it:** units choose how to hit the denominator.
- **Bureaucracy check:** don't add layers to make up for weak controls. Fix the controls people instead.
- **Relentless consistency:** an annual budget review that starts from cost.

**Gate 7: Technology triage (PASS).**

| Technology | Classification |

**Gate 8: Doom-loop check.** The prior era shows the classic signs: new directions one after another (cards, branches, international, joint ventures), acquisition-style shortcuts (the German stake), and no consistent strategy. The plan's defense is to push only the six-step loop for at least three years.

**Gate 9: Built to Last (PASS).**
- **Clock-building mechanisms:** profit-per-employee reporting; an independent credit committee; a board exposure dashboard; a successor bench developed from home-market line heads.

**Gate 10: Remaining stages.**

- **Undisciplined pursuit of more:** the pull toward deregulation ventures or a big acquisition.
- **Denial of risk:** watch Latin American and real estate concentration.

- **Capitulation:** unlikely while the franchise is still profitable.
