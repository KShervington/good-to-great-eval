# Harlan Pacific Corporation: Good to Great

## Verdict

**A good franchise in decline. Run this as a turnaround, and narrow hard.**

- **Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** Be the best-run, lowest-cost bank in the West. Earn more profit per employee than any peer by serving home-region customers in the lines we know, and do nothing else.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** turning lower costs into *more* home-region customers. The cuts must not hollow out the franchise, and Rival B will always have more branches.
- **Top three stop-doing items:** (1) overseas offices, foreign equity stakes and new foreign lending; (2) the branch-count race with Rival B; (3) new ventures opened up by deregulation that sit outside the core.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** the caps on deposit rates are being phased out. A cost base built on cheap deposits is now too expensive to carry.
- **Missing numbers that could change this:** profit by business line, Latin American exposure against equity, the efficiency ratio, and profit per employee.
- **Confidence:** moderate on direction, low on size. The verdict rests on the dossier's history and has no financial statements behind it.

Greatness potential: 40/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Harlan Pacific is a focused regional bank. It does not try to beat money-center banks abroad or outspend Rival B on branches. It runs consumer banking, middle-market lending and real estate lending in its home state and the West with a lower cost structure than anyone else. Every line, branch and office has to show what it earns per employee and per dollar of expense. The money freed by leaving international ventures and loss-making branches goes back into the home franchise through automation and disciplined in-market growth. The bank stays great because tight cost control, independent credit discipline and honest profit reporting are built into how it runs, and do not depend on one CEO's instincts.

**Confirmation tests:**
- *Does it explain past wins?* Yes. Real estate lending, a local-knowledge business, became a strong profit contributor. The 1970s growth came from the home franchise.
- *Does it explain past losses?* Yes. Every loss named in the dossier came from outside the home region or from weak controls.

- *Is it uncomfortable?* Yes. It means closing offices, cutting headcount and shrinking parts of the bank.

**Evidence that would confirm it:** profit by line showing that in-region lines earn above their cost of capital while international lines earn below it, and cost cuts that do not lose deposits or loan share.

## 3. The flywheel

```
 (1) Exit non-hedgehog lines; cut overhead and unprofitable branches
              │
              ▼
 (2) Lower cost per dollar of loans and deposits (efficiency ratio falls)
              │
              ▼
 (3) Absorb rising deposit costs and still price competitively in home markets
              │
              ▼
 (4) Higher PROFIT PER EMPLOYEE and ROA
              │
              ▼
 (5) Capital to reinvest: automation and in-market growth
     (incl. acquisitions where costs can be stripped) [ASSUMPTION]
              │
              ▼
 (6) More home-region customers on the same cost base ──► back to (1)
```

**Current push:**
1. Build profit-by-line and profit-by-branch reporting.
2. Use it to make the first round of exits and cost cuts.

**Weakest link:** step 3 to step 6, whether lower costs actually win customers or just shrink the bank. This is the link to watch.

**Doom-loop check:** the past 16 years show classic doom-loop signs:
- direction after direction (card consortium, national charter, branch expansion, overseas ventures, then "time to slow down");
- ventures meant to restart growth;
- an abrupt leadership exit.

What to watch now: new ventures pursued just because deregulation allows them, and a big acquisition treated as a rescue.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. Stop maintaining or growing overseas branches, representative offices and foreign equity stakes. Keep only trade services that home-region clients need `[ASSUMPTION]`.
2. Stop making new Latin American and other foreign sovereign or cross-border loans.
3. Stop competing with Rival B on branch count. Consolidate or close branches that fail the profit-per-branch test.
4. Stop entering ventures because deregulation allows them. Each must pass the Hedgehog test first.
5. Stop chasing national and multinational corporate loans where the bank has no pricing or knowledge edge `[ASSUMPTION]`.
6. Stop approving any budget where expenses grow faster than revenue.
7. Stop allocating capital to lines whose profit nobody can measure.
