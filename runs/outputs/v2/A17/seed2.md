# Good to Great: Stanwick Mailing Systems

**Verdict: good, not yet great. Narrow the company; don't diversify it.**

- **Hedgehog (working):** Stanwick can be best in the world at running the business mailroom: leasing, financing and servicing the equipment that companies send their mail through. Success is measured as profit per business customer.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** turning the field force's recurring visits into more mailroom equipment and financing per customer. Revenue per customer has never been measured.
- **Top three stop-doing:** (1) retail electronics and point-of-sale; (2) fighting the copier giant head-on; (3) treating meters as a cash cow that funds diversification.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** Stanwick just lost $42M (about 11% of revenue) outside its core, and profit by business line isn't visible.
- **Numbers that would change this:** return on capital by line, copier profitability, customer count and revenue per customer, the new CEO's record.
- **Confidence:** moderate-low. The verdict rests on the 85–90% share and the recurring lease-and-service model, not on profit data.

Greatness potential: 60/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Stanwick does not try to be an office-electronics conglomerate. It owns the business mailroom. Its legally required lease-and-service model is its moat. The patents are free under the decree, but the installed base, the national field force and the regular customer visits are not. Every visit deepens the relationship. Every customer gets more mail-handling equipment, financed by Stanwick credit and serviced by Stanwick people. Profit per business customer rises year after year. Stanwick applies electronics to the meter and the mailroom before anyone else does, so a decree licensee can never leapfrog it. It treats the U.S. Postal Service as its most important relationship, and that relationship is managed, not left to luck. Lines that don't serve the mailroom customer either earn their cost of capital on their own terms or leave.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop investing in retail POS and electronic cash registers.** Complete the exit. No follow-on "fix it" capital.
2. **Stop fighting the copier leader head-on for large accounts.** Give copiers 12 months to show they earn above the cost of capital when sold to existing mailroom customers through the field force. If they can't, exit.
3. **Stop using price-marking as a beachhead into retail systems.** That route led to the POS loss. Price-marking stands on its own returns, and divestiture is on the table if it doesn't serve the core.
4. **Stop treating meters as a mature cash cow whose job is funding diversification.** Meters are the growth platform, by depth per customer.
5. **Stop buying growth** through acquisitions or joint ventures in markets where larger, better-funded firms lead.
6. **Stop judging the business on consolidated revenue.** No line keeps capital without a return-on-capital figure.
7. **Stop leaning on patents as the moat.** The decree made them free. Invest instead in service, the installed base and technology lead.

**Non-negotiable disciplines:**
- Every field visit includes a needs check of the customer's mailroom.
- A quarterly return-on-capital review by line, against a published hurdle.
- Every USPS ruling or rate proposal gets a written impact note within two weeks.

**Framework boundaries:**
- *Freedom:* the field and line leaders choose how to grow profit per customer.
- *Framework:* they may not take capital outside the mailroom-and-finance Hedgehog without a CEO and board exception against the hurdle.

**Bureaucracy check:** any approval layers added after the POS loss should be replaced by the hurdle-rate rule plus the right people, not by more sign-offs `[ASSUMPTION]`.
