# Stanwick Mailing Systems: Good to Great

**Verdict: good, not yet great. The path to great is to narrow back to the customer relationship Stanwick already owns.**

- **Hedgehog (working hypothesis):** Be the best in the world at keeping a business's mail and back-office paperwork moving, and earn more profit per business customer through a field-service relationship no rival can copy.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** there is no evidence yet that second products, such as copiers, raise profit per customer.
- **Stop doing:** (1) retail point-of-sale and retail electronics; (2) price-led copier fights for customers who don't use meters; (3) buying growth through joint ventures or acquisitions in markets that bigger firms lead.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** Stanwick is diversifying without knowing which lines make money. The $42M write-off is the proof.
- **Missing numbers that would change this:** profit and return on capital by line; copier sales to meter customers versus everyone else; customer count and revenue per customer.
- **Confidence:** moderate on direction, low on line-by-line calls. It rests on meter dominance, the field force, and the point-of-sale loss.

Greatness potential: 50/100

---

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

In the great version, Stanwick is the company businesses rely on to get their mail and paperwork out the door. Its field force already visits a very large base of business customers every year because the meters must be leased and inspected. The great version treats those visits as its core asset rather than a cost. It learns how each customer's back office really works. It engineers the next machine that customer needs, leases it through captive finance, and services it on the same route. It grows by earning more profit from each business relationship, not by entering markets where a larger, better-funded firm already leads. It says no to retail systems, to head-on copier price wars, and to revenue growth for its own sake. It treats the Postal Service as a partner to shape, not a risk to ignore. And it no longer depends on one retiring chairman's judgment.

| **Economic engine (denominator)** | Profit per meter; revenue per product line; profit per business customer; profit per service call | **Profit per business customer** `[ASSUMPTION: the leasing and service model makes the customer relationship, not the unit, the real earner]` | Revenue growth, which rewards the diversification that produced the $42M loss |
| **Passion** | The meter heritage; office technology in general | Pride in the meter and in serving business customers well `[ASSUMPTION: inferred from a 50-year meter-centered identity and the outgoing chairman's legacy]` | "Being an office-electronics company" as an aspiration for its own sake |

**Tests of the candidate:**
- *Explains past wins?* Yes. The meter's success comes from Post Office approval combined with a leased, serviced, recurring relationship. Revenue grew about 6.75×, roughly 16% a year in nominal terms over 13 years.
- *Rules things out?* Yes. It rules out retail point-of-sale, probably price-marking expansion, and copiers sold outside the base.
- *Uncomfortable?* Yes. It questions price-marking, a #1 business, and it asks a new CEO to forgo the visible growth stories.

**Confirmation evidence needed:**
1. Profit per customer is higher for meter customers who also take a second Stanwick product than for meter-only customers.
2. Copier profit on meter customers is positive. On customers outside the base, it is weaker.
3. Field-force cost per customer falls as products per customer rise.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   ▼                                                          │
1. Field force installs, inspects and services meters         │
   at a huge base of business customers (recurring visits)    │
   ▼                                                          │
2. Intimate knowledge of each customer's mail and             │
   back-office flow                                           │
   ▼                                                          │
3. Engineer the next back-office machine for that flow        │
   (mail handling first, then document handling)              │
   ▼                                                          │
4. Lease it through the same field force + captive finance    │
   ▼   ◄── WEAKEST LINK (4→5): unproven that add-ons raise profit
5. Higher PROFIT PER BUSINESS CUSTOMER + recurring service    │
   revenue                                                    │
   ▼                                                          │
6. Fund field density, service quality and mail-tech R&D ─────┘
```

**Current push (next one or two turns):**
1. Measure link 4→5. Find out which non-meter products actually add profit per customer.
2. Redirect copier selling toward meter customers only, sold as a serviced package and not on price. Stop any product that fails the test above.

**Doom-loop check:** the warning signs are present.
- The company jumped into copiers, then price-marking, then finance, then retail point-of-sale, all within about six years.
- It used a joint venture to jump-start growth in a market led by others, and that ended in the $42M write-off.
- A leadership change creates pressure for "a new direction."

The fix is to stop announcing new directions and push the same loop consistently.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Retail point-of-sale and retail electronic systems.** No new entry, no rescue, no follow-on joint venture.
2. **Price-led copier competition for customers who don't use meters.** Sell copiers only as serviced packages into the meter base, and only if they show positive profit per customer.
3. **Buying growth through acquisitions or joint ventures in markets where a larger firm leads.**
4. **Using total revenue growth as the headline goal.** Replace it with profit per business customer.
5. **Expanding price-marking into adjacent retail technology.** Run it for cash pending review. Selling it is a live option if it can't connect to the engine `[ASSUMPTION]`.
6. **Letting the finance subsidiary lend on equipment Stanwick didn't make.**
7. **Treating meter market share as the strategy.** An 85–90% share under a consent decree is a ceiling and a political target, not a growth plan.
