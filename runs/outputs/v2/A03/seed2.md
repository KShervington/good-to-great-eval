# Brennard Laboratories: Good to Great

**Verdict: a good company that is now in a turnaround. Its great version is a focused hospital-products company. It is not a drug-discovery company and not a consumer-brands company.**

- **Hedgehog (working hypothesis):** Be the hospital's most trusted maker of products that lower the total cost of care: IV solutions, clinical nutrition and diagnostics.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** trust. The IV recall (434 infections, 49 deaths) broke the "safe and dependable" step that every other step depends on.
- **Top 3 stop-doing:** (1) consumer brands: golf balls, gloves, creamer and sweetener; (2) racing research-led rivals for blockbuster drugs; (3) spreading capital across five divisions.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** there is no strategy, while both historic profit engines (the sweetener and the patented antibiotic) are gone.
- **Missing numbers that could change this:** profit and return on capital by division; infant-formula and diagnostics market share; research spending as a share of sales.
- **Confidence:** moderate to low. The verdict rests on which way the evidence points, not on financials.

Greatness potential: 30/100

---

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Brennard is the supplier hospitals rely on to deliver care for less. It sells IV solutions with a safety record hospitals can point to, clinical and infant nutrition, hospital-use drugs such as its intravenous anesthetic, and diagnostics that make analysis cheaper and faster. It no longer sells golf balls or creamer. It no longer tries to out-discover research-led rivals like Rival U. Its research arm does applied development of cost-saving clinical products. Every capital decision is tested against one question: does this lower a hospital's cost of care, and does it raise our profit per employee? Its quality system reports independently to the board, so the recall cannot happen again. The company runs on mechanisms, not on whoever happens to be CEO. That matters because the last CEO died suddenly.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   ┌─► 1. Dependable products that lower hospitals' total cost of care
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │    2. Hospitals adopt across lines (IV, nutrition, anesthetic, diagnostics)
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │    3. Volume and manufacturing scale on standardized products
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │    4. Higher PROFIT PER EMPLOYEE (denominator)
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │    5. Reinvest in quality systems + applied development of the next cost-saver
   │            │
   │            ▼
   └── 6. Reputation for safety and value with hospitals and regulators
```

**Weakest link:** step 6 to step 1, which is trust. Until the IV safety record is rebuilt in public, adoption in step 2 stays slow.

**Current push (next one or two turns):**
1. Rebuild sterile-manufacturing trust in a visible, audited way.
2. Move capital from consumer brands into diagnostics and clinical nutrition.

**Doom-loop check: signs are present now.**
- The sweetener ban led to diversification into unrelated consumer goods. That is reaction without understanding.
- The previous CEO pushed research and international expansion. The current CEO pushes consumer diversification. That is a new direction under each leader.
- Capital is spread across five divisions with no organizing idea.

The flywheel has to replace this pattern, not sit alongside it.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. Golf balls, household rubber gloves and non-dairy creamer. Begin structured exit or sale.
2. The tabletop sweetener and the consumer-brand building around it.
3. Competing head-to-head with research-led drug makers for blockbuster discoveries.
4. Spreading capital across five divisions. Allocate by fit with the Hedgehog Concept and by profit per employee.
5. Acquisitions meant to replace lost income (the sweetener, the antibiotic) unless they are hospital cost-of-care businesses.
6. Treating quality as a cost to manage. Any sterility doubt stops shipment.
7. Copying rivals' adjacent moves, such as agricultural seeds.
8. Relying on regulators to reverse decisions when the company should adapt ahead of them.

**Non-negotiable disciplines:**
- Every product proposal states how it lowers a hospital's total cost of care.
- Quarterly profit per employee is reported by division.
- Quality holds independent stop authority.

**Framework boundaries:**
- Division heads have full freedom *inside* hospital cost-of-care lines.
- Anything outside those lines needs board-level challenge.

**Bureaucracy check:** rules that exist to police a weakened senior team should fall away as the bus is fixed.

## 7. Core ideology and the BHAG

**Core values:**
1. Patient safety comes before schedule and cost.
2. Be candid with regulators, customers and ourselves.
3. Practical value: every product must lower someone's cost of care.
4. Rigor and standardization, carried over from the founding.

**Core purpose:** Make dependable health care affordable.

**BHAG (20 years):** Become the supplier hospitals consider indispensable for delivering care at lower cost, with no repeat of a product-safety death.

**Clock-building mechanisms:**
- An independent quality function with stop authority that reports to the board.
- Divisional profit-per-employee reporting.
- A capital-allocation rule tied to the Hedgehog Concept.
- A formal CEO succession plan with named internal candidates.
- A red-flag escalation rule.
