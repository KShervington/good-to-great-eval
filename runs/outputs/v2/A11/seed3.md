## 1. The great version in one paragraph

The great Lindqvist does not try to be a national grocer everywhere. It is the dominant food-and-drug superstore in a deliberately chosen set of metro markets, holding #1 or #2 share in each. Each store pairs a full supermarket with a pharmacy and specialty departments under one roof, so it captures most of a household's weekly basket.

What goes in each store is decided by evidence. That evidence is the 40-year tradition of treating purchases as shopper votes, now sharpened by checkout scanners that Lindqvist was first to put into real-world use. Manufacturing exists only where it gives the store brand a real cost or quality edge. Lindqvist leaves markets where it can't lead, calmly and early, instead of losing them slowly to price wars. It wins the price war by having a better store, not by being the cheapest one.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   ┌──► 1. Shopper votes + scanner data on every item and department
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │    2. Right store: combo food + drug superstore, department mix set by data
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │    3. More of each household's weekly basket (food + pharmacy + specialty)
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │    4. Higher local market share → #1/#2 position in the metro
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │    5. Local density lowers cost per unit (ads, distribution, store-brand supply)
   │            │       → profit per local household rises
   │            ▼
   └─── 6. Reinvest: competitive prices + convert/replace more stores in chosen metros
```

**Current push (the next two turns):**
1. **Prove link 2.** Use the scanner and consumer-research capability to measure the economics of the paired and larger test stores against conventional stores, in the same metro and with like-for-like data.
2. **Draw the market map.** Rank every metro by current share and by its realistic path to #1 or #2. Commit to the winners and set orderly exit criteria for the rest.

**Doom-loop check:** there are warning signs. Over about 13 years the company changed direction several times: drugstores, then family centers, then manufacturing consolidation, then specialty departments, with market exits spread throughout. It added formats and plants without a stated unifying concept, and there is a risk of now reacting to Rival A's price war without understanding it. The loop does close, but only if link 2 is proven. This is why the gate is CONDITIONAL.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop defending metros where Lindqvist can't reach #1 or #2.** Set exit criteria in advance instead of selling under pressure.
2. **Stop building, expanding or heavily remodeling small conventional stores.** Capital goes to combo superstores in chosen markets.
3. **Stop matching Rival A's deep discount across the board.** Price competitively on key-value items, and compete on the store itself.
4. **Stop investing in manufacturing capacity for its own sake.** No new plants unless they give store-brand cost or quality an edge that a supplier can't match.
5. **Stop expanding the general-merchandise family-center format** until it proves it fits the hedgehog `[ASSUMPTION]`.
6. **Stop trading product quality for cost in processed foods.** One brand covers every product line, so one quality lapse damages all of them.
7. **Stop pursuing growth through acquisitions.** Antitrust limits make it slow and risky, so share has to be won store by store.

**Non-negotiable disciplines:**
- Every store decision cites shopper-vote or scanner evidence.
- Every metro gets a share review every year.
- Every plant is scored on its contribution to the stores.

**Framework boundaries:** headquarters chooses the markets and formats. Store and metro managers have autonomy over local mix, staffing and service within the combo format.

**Bureaucracy check:** no evidence either way. Watch for headquarters controls that exist because local managers can't be trusted with data. The fix there is people, not rules.
