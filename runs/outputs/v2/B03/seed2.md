## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Larkspur is the bank most American households choose as their *primary* bank and keep for decades. Customers hold more products there because they use them. Nobody was told to sell them. Its edge is the lowest-cost, most stable deposit base in the industry, gathered through 9,000+ stores and the digital channels that sit beside them. That edge lets it price fairly, lend through cycles, and earn the most profit per active relationship. "Cross-sell" survives only as a by-product of serving customers well. It is never paid on and never quota'd. Risk, conduct and incentive design report to an enterprise function that the business lines cannot overrule, so a problem in one unit cannot stay hidden there. Great Larkspur outperforms because customers trust it. It does not outperform because team members are pressed to open accounts.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence (working hypothesis):** *Be the primary bank of American households and small businesses, earning the most profit per active, trusting relationship, funded by the cheapest and stickiest deposits in the country.*

| Circle | Candidates considered | Kept | Explicitly *not* best-in-world at |
|---|---|---|---|
| Best in the world at | (a) Cross-selling products; (b) Mortgage origination volume; (c) Low-cost core deposit franchise plus community-store distribution; (d) Commercial real estate lending | **(c)**, the scale of the deposit and relationship franchise ("one in three households") | Investment banking and capital markets; mortgage *volume* as an end in itself; high-cost or subprime consumer lending |
| Economic engine denominator | Products per household; profit per store; profit per team member; **profit per active customer relationship** | **Profit per active primary relationship (household or small business)** `[ASSUMPTION: Larkspur does not report this today]` | Product count, which costs nothing to inflate and is easy to game |
| Passion | Community banking culture; "stores" and "team members" vocabulary; serving all of a customer's needs | **Helping customers succeed financially in their local communities** | Selling for its own sake |

**Why the denominator changed:** the vision statement says the goal is meeting customers' needs, but the yardstick counts products.
- A count rises when a product is opened, whether or not the customer needs or uses it.
- Profit per *active* relationship rises only when the customer actually brings business.
- Products the customer never wanted cost the bank money to service and carry risk.

So the new denominator keeps the original intent and removes the incentive to game it.

**Confirmation tests:**
- **Does it explain past wins?** Yes, it explains why Larkspur came through the crisis better than rivals: stable deposits and a relationship base.
- **Does it rule things out?** Yes: quotas, subprime-style lending, and a volume-first mortgage push.
- **Would it be uncomfortable to commit to?** Yes. It dethrones the cross-sell number the culture is built around.

**What would confirm it:**
- Activity data showing that active products per household, and not raw count, drive profit per household.
- Primary-checking households growing without quota pressure.

**Current push (the next two turns):**
1. **Rebuild link 4 so customers pull rather than the bank pushing.**
   - Measure active and funded products.
   - Remove product-count pay and quotas.
   - Make needs-based conversations the store's job.
2. **Use the end of integration to deepen primary relationships in the acquired East Coast footprint.** That means primary checking and deposit share, not product count.

**Doom-loop check:**
- **Big program to jump-start momentum:** the large acquisition is done. Watch for the next one being reached for once integration savings stop flattering earnings growth.
- **Forced pushes disguised as momentum:** this is the live danger. The loop runs: earnings pressure → raise sales goals → manufactured or unwanted products → complaints and regulatory action → fines and lost trust → more earnings pressure. The subsidiary finding shows this loop has already turned once inside Larkspur.
- **Inconsistency or lurching direction:** none visible. The strategy has been consistent for years. That is the strength to keep.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Daily and monthly product-sales quotas** for store team members.
2. **Incentive pay tied to products sold or to cross-sell count.**
3. **Holding "eight products per household" as a goal.** Retire it, internally and in investor messaging, in favor of active-relationship metrics.
4. **Leading investor communications with raw cross-sell** without disclosing how many of those products are active or funded.
5. **Letting business lines own their own risk and HR oversight** for conduct issues.
6. **Pricing loans by sales opportunity instead of customer qualification.** End anything resembling the subsidiary's steering of qualified borrowers into higher-cost loans, and audit mortgage pricing against the fair-lending allegation.
7. **Chasing mortgage origination volume for share.** Being the largest originator is not the same as earning the most per relationship.
8. **Reaching for a major acquisition** to replace the earnings growth that integration savings provided.
9. **Treating the subsidiary fine as "legacy" or "isolated"** without testing whether the same design produces the same behavior in stores.
