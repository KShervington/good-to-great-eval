# Brightwater Drug Corporation: Good to Great

**Verdict: good, not yet great, and drifting away from its core.**

- **Hedgehog Concept (working):** Be the lowest-price, most convenient self-service drugstore in every Sunbelt market Brightwater enters, managed on profit per customer visit.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** profit per visit. Nobody measures it, and acquired chains and four unrelated businesses likely dilute it.
- **Top three stop-doing items:**
  1. Stop buying businesses outside drugstores.
  2. Stop owning the department stores, security business and food-service supplier.
  3. Stop buying drug chains faster than they can be integrated.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** The largest U.S. drug chain (about 600 stores, about $1B in sales) has just entered the home state. Meanwhile the founder's attention has been on politics for five years.
- **Missing numbers that would change the answer:** profit by business line, sales and profit per store and per visit, store count, and home-state share.
- **Confidence:** low to moderate. The verdict rests on the shape of the dossier, not on any financials.

Greatness potential: 22/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Brightwater is a pure drugstore company. Its stores sit in dense clusters across the Southeast and Texas, mostly next to busy supermarkets, so a store is the easiest place nearby for a customer to fill a prescription or buy everyday items at a discount. It has sold the department stores, the security business and the food-service supplier. Optical centers stay only if they raise profit per visit inside drugstores. Leaders judge every site, acquisition and price move by one number: profit per customer visit. It grows by filling in its existing regions before planting flags in new ones. It responds to Rival W by being denser and cheaper in its own markets, not by buying something new. It has a professional chief executive and an independent board, so it does not depend on whether the founder is in the building, in the Senate, or retired.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence:** *Be the lowest-price, most convenient self-service drugstore in every Sunbelt market we enter, run on profit per customer visit.*

| **Best in world at** | National drug chain, diversified retail/services group, Sunbelt convenience drugstore, discount pricing | Self-service discount drugstores co-located with supermarkets in the Southeast and Texas, a narrow enough world to win | Junior department stores, security guarding, food-service distribution, candy (already exited), national scale against Rival W |
| **Economic engine (denominator)** | Profit per store, per square foot, per prescription, per customer visit | **Profit per customer visit** `[ASSUMPTION]`. | **Passion** | Founder: self-service innovation, beating price-fixing, employees. **Tests:**
- **Explains past wins:** yes. The early self-service conversions, the supermarket-adjacent sites and the fair-trade court victory all match the concept.
- **Rules things out:** yes. It excludes four of the five non-drug moves.
- **Uncomfortable to commit to:** yes. It means selling businesses the founder chose and admitting that the "diversified" story was a detour.

**Confirmation tests (working hypothesis):**
1. Drugstore profit per visit is higher than the return on capital in each non-drug line once measured `[ASSUMPTION]`.
2. Stores co-located with supermarkets out-earn stand-alone stores per visit.
3. Brightwater's stores in dense clusters hold their per-visit profit where Rival W opens nearby.

**Verdict: `CONDITIONAL`.** *Condition:* the "best in world" claim and the denominator are untested. *Evidence for PASS:* per-store and per-visit economics by region and by origin (organic vs acquired), and a comparison with Rival W in the home state.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   Lowest everyday prices (discount, no fair-trade constraint)
                 │
                 ▼
   Convenient sites in dense clusters, beside supermarkets
                 │
                 ▼
   More customer visits per store
                 │
                 ▼
   Higher PROFIT PER CUSTOMER VISIT
   (purchasing scale, shared distribution, store density)
                 │
                 ▼
   Cash to open/fill in stores in the SAME clusters
                 │
                 ▼
   Greater regional density → stronger purchasing & distribution leverage
                 │
                 └──────────► back to: lowest prices
```

**Current push (the next two turns):**
1. **Measure the denominator.** Profit per visit by store, region and origin. Today the flywheel spins blind.
2. **Defend and densify the home state.** Put capital into filling in around Rival W's 29 entry stores rather than into new regions or new businesses.

**Doom-loop check:** **several signs are present.**
- Acquisitions in five unrelated fields over about nine years, one of which (candy) was already bought and then sold.
- Drug-chain acquisitions in four or more states within four years, with no evidence they were integrated.
- Strategy driven by "whether to keep diversifying" rather than by a single denominator.
- A risk of grasping for a big deal in response to Rival W.

**Verdict: `CONDITIONAL`.** The loop closes on paper. *Condition:* doom-loop behavior is ongoing. *Evidence for PASS:* a board-adopted acquisition moratorium outside drugstores, and per-visit data showing acquired stores converging with the core.

5. **Stop opening optical centers** until a test shows they raise profit per visit in host drugstores.
6. **Stop buying drug chains in new regions** until the Louisiana, Texas, Georgia and Mid-Atlantic acquisitions hit core per-visit profit `[ASSUMPTION that they currently don't]`.
7. **Stop measuring success by sales growth or store count alone.**

**Non-negotiable disciplines:**
- Every site, deal and price change gets a profit-per-visit case.
- Cluster first: fill in existing markets before entering new ones.
- Price leadership in every market where Brightwater operates.

**Framework (freedom within boundaries):** Store managers own staffing, service and local assortment within the price and format standard. Head office owns pricing, sites, purchasing and capital.
