# Kestrel Stores: Good to Great

## Verdict

**Kestrel was great on shareholder returns but is now slipping toward good.** It should be run as an early turnaround, before any crisis, and not left in stewardship mode.

- **Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** be America's best place to buy complex, high-ticket home electronics, run on the category's best distribution and store operations, and measured by profit per store.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** whether expert help actually produces higher profit per store. Rival B wins on profit per store without commissions.
- **Top three stop-doing items:** (1) chasing the 800-store, $15B headline; (2) keeping the used-car business inside Kestrel's management attention (separate it); (3) building stand-alone appliance stores.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** Rival B has led Kestrel on profit per store, earnings and share for about four years.
- **Missing numbers that would change this:** electronics profit per store, margins and ROE versus Rival B; store age and remodel cost; share by category.
- **Confidence:** low to moderate. The verdict rests on directional facts in the dossier, without unit economics.

Greatness potential: 24/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

In its great version, Kestrel's whole management attention is on electronics. Every store is current, well stocked and visibly better run than the competition. When a household buys a TV, a home-theater system or a computer and wants to get it right, it goes to Kestrel because the help is real and the price is fair. Kestrel stops measuring itself by revenue and store count. It measures profit per store, store by store and category by category, every month. It funds remodels and people before new stores and new ventures. The used-car business has its own owners and its own leaders. Kestrel no longer invents formats or enters categories where others hold the structural advantage. It closes the profit-per-store gap with Rival B and then passes it. It keeps that lead through changes of CEO, because the disciplines live in mechanisms rather than in one leader's judgment.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence (working hypothesis):** *Kestrel is the best place in America to buy complex, high-ticket home electronics, run on the industry's best distribution and store system, and it measures itself by profit per store.*

| Circle | Candidates considered | Kept | Ruled out (cannot be best at) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Best in the world | Store operations and distribution; commissioned advice; wide selection; low-price self-service; inventing formats; big-box retail of anything | **Distribution and store operations, which the industry rates highly, applied where advice matters (complex, high-ticket electronics)** | Low-price self-service commodity selling (Rival B already owns this); creating media formats (the disc-rental venture failed); used-car retail *as an electronics company*; major appliances against home-improvement chains `[ASSUMPTION: those chains hold traffic and price advantages]` |
| Economic engine | Revenue growth; store count; profit per store; profit per customer; profit per square foot | **Profit per store (electronics)** | Revenue and store count, which are the current headline metrics and have led to missed targets |
| Passion | Retail operations; electronics; the sale itself; growth | **Running great stores and helping customers buy technology well** `[ASSUMPTION: the dossier has no evidence on passion]` | Growth for its own sake |

**Why this denominator:** profit per store is the measure on which Rival B demonstrably beats Kestrel. It also settles the live choices. Remodeling the existing base competes with opening new stores, keeping appliances competes with dropping them, and commission competes with self-service. Profit per store judges each of them.

**Tests:**
- *Does it explain past wins?* Yes. Growth from $1B to $10.6B came from superstores plus systems plus counselors.
- *Does it rule things out?* Yes: the disc-rental format, used cars, stand-alone appliance stores and the race to 800 stores.
- *Is it uncomfortable?* Yes. It questions 14% of sales (appliances) and the company's second business.

**Confirmation tests over 12 months:**
1. A controlled test shows that counselor-served complex categories beat self-service on profit per store and customer satisfaction.
2. The profit-per-store gap with Rival B stops widening.
3. Remodeled stores beat new stores on return per dollar.

**If test 1 fails,** commissioned advice is not a best-in-world advantage. Kestrel would then be playing Rival B's game as the second player, and its honest ceiling may be "good."

## 3. The flywheel

```
 (1) Best-in-class distribution & store operations
        ↓
 (2) Right selection, in stock, fair prices, current stores
        ↓
 (3) Genuinely expert help on complex, high-ticket purchases   ← WEAKEST LINK (unproven vs. self-service)
        ↓
 (4) Higher conversion, attachment, trust and repeat visits
        ↓
 (5) PROFIT PER STORE  (the denominator)
        ↓
 (6) Reinvest in remodels, systems and counselor quality; new stores only above hurdle
        ↓
     back to (1)
```

**The loop closes.** It breaks today in two places. Link 3→4 is unproven against Rival B's model. Link 5→6 is leaking, because profits have gone to the disc format, used cars and store count instead of reinvestment in the existing base.

**Current push (next one or two turns):**
1. Redirect capital from ventures and new-store count into remodeling the existing base.
2. Turn the 30–35 store remodel test into a controlled experiment of the sales model, so that link 3 is decided by evidence.

**Doom-loop check: signs present.**
- One new direction after another: the disc format, used cars sold through a tracking stock with an 80-store goal, and now stand-alone appliance stores.
- Big programs launched to jump-start momentum: the $15B target.
- Reaction without understanding is a risk on the commission question if it gets flipped all at once.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Chasing 800 stores and $15B as goals.** At about 25 new stores a year, 800 is roughly nine years away (derived). Retire the headline and replace it with profit per store.
2. **Keeping used cars inside Kestrel's management system.** Separate it completely and stop treating it as Kestrel's growth story.
3. **Stand-alone appliance stores.** Cancel the six-store test.
4. **Inventing proprietary formats or standards.** Kestrel sells other companies' technology. The disc-rental format cost about $233M in development and promotion, plus losses and charges across two years.
5. **Opening new stores ahead of fixing the existing base.** New stores should go ahead only when they clear a profit-per-store hurdle that remodels can't beat.
6. **Answering investor concern with growth rates.** Report profit per store and the gap to Rival B instead.
7. **Treating commission as an all-or-nothing, ideological choice.** Decide it category by category, from the test.
8. **Defending appliances out of habit.** Set an exit criterion within six months, with a bias toward exit unless per-category profit justifies staying.

## 7. Core ideology and the BHAG

- **Core values `[ASSUMPTION: inferred, not stated in dossier]`:**
  - Customers leave with the right product, not just a product.
  - Operational excellence in every store, every day.
  - Disciplined stewardship of capital.
  - Respect for and investment in the people on the floor.
- **Core purpose:** help households get technology right in their homes.
- **BHAG (15 years):** become the electronics retailer American households trust most for big technology purchases, and the most profitable per store in the category.

**Mechanisms (clock building):**
- A monthly profit-per-store review by store and category.
- A standing Rival B benchmark in every board pack.
- A capital rule: any initiative outside the hedgehog must pass a written hedgehog test and stay within a capital cap set by the board.
- A fixed remodel cycle for the store base.
- A blameless post-mortem after every failed initiative.
- A deep internal succession bench below the CEO.
