# Kestrel Stores: Good to Great

## Verdict

**Kestrel is good and was once great. It is now losing ground, and its record year is hiding that.**

- **Hedgehog (working hypothesis):** Kestrel helps people get the right technology home and working, through expert guidance in the best-run electronics stores, measured by profit per store.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** whether expert advice actually raises profit per store. It is unproven, and commission pay may undercut the trust that advice depends on.
- **Top three stop-doing items:** (1) appliances, starting with the six stand-alone stores; (2) running the used-car business from Kestrel management, which means finishing the separation; (3) revenue and store-count targets.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** Rival B has led Kestrel on sales, earnings, profit per store and share for four years.
- **Missing numbers that would change this:** profit per store against Rival B, ROE and margin trends, store age and remodel cost, appliance profitability.
- **Confidence:** low to moderate. It rests on the dossier alone, which has no unit economics.

Greatness potential: 25/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Kestrel is the place Americans go when a technology purchase is too important to get wrong. Its advisors steer customers to the right product rather than the biggest ticket. They are paid on outcomes the customer also cares about. Kestrel delivers, sets up and supports what it sells, so the product works at home. The well-regarded distribution and store-operations machine serves a deliberately narrower range: TV and video, audio, computers and home office, and games. That range sits in stores renovated around how customers now shop. Management stopped chasing store counts, revenue milestones, appliances, cars and proprietary formats. It watches one number above all: profit per store, set against Rival B. Its stores earn more than Rival B's, and the lead holds through a CEO transition and through product cycles.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence (working hypothesis):** *Kestrel helps people get the right technology home and working, through expert guidance in the best-run electronics stores, measured by profit per store.*

| Circle | Candidates considered | Kept | Ruled out, and why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Best in world at** | (a) Running large electronics stores and distribution efficiently; (b) guided selling of complex, fast-changing tech; (c) major appliances; (d) lowest-price self-service; (e) used-car retail; (f) creating proprietary media formats | (a)+(b) combined. Operations are proven. Guided selling is the original formula, and Rival B has left that space open. | (c) Home-improvement chains own the kitchen-remodel customer and are starting price wars. (d) Rival B is already there and ahead. (e) Possibly best-in-world, but as a separate business with its own Hedgehog. (f) DIVX showed that Kestrel cannot set industry standards when other retailers won't carry the product. |
| **Economic engine** | Profit per store; profit per square foot; profit per region; revenue growth | **Profit per store** (square-foot-adjusted where store sizes differ). This is the exact measure on which Rival B leads, and it exposes format quality rather than expansion. Profit per region is the backup denominator, because distribution density is a known strength. | Revenue growth and store count. These drove the last decade and produced the $15B and 800-store targets, which are now being missed. |
| **Passion** | Helping customers with technology; operational execution; growth | Counselors' product enthusiasm and operations pride [ASSUMPTION: the dossier has no culture data] | "Growth" as a passion. It is an outcome, not a cause, and it is the passion the magazine profile celebrated. |

**Tests:**
- *Does it explain past wins?* Yes. Knowledgeable counselors plus excellent operations built the 69-to-600-store run.
- *Does it rule things out?* Yes: appliances (about $1.5B, or 14% of store sales), the car business, format ventures, and store-count goals.
- *Is it uncomfortable to commit to?* Very. It forces the company to question its signature commission model and to give up revenue.

**Confirmation tests (12 months):** Turn the planned 30–35 remodels into a controlled format test with three arms:
- (A) advice plus setup and installation, with redesigned pay;
- (B) the current commission model in a new box;
- (C) self-service.

The hypothesis is confirmed if A beats B and C on profit per store, with no worse close rate and fewer returns. If C wins, the Hedgehog falls back to pure operations excellence. In that world, Kestrel is chasing Rival B on Rival B's terms and should accept that it may only ever be good.

## 3. The flywheel

```
  ┌─► 1. Expert advice + setup: customer gets the right product, working
  │                    │
  │                    ▼
  │   2. Higher close rate, attached services, fewer returns
  │                    │
  │                    ▼
  │   3. PROFIT PER STORE rises   ◄── denominator
  │                    │
  │                    ▼
  │   4. Reinvest in winning-format remodels + advisor training/pay
  │                    │
  │                    ▼
  │   5. Trust → repeat visits, referrals, regional share
  │                    │
  │                    ▼
  └── 6. Regional density → distribution leverage, in-stock, delivery
```

**Weakest link:** 1 → 2 → 3. Kestrel has not shown that its advice beats Rival B's self-service on profit per store, and Rival B has gained share since it dropped commissions.

**Current push:** (1) Run the remodel test as an honest experiment, with the metrics fixed before the stores open. (2) Move capital from appliances and old-format new stores into the format that wins.

**Doom-loop check:** Several signs are present.
- **New direction after new direction:** DIVX, then cars, then stand-alone appliance stores, then remodels.
- **Big programs to jump-start growth:** about $233M on DIVX, and a car-venture IPO to fund 80+ stores.
- **Reversals:** DIVX shut down, car expansion halted.

The next doom-loop move to watch for is a panic copy of Rival B's self-service model, or a chain-wide remodel before the test reads out.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop the six stand-alone appliance stores now.** Exit major appliances over 12–18 months and give the floor space to the Hedgehog categories. Revisit only if the data shows appliance profit per square foot above the store average.
2. **Stop running the used-car business from Kestrel's management.** Finish the separation.
3. **Stop setting revenue and store-count targets** ($15B, 800 stores). Replace them with a profit-per-store goal.
4. **Stop opening stores in the old format.** Cut the 25 planned openings back to infill in the most profitable regions [ASSUMPTION: some of those sites are already committed] until the format test reads out.
5. **Stop proprietary-format ventures.** Sell the formats the industry adopts. Don't try to own them.
6. **Stop guiding to 20–25% earnings growth.** That forecast pushes management to manufacture growth.
7. **Stop paying for volume where it conflicts with customer outcome.** The pay redesign depends on the test, but the principle holds now.

**Non-negotiable disciplines:**
- A monthly profit-per-store scoreboard, benchmarked against Rival B.
- A written Hedgehog test for every new initiative.
- No chain-wide rollout without controlled store evidence.
- Fanatical consistency on in-stock, delivery and setup.

**Freedom within a framework:** Store managers and advisors have autonomy in how they serve customers. The framework covers what Kestrel sells, how advisors are paid, and the metrics everyone answers to.

**Bureaucracy check:** [ASSUMPTION] Look for scripts, quotas and return-policy rules that exist mainly to police high-pressure commission selling. If pay changes, those rules should shrink.
