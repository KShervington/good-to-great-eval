# Good to Great: Duplicon Corporation (as of late Year 0)

## Verdict

**Good, but declining. Run this as a turnaround: narrow the company instead of leaping into new technology.**

- **Hedgehog (working hypothesis):** Be the best in the world at equipping quick-print shops to produce short runs more cheaply than any copier, and earn the profit on each installed press through supplies and service.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** offset's cost-per-copy advantage over plain-paper copiers. That advantage shrinks as copiers improve `[ASSUMPTION]`.
- **Top three stop-doing:** (1) betting the company on electronic-office leadership against much larger firms; (2) growth investment in plate-based office addressing (harvest it instead); (3) ventures or acquisitions meant to restart growth.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** the core products are being replaced by a technology Duplicon doesn't lead, and sales peaked about six years ago.
- **Missing numbers that would change this:** profit and return on capital by line; market share in quick-print and cards; supplies and service share of revenue.
- **Confidence:** low to moderate. The verdict rests only on the dossier's qualitative facts. There are no current financials.

Greatness potential: 12/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

A great Duplicon is smaller and more profitable. It is the standard equipment and supplies partner for independent quick-print shops and in-plant print rooms. Its presses and platemaking make short-run printing cheaper per page than any office copier for the jobs those shops live on. Most of its profit comes from a growing stream of supplies and service on every installed machine. It runs office addressing for cash instead of growth. It has either built credit-card embossing into a second focused engine or sold it to an owner who will. It treats plain-paper copying as a fact to price against, not a war to fight. It spends research money only where it can lead. If it disappeared, thousands of small printers would lose the one supplier built entirely around their economics. That is the "hole" test of great, and nothing more general than that.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   ┌──► 1. Installed Duplicon presses in quick-print shops
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │    2. Supplies + service profit per installed machine  (denominator)
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │    3. Focused R&D: faster, cheaper platemaking & presses
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │    4. Lower cost per copy than copiers on the shop's typical runs
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │    5. More shops open, upgrade, and standardize on Duplicon
   │            │
   │            ▼
   └─── 6. Denser dealer/service network → better uptime → more installs
```

**Current push:**
1. Measure the copier-versus-offset crossover run length, and track it every quarter.
2. Redirect R&D spending toward platemaking speed and cost, which is the turn most likely to protect link 4.

**Doom-loop check:**
- **Five lines and 27 subsidiaries.** This signals coverage without focus.
- **Three competing strategic options are open at once.** This is a risk of new direction after new direction.
- **Temptation toward a big electronic-office program.** This is the textbook attempt to jump-start momentum. Rival S's write-off this year on a point-of-sale terminal venture is a live peer example.

Verdict: the doom-loop risk is **high**. The decision not yet made is the danger.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. Stop pursuing leadership in electronic office technology against much larger firms.
2. Stop growth investment in plate-based office addressing. Run it for cash and customer retention.
3. Stop launching ventures or making acquisitions to restore growth. That includes point-of-sale terminals and any equivalent of Rival S's write-off.
4. Stop competing head-on with Rival X in plain-paper copiers.
5. Stop funding engineering graphics and typesetting for growth until they show a leadership position. The default is divest or harvest.
6. Stop measuring success by total sales or a return to the $400M+ peak. Measure profit per installed machine and return on capital instead.
7. Stop running 27 subsidiaries as separate fiefdoms. Consolidate reporting to line-level return on capital.
