# Good to Great: Duplicon Corporation

**Verdict: good once, sliding now. Run this as a turnaround.**

- **Hedgehog (working hypothesis):** Be the complete system the small printer runs on (prepress, press, supplies and service), earning profit per installed press.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** whether new quick-print demand grows faster than copiers take short-run jobs away.
- **Top three stop-doing items:**
  1. Stop the heavy bet on electronic office technology against much larger firms.
  2. Stop competing for office convenience copying, where Rival X leads.
  3. Stop running 27 subsidiaries without segment returns. Sell or harvest whatever sits outside the hedgehog.

- **Missing numbers that would change this:** segment profit and return on capital (especially card systems against printing), quick-print share, and how fast addressing is declining.

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

The great Duplicon is smaller and sharper. It is not trying to be an office-technology company. Instead it is the system the world's small printers run on: quick-print shops, in-plant print departments, and school and government print rooms. Every shop that opens buys a Duplicon press, plus the typesetting and platemaking upstream of it. From then on it buys plates, masters, ink and service from Duplicon for as long as the press runs. Profit comes from the installed base, not from new launches. That profit funds steady improvements that lower the shop's cost per job, so more shops choose Duplicon, which grows the base. Plate addressing is run down for cash on purpose, with no attempt to rescue it. Plain-paper copying is conceded to Rival X at short runs, while Duplicon wins the longer-run, higher-quality work where offset economics still beat copying. Card systems are either a second, separately run business that must justify its own capital or are sold to strengthen the core. The company no longer runs a holding portfolio, and it no longer waits for one big product to save it.

**Denominator:** profit per installed press. The alternative is profit per printing customer, if shops run more than one Duplicon device.

**Why this candidate:**
- **It explains past wins.** The one blockbuster press model made Duplicon "a leading name" in quick print.
- **It rules things out.** No copiers, no electronic office systems, no new addressing products, no engineering graphics.
- **It is uncomfortable.** It walks away from the founding franchise and from the corporate story of being a broad office-equipment company.

**The runner-up matters.** Embossing is a single competence that runs from addressing plates to credit cards, and the card market may be growing faster than quick print `[ASSUMPTION]`. If segment data shows card systems with higher return on capital and stronger share, the hedgehog should flip to card systems and printing should be sold. Collins's discipline means choosing one hedgehog, not funding both equally.

**Confirmation tests (the evidence that turns this into a PASS):**
1. The small-printer segment's return on capital is at or above the corporate average and above card systems.
2. Duplicon's share of quick-print press placements is the clear #1 `[ASSUMPTION]`.
3. Supplies and service revenue per installed press is stable or rising.
4. Shop owners in interviews say Duplicon is the default and name what would make them buy again.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   (1) More small printers choose Duplicon presses + prepress
              │
              ▼
   (2) Bigger installed base, denser dealer/service network
              │
              ▼
   (3) Recurring supplies & service → PROFIT PER INSTALLED PRESS
              │
              ▼
   (4) Reinvest in what lowers the shop's cost per job
       (faster makeready, simpler platemaking, integrated typesetting)
              │
              ▼
   (5) Shops win more jobs against copiers at mid/long runs,
       more shops open and upgrade
              │
              └──────────► back to (1)
```

**Current push (the next one or two turns):**
1. Find out the true profit per installed press, and the split between supplies and equipment, so management knows what the engine actually is.
2. Redirect R&D money away from broad electronic-office projects and into lowering the small printer's cost per job. Platemaking and typesetting integration are the likely highest-leverage areas `[ASSUMPTION]`.

**Weakest link:** step (5) to step (1). Copiers will take short runs. The flywheel only works if mid-length and long-run work grows fast enough to keep new shops opening. This is the key fact to research. It can't be checked in this session.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop funding a broad move into electronic office technology** against much larger firms. Rival S, a near-monopolist, still took a large loss trying something similar.
2. **Stop competing for office convenience copying.** Concede short-run office copying to Rival X.
3. **Stop new product development in plate-based addressing.** Harvest it.
4. **Stop running the portfolio as 27 semi-independent companies** without segment return reporting.
5. **Stop treating engineering graphics as a core line.** Sell it.
6. **Stop presenting the company as a broad "office equipment" firm.** That identity invites the wrong fights.
7. **Stop any acquisitions** until the hedgehog is confirmed.
8. **Stop splitting R&D across all lines.** Most of it should push flywheel step (4) `[ASSUMPTION on current split]`.

## 7. Core ideology and the BHAG

- **Core values** `[ASSUMPTION: to be validated with long-tenured staff]`:
  1. Machines that keep running.
  2. Serving the customer for the life of the equipment.
  3. Practical engineering over fashion.
  4. Honest numbers.
- **Core purpose:** Help small organizations put their words on paper (and cards) reliably and affordably.
- **BHAG (20-year):** Make Duplicon the system that most of the world's independent and in-plant printers run on. If the hedgehog flips: the system behind most of the world's embossed cards.
- **Clock-building mechanisms:**
  - Segment return on capital reviewed quarterly by the board.
  - A capital rule: no investment outside the hedgehog without board approval and a stated kill-criterion.
  - A standing harvest plan for addressing.
  - Leadership succession bench drawn from the small-printer unit.
