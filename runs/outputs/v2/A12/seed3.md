# Continental Provision Company: Good to Great Run

## Verdict

**A good company in crisis, run as a turnaround. It has to shrink in order to win.**

- **Hedgehog (working hypothesis):** In the regions where we can be #1 or #2, be the lowest-cost, best-stocked grocer, built on our own brands and run in stores big enough to operate efficiently.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** turning bigger stores into a lower cost per sales dollar. Last year's price cuts failed because costs did not fall with them.
- **Top three to stop:** (1) chain-wide deep discounting that cost cuts don't pay for; (2) subsidizing stores and regions it cannot win; (3) paying out most profit as dividends while the stores decay.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** competitors matched the price cuts. The resulting ~$51M loss is roughly a full normal year's profit `[ASSUMPTION: ~1% margin on >$5B sales]`. Price alone cannot win with this store base.
- **Missing numbers that would change this:** profit by store and by region, regional market share, results of any large-store test, and closure costs under the labor contracts.
- **Confidence:** low to moderate. It rests on the dossier alone, with no unit economics.

Greatness potential: 12/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

The great Continental Provision is smaller, denser and modern. It operates only in regions where it holds #1 or #2 share. Its stores are large enough to run a high-volume, low-cost format. Its century of store-brand manufacturing becomes a real cost and quality advantage instead of a reason shelves sit empty. Low prices are earned by cost reductions first and only then passed to customers. Shelves are full, national brands included, and checkout lines are short. Every capital dollar goes to the core regions. Leadership mixes long-tenured operators with a few outside specialists in the seats the company has never had to fill. Above all, its people treat profit per region as the scoreboard. Total sales volume and the title of "largest" are not the goal.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence (working hypothesis):** *In the regions where we can be #1 or #2, be the lowest-cost, best-stocked grocer, built on our own brands and run in stores big enough to operate efficiently.*

| Circle | Candidates considered | Chosen | What it rules out |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Best in the world at** | National coverage; lowest prices chain-wide; modern superstores; store brands at scale; regional density | **Low-cost, own-brand grocery in dense regional clusters.** The basis is vertical integration (including a 1.5M sq ft plant), a century-old brand, and scale. | Being everywhere. Winning a national price war from small old stores. Out-innovating Rival L on specialty or combination formats in the near term. |
| **Economic engine (denominator)** | Profit per store; profit per sq ft; sales volume; profit per region | **Operating profit per regional market** where the company holds #1 or #2 share. Profit per selling sq ft serves as the store-level check. | Measuring success by total sales or store count. |
| **Passion** | Heritage name; grocery as a craft; store brands; the company's own scale | **A long-tenured workforce that cares about being "the" grocer in its towns** `[ASSUMPTION: no evidence in dossier; must be tested]` | Passion for the size or history of the company as such. |

**Tests:**
- *Explains past wins?* Plausibly. The company dominated when its scale and own-brand cost advantage were concentrated and its stores were competitive.
- *Rules things out?* Yes: weak regions, small stores, unfunded price cuts, and volume for its own sake.
- *Uncomfortable?* Very. It means fewer stores, lower total sales, a dividend cut, and outsiders in senior seats.

**Evidence that would confirm it:**
1. Store and region P&Ls, before and after the price war, show clusters that earn at or above the chain's traditional margin.
2. Own-brand items carry a measurable cost and margin edge over national brands once out-of-stock losses are counted.
3. A large-store pilot in one core region hits its targets for sales per sq ft and labor cost per sales dollar within 12–18 months `[ASSUMPTION: timeline]`.

```
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │    3. Lower cost per sales dollar  ◄── WEAKEST LINK
   │       (labor productivity, distribution density,
   │        own-brand plant volume, scanners)
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │    4. Funded low prices + full shelves + short lines
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │    5. More visits, higher sales/sq ft, higher regional share
   │            │
   │            ▼
   └─── 6. Higher operating profit per core region (denominator)
             → reinvest in step 2
```

- **Reaction without understanding:** price cuts with no cost controls behind them.
- **New direction after new direction:** discount conversions four years ago, then a chain-wide deep-discount program last year.
- **A big program to jump-start momentum:** the chain-wide conversion.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. Chain-wide deep-discount pricing that verified cost reductions don't pay for.
2. Operating and subsidizing stores and regions where the company cannot reach #1 or #2.
3. Paying out most profit as dividends while the store base ages.
4. Cutting store labor to the point of empty shelves and long checkout lines.
5. Pushing store brands at the expense of national-brand in-stock.
6. Remodeling or converting stores too small to be viable.
7. Keeping losing stores open to protect plant utilization.
8. Filling every top seat from inside by default.
9. Answering each competitor move, or the bid, with a new chain-wide program.

**Diagnosis on Collins's decline stages:**
- **Stage 1 (hubris):** history. The "largest and oldest" identity is the residue.
- **Stage 2 (undisciplined pursuit of more):** visible in the volume-first goal.
- **Stage 3 (denial of risk):** visible in the dividend policy and the conservative culture.
- **Stage 4 (grasping for salvation):** the company is here now, through the chain-wide price program.
- **Stage 5 (capitulation):** the danger is either a slow shrink with no plan behind it or a sale driven by exhaustion.
