# Good to Great: Brightwater Industries

**Verdict: a good company in crisis. Run this as a turnaround. Separate the pencil business, and become a disciplined toy-brand house.**

- **Hedgehog:** Brightwater keeps a small stable of owned and licensed children's toy brands selling for decades, and earns more profit per brand-year than anyone in its industry.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** cash to reinvest in fewer, bigger brands. Debt and the bankers control it.
- **Top 3 stop-doing:** chasing electronic games; diversifying outside toys; launching one-season novelties without a safety screen.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** the family feud threatens to split control while lenders already constrain the company.
- **Missing numbers that could change this:** toys vs. pencils profit, debt terms, Year −1 results, profit by toy line.
- **Confidence:** low to moderate. It rests on the dossier alone, with no segment profits. If pencils carry all the profit, the toy Hedgehog may be wrong.

Greatness potential: 25/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Brightwater is a focused toy company, no longer a toy-and-pencil conglomerate held together by a family truce. It owns or licenses a deliberately small number of children's brands: preschool classics, the decades-old build-a-face toy, the comic-strip licence, and possibly a cost-redesigned revival of its own former blockbuster action-figure brand. Each brand is run to last for years, not for one season. It is refreshed, advertised and replenished every year, and judged on profit per brand-year. The company passes up categories it cannot fund or win, such as electronic games. It does not diversify into businesses unrelated to toys. No product ships without a safety screen. Debt goes down before dividends return. The pencil business thrives separately under the person who loves it.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence (working hypothesis):** *Brightwater builds and sustains a small stable of children's toy brands that families buy year after year, and earns more profit per brand-year than any rival instead of betting on one-season hits.*

Pencils may be best-in-country, but they are a different business with a different passion owner (see Gate 3). |
| **Economic engine** | Profit per launch; profit per sales dollar; **profit per brand-year** | **Profit per brand-year.** It rewards durability, spreads tooling and advertising cost over many seasons, and punishes the one-shot launches behind the losses. `[ASSUMPTION]`: evergreen lines carry better lifetime margins than new launches. This must be confirmed with line-level data. |
| **Passion** | Son: toys (he has run the toy line for 6 years and is already pruning it). Uncle: pencils. | **Toys** for the company that the son leads. |

**Confirmation tests:**
1. Line-level P&L shows that brands older than three years earn more lifetime profit per year than launches under three years. If not, the denominator is wrong.
2. A separated toy company can service its debt on its own. If not, the Hedgehog Concept may only be viable with pencils kept.
3. Over the next two seasons, refreshed classics hold or grow retailer reorders.

**Verdict: CONDITIONAL.** The engine circle is unconfirmed because segment and line profits are missing.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   ┌──► 1. Fewer, durable brands (strict evergreen + safety criteria)
   │              │
   │              ▼
   │    2. Deeper investment per brand (refresh, TV advertising, replenishment)
   │              │
   │              ▼
   │    3. Higher sell-through → retailer reorders and shelf space year after year
   │              │
   │              ▼
   │    4. Rising PROFIT PER BRAND-YEAR (lower tooling/ad cost per unit over time)
   │              │
   │              ▼
   │    5. Debt paydown → restored banker confidence → investable cash
   │              │
   │              ▼
   └──── 6. Add one brand at a time that passes the evergreen test
          (licence, or revival of owned brand)
```

**Weakest link: step 2 → 5.** Deeper investment needs cash, and cash is controlled by lenders until debt falls. The flywheel has to start on savings from pruning products, not on new money.

**Current push (next 1–2 turns):**
1. Finish the product-line cuts that began in Year −2. Rank every line by profit per brand-year and cut the bottom tier `[ASSUMPTION: the cut threshold will be set once line P&L exists]`.
2. Redirect the freed advertising and tooling budget to the top three brands and the comic-strip licence.

**Doom-loop check:** the history shows clear signs. The nursery schools were a reaction to a proposed subsidy. Cookware was a celebrity-driven jump. Novelty launches like the lawn darts and water gun had no safety discipline. The fast-growing electronic-games category is now the obvious next salvation bet. A risk also sits on the other side: reviving the action figure as a single bet to "save the company" would recreate the old concentration in which one brand made about two-thirds of sales. Revive it only as one brand within the stable.

**Verdict: CONDITIONAL.** The loop closes, but the funding link is unproven until the debt terms are known.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop chasing electronic games.** It is the fastest-growing category, but Brightwater has no cash, no capability and no claim to be best there.
2. **Stop diversifying outside toys.** No more ventures like the nursery schools or the cookware line, and no more moves triggered by subsidies or celebrities.
3. **Stop launching one-season novelties without a safety screen.** The lawn darts and water gun brought recalls and bad press.
4. **Stop running two businesses that compete for one capital budget.** Separate pencils.
5. **Stop using the owned TV show as a de facto toy commercial.**
6. **Stop high new-product launch counts as a growth strategy.** Hold to the cap the son has started.
7. **Stop discussing dividends** until the debt targets are met.

## 7. Core ideology and the BHAG

- **Core purpose:** to make the toys that become part of childhood, and stay part of it across generations.
- **Core values:** (1) safe for children, always; (2) durability over novelty; (3) honest dealing with families, retailers and lenders; (4) family stewardship that serves the company, not the reverse.
- **BHAG (20 years):** *Every brand in Brightwater's stable is bought by a second generation of parents who played with it themselves.*
