## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Brightwater is a focused toy company. It is no longer a toy-and-pencil holding company, a nursery-school operator or a cookware brand. Most of its profit comes from a portfolio of character toys that sell year after year at prices parents don't think twice about. It has three sources of those characters: classics like the build-a-face toy, licensed comic-strip characters, and dormant brands it owns and relaunches in cheaper formats that use less plastic. It doesn't depend on one hit. No single brand dominates sales, and each line earns its shelf space on profit per brand-year. Cash pays down debt first and then funds brand refreshes and new licences. A safety gate stops products that could hurt a child or the company's name. The family dispute is resolved: the pencil business goes its own way under the uncle, and the toy company has one clear leader, a successor bench and independent directors. In Collins's terms, great means three things here. It outperforms toy-industry peers on return on capital for a decade or more. Its evergreen brands would leave a real gap if they vanished. And it survives both the current leader and the fading of any single brand.

**Reverse case:** if the toy business loses money even on its classics, the honest Hedgehog is the pencil business. In that case the family should invert the split, keeping pencils and selling or licensing out the toy brands.

**Current push (the next two turns):**
1. **Free the cash link.** Separate pencils on terms that don't strip the toy company of solvency. Restructure the debt. Cut the toy line to brands with positive profit per brand-year. The son has already started on this last step.
2. **Test one dormant-brand relaunch.** The action figure was discontinued because plastic costs rose. A smaller format that uses less plastic could bring back a brand that once made nearly two-thirds of sales, but only if its brand equity has survived `[ASSUMPTION]`. Test it with retailer pre-orders before committing TV spend. Cap its share of sales (see Gate 10).

**Doom-loop check:** the past decade shows clear doom-loop signs. New directions came one after another (nursery schools, cookware, a celebrity tie-in), launches were reactive and the company depended on one hit. Electronic games are the next likely "grasping for salvation" move. The son's pruning since Year −2 is the first consistent push in the right direction. The loop closes, but it depends on the Hedgehog Concept being confirmed.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop running toys and pencils under a disputed chain of command.** Separate them.
2. **Stop pursuing electronic games.** There is no capability, no cash, and Rival M has the scale. Revisit only after the debt is fixed, and then only through licensing, never through in-house development.
3. **Stop diversifying outside toys.** That means no schools, no cookware and no celebrity product lines.
4. **Stop launching any product that hasn't passed an independent safety and press-risk review.** No more lawn-dart- or water-gun-style novelties.
5. **Stop chasing hits with a high volume of new launches.** Cap new launches each year and require a multi-year brand case for each one.
6. **Stop expanding the TV show as an advertising vehicle.**
7. **Stop restoring the dividend before the debt targets are met,** even when the family wants it back.
