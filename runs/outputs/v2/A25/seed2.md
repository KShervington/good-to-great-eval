# Dunmore-Kessler Corporation: Good to Great Run

## Verdict

**Hedgehog (working hypothesis):** Dunmore-Kessler builds the most reliable systems for moving information. It proves them on space and defense programs, then sells them only into the commercial niches that pay for that reliability, including the printing trade it already leads.

**Weakest flywheel link:** turning government designs into profitable commercial products. Six years after the merger, this is still unproven.

**Top three stop-doing:**
1. No new acquisitions until last year's two earn their keep.
2. No new development money for hot-metal and other purely mechanical lines.
3. No head-on fights with electrical giants in products where Dunmore-Kessler has no edge.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** sales went from about $200M before the merger to $380M, but earnings have been weak for five years. The acquisitions bought revenue, not profit.

**Numbers that would change this:** segment profit and return on capital. If printing earns most of the profit at higher returns, the Hedgehog flips toward printing.

**Confidence:** low to moderate. It rests on the dossier alone, with no segment economics.

Greatness potential: 24/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

The great Dunmore-Kessler is one company, not a holding company of five. It is an engineering house for communications and information systems. Government programs pay for its hardest problems, and it reuses those designs in a few commercial niches where reliability earns a premium: broadcast and microwave links, data communications, and electronic systems for the graphic arts. In the graphic arts its press and finishing franchise gives it access to customers that no electronics competitor has. It earns more profit per engineer every year, it treats acquisitions as an occasional accelerator and not a growth engine, and senior leadership sits where the engine is. Mechanical lines that electronics will make obsolete are run for cash or sold. They are not defended. `[ASSUMPTION: this direction holds only if segment data show electronics can earn returns at least equal to printing; see Gate 5 decision rule.]`

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence (working hypothesis):** *Build the most reliable information-moving systems, proven on space programs, and sell them only where reliability pays, starting with the printing trade we already lead.*

| Circle | Candidates considered | Narrowed to | Ruled out |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Best in the world at** | (a) Printing presses and finishing; (b) space and defense tracking, telemetry and data systems; (c) commercial broadcast and communications broadly; (d) electronics applied to the graphic arts | **(b), carried into a few niches, plus (d).** (d) is the one combination no competitor holds: a leading press franchise plus space-grade data engineering. | (c) broadly: rivals are large conglomerates. Computer terminals as a stand-alone line `[ASSUMPTION]`. Corrugated machinery `[ASSUMPTION: no evidence of leadership]`. Mechanical presses as a long-term "best in world" bet, because leadership is domestic and the machines are mechanical. |
| **Economic engine** | Profit per government program; profit per installed press; profit per engineer; profit per reused design | **Profit per engineer.** The test is whether one engineer's government-funded know-how gets sold many times commercially. | Sales growth. It has risen for years while earnings stalled. |
| **Passion** | Hard technical problems; the press-making craft; the mission of space programs | **Solving hard reliability problems for demanding customers** `[ASSUMPTION: inferred from the engineering culture the company paid a premium for; not evidenced]` | Running a diversified portfolio for its own sake |

**Confirmation tests:**
- **Explains past wins?** The aerospace firm grew to $50M+ on extreme-reliability systems. This is partly confirmed.
- **Rules things out?** Yes. It excludes commodity commercial lines, mechanical-only R&D and diversification acquisitions.
- **Uncomfortable?** Yes. It treats the 80-year founding business as a channel and a cash source, not the center.

**Decision rule:**
- If segment data show printing earns the majority of profit at a higher return on capital than electronics, *and* electronic graphic-arts products cannot be built within about 2 years, rerun this gate with a printing-first Hedgehog and consider separating electronics.
- If government work earns most of the profit and commercial electronics loses money, narrow the Hedgehog further. Commercial products would then be limited to direct reuse of funded designs.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   ┌─► 1. Government funds the hardest comms/data problems
   │              │
   │              ▼
   │   2. Proven designs + deepening engineering talent
   │              │
   │              ▼
   │   3. Reuse designs in 2–3 commercial niches where reliability pays
   │      (broadcast/microwave links, data comms, electronic graphic arts)   ◄── WEAKEST LINK
   │              │
   │              ▼
   │   4. Rising PROFIT PER ENGINEER
   │              │
   │              ▼
   │   5. Self-funded R&D + ability to attract/keep the best engineers
   │              │
   │              ▼
   └── 6. Credibility to win more demanding programs
```

**Current push:**
1. Pick the two or three commercial niches and stop pushing everything else from government work into commercial markets.
2. Ship one electronic product for the graphic arts that printing customers actually buy. That one sale is the proof that link 3 works and that the two halves belong together.

**Doom-loop check:** several warning signs are present.
- Serial acquisitions: three in the merger era, two more last year, all while earnings stalled.
- Several integrations running at once.
- A "big program" logic, where the merger itself was meant to supply momentum.
- Leadership physically separated from the engine.

Treat the company as at risk of a doom loop, not in one.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop acquiring** until last year's $25.5M of cash purchases each report segment profit and are integrated.
2. **Stop new development on hot-metal typesetting** and other purely mechanical lines. Run them for cash.
3. **Stop competing head-on with electrical conglomerates** in commercial products where no proven government design gives an edge. TV cameras and stand-alone terminals are likely examples `[ASSUMPTION]`.
4. **Stop rewarding commercialization for its own sake.** Only niches that pass the Hedgehog count toward promotion.
5. **Stop managing by consolidated sales.** No major decision without segment profit and return on capital.
6. **Stop running two centers of gravity.** One leadership team, located with the engine.
7. **Stop treating corrugated machinery as core** unless the data show leadership and returns `[ASSUMPTION]`.
