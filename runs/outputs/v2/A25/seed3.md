# Dunmore-Kessler Corporation: Good to Great

## Verdict

**Hedgehog (working hypothesis):** Dunmore-Kessler can be best at turning government-funded communications and information technology into commercial systems for markets where information moves: broadcasting, communications links, and electronic printing and publishing. The engine is profit per engineer.

**Weakest flywheel link:** converting government technology into *profitable* commercial products. It is the company's stated strategy, and the dossier shows no proof that it works.

**Top 3 stop-doing:**
1. No new acquisitions until segment returns are known.
2. No development money for purely mechanical or hot-metal products.
3. Stop rewarding managers for product launches. Reward commercial profit.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** sales grew to $380M, but earnings have been flat for about five years. The company bought its growth; it did not earn it.

**Missing numbers that would change this:** segment profit and return on capital, government backlog, and printing market share.

**Confidence:** low to moderate. The verdict rests on a strategy the company itself states and on a known earnings stall, with no segment economics behind it.

Greatness potential: 25/100

---

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

The great Dunmore-Kessler is no longer a printing company that collects electronics businesses. It is one information-systems company. Government programs pay for the frontier engineering, and a disciplined transfer pipeline turns a few chosen technologies into commercial systems where technical edge decides who wins. Those markets are broadcast, point-to-point and data communications, and electronic prepress and press control for the printers it already serves. It enters only niches where it can lead. It reports and rewards profit per engineer and return on capital by segment. It stopped buying companies until each acquisition earned its keep, and it lets mechanical-only lines shrink or sell instead of defending them. Its earnings grow faster than its sales, so the stock becomes something investors seek out. It is no longer a currency the company spends to buy growth.

**Confirmation tests:** if this hedgehog is right, the evidence below should show it within two years.
1. Segment data shows government-derived commercial products earning above-company returns.
2. At least two transferred technologies reach meaningful commercial sales without price wars against conglomerates `[ASSUMPTION threshold: ≥$10M each]`.
3. Profit per engineer rises while the engineering headcount holds steady or grows.
4. Electronic printing products win orders from the existing press customer base.

**What would kill it:** transferred products only break even, or the profit sits entirely in mechanical printing. In that case the honest hedgehog is either "a focused government electronics contractor" or "a printing equipment leader", but not both.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   ┌─► 1. Government programs fund frontier engineering
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │   2. Engineers build core technologies
   │      (telemetry, data handling, radio/microwave links)
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │   3. Transfer pipeline picks the few with a commercial edge  ◄── WEAKEST LINK
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │   4. Commercial systems in niches where tech decides:
   │      broadcast, data comms, electronic prepress/press control
   │      (sold into the existing printing customer base)
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │   5. Higher profit per engineer → funds commercial R&D,
   │      attracts top engineers, steadies earnings vs budget swings
   │            │
   └────────────┘  (better engineers + track record win better programs)
```

**Current push (next one or two turns):**
1. Make link 3 real: one owner, a stage-gated pipeline, and two or three chosen bets instead of many launches.
2. Run the first transfer bet into the printing base (electronic typesetting or press controls). That is where the company already has customers, service networks and trust.

**Doom-loop check:** several signs are **present**:
- Six acquisitions in roughly 15–20 years, four of them in the last six years, two of those in the last year alone.
- Integration costs are cited as a drag on profit.
- About five years of weak earnings.
- A premium stock deal (target valued at about twice the company's own multiple).
- An incentive system that rewards new products, which drives a steady stream of new lines.

These are the classic markers of buying momentum instead of building it. The loop closes on paper but has not been proven in practice. Verdict: `CONDITIONAL`.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop acquiring** until segment profit and return on capital are published internally and each recent acquisition has a clean integration review. `[ASSUMPTION: about a 24-month moratorium]`
2. **Stop funding development** of purely mechanical presses and hot-metal typesetting beyond what existing customers need for service.
3. **Stop promoting managers for product launches.** Promote for commercial profit and return on capital from transferred technology.
4. **Stop paying premium multiples in stock** for earnings. With the stock already ignored, it dilutes shareholders and rewards buying growth.
5. **Stop entering commercial markets where scale decides.** Do not fight conglomerates on breadth, for example general studio equipment, unless a technical edge is demonstrable. `[ASSUMPTION]`
6. **Stop treating corrugated-paper machinery as core.** Review it for sale.
7. **Stop bidding on government programs with no reusable technology** unless the margin alone justifies them.

## 7. Core ideology and the BHAG

- **Core values `[ASSUMPTION]`:**
  - Engineering excellence that customers can measure.
  - Customer commitment that outlives a product cycle.
  - Candor about results.
  - Profit as proof of usefulness.
- **Core purpose:** to help people move and use information reliably, from orbit to the printed page.
- **BHAG (20-year horizon):** become the company that broadcasters, communications carriers and publishers count on for the electronics that move their information, with commercial markets earning most of the profit and government work serving as the R&D engine.
- **Mechanisms (clock building):**
  - Segment return-on-capital reviews.
  - A stage-gated technology-transfer pipeline.
  - Incentives tied to profit per engineer.
  - Successor development that includes electronics leadership.
  - An acquisition rule requiring hedgehog fit plus integration capacity.
