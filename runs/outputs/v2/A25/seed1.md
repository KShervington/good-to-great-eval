# Dunmore-Kessler Corporation: Good to Great

## Verdict

**Hedgehog (working hypothesis):** Take communications and data technology that government customers pay to develop, and turn it into dependable systems for professional information movers (broadcasters, communicators, publishers). Win on profit per engineering dollar.

**Weakest flywheel link:** Converting government technology into *profitable* commercial products. The evidence shows the intent but not the results.

**Top three stop-doing items:**
1. Making new acquisitions before the current five earn their keep.
2. Putting new capital into hot-metal typesetting and other purely mechanical lines.
3. Rewarding managers for launching commercial products rather than for the profit those products earn.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** For about five years, sales grew through acquisitions while earnings barely moved.

**Missing numbers that would change this:** Segment profit, return on capital by unit, government backlog, and printing market share.

**Confidence:** Low to moderate. It rests on the dossier's narrative, not on segment economics.

Greatness potential: 33/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

The great Dunmore-Kessler is no longer "a printing company that bought electronics." It is one engineering company with one engine. Government space and defense programs pay for hard engineering problems in tracking, telemetry, data handling and radio. The company then turns the best of that work into reliable commercial systems for customers whose business is moving information: broadcasters, point-to-point and microwave users, and newspaper and printing customers who are moving from metal and mechanics to electronic text and controls.

The printing heritage survives where electronics transform it. Commodity mechanical lines are harvested or sold. Every unit reports its own return on capital. Acquisitions stop until the ones already made produce profit. Earnings then grow faster than sales, year after year, through budget cycles. That consistency is what finally earns the attention of an indifferent market.

**Denominator:** operating profit per dollar of total engineering spend, tracked by business unit.

**Why this candidate and not "back to printing":** The printing business may well be today's main source of profit. If segment data shows that, this hypothesis must be revisited. But a largely mechanical product line facing electronic substitution `[ASSUMPTION about industry direction]` cannot be the long-run "best in the world" circle on its own.

**Confirmation tests (12–24 months):**
1. Commercial products derived from government work earn margins at or above the company average `[ASSUMPTION threshold]`.
2. Printing lines with electronic content outperform the purely mechanical lines on ROIC.
3. Engineering talent from the aerospace merger is retained. Talent was half the reason for paying about twice the company's own multiple.
4. At least two transfer products win against conglomerate competitors on reliability or performance, not on price.

**Does it explain past wins?** Partly. It explains why the company was willing to pay a premium for the aerospace firm. It does not yet explain any profit, because there is no profit evidence.

**Is it uncomfortable to commit to?** Yes. It demotes most of the founding printing business to "electronify it or harvest it," and that decision falls hardest on a Great Lakes headquarters built on printing.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop acquiring** until each acquisition from the past six years shows unit profit at or above the company's cost of capital `[ASSUMPTION: threshold]`, or 18–24 months pass with a board review.
2. **Stop investing new capital in hot-metal typesetting.** Maintain it for existing customers and plan the harvest.
3. **Stop promoting managers for the *number* of commercial products derived from defense work.** Promote them for commercial *profit* on those products.
4. **Stop paying premium stock multiples for talent or earnings.** Any future deal is cash-or-stock at a multiple the existing flywheel can earn back.
5. **Stop bidding government programs that neither earn a solid margin on their own nor feed a commercial product line.**
6. **Stop competing feature-for-feature with large conglomerates in commodity broadcast equipment** where there is no reliability or technology edge.
7. **Stop treating the whole printing portfolio as one unit.** Split it into electronic-transformation candidates and harvest lines.
