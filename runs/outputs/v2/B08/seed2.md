# Meridian Radio Corporation: Good to Great run (as of late summer, Year 0)

## Verdict

**Already great, so this run is in stewardship mode. The catch: Meridian's greatness rests on an outcome, trusted quality, while its leaders are defending a method, hand-wiring.**

- **Hedgehog:** Meridian makes the TV that American families, dealers and repairmen trust most. It earns a quality premium on every set, using whatever technology best delivers that trust.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** reinvestment. Money flows to hand-wiring, inspection, pay TV and a share race against Rival R. Next-generation reliability engineering gets little.
- **Temptations to refuse:** (1) a debt-funded race to pass Rival R in color share; (2) treating hand-wiring as the brand's identity; (3) open-ended pay-TV spending.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** leaders publicly dismiss both low-priced imports and integrated circuits. Those are the two forces most able to erase Meridian's cost and quality edge.
- **Numbers that would change this:** cost and service-call rates for hand-wired versus printed-circuit sets; import volumes and price points; full-year results, debt and capital spending for Years −5 to −1.
- **Confidence:** moderate on direction, low on magnitude. The verdict rests on one quarter, one annual snapshot from Year −6, and the stock record.

Greatness potential: 25/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Meridian is the most trusted name in American television. The trust is earned on field reliability, ease of service and dealer economics, not on a particular way of building a chassis. It moves deliberately into printed circuits, solid state and eventually integrated circuits. It is not first, but it ships each technology once it beats the old one on reliability. It designs those sets around modular, swap-in boards that keep repairmen as its loudest advocates. It adds color capacity in step with dealer sell-through, not to win a share contest with Rival R, and it keeps the balance sheet that let it grow without debt. Low-priced imports are tracked quarterly as a threat, not waved off. Leadership has a named successor generation. Technical bets are settled by tests rather than public pronouncements. Pay TV is either a funded, bounded option or gone.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   (1) Reliable, easy-to-service sets
            │
            ▼
   (2) Repairmen & dealers recommend Meridian
            │
            ▼
   (3) Quality premium + dealer loyalty  ──►  PROFIT PER SET
            │
            ▼
   (4) Tight inventory & dealer-stock control keeps dealers profitable
            │
            ▼
   (5) Reinvest in reliability engineering for the NEXT technology   ◄── weakest link
            │
            └──────────────► back to (1)
```

The loop closes. Its weakest link is step 5. Today's reinvestment goes into the old method (hand-wiring plus about one inspector per six production workers), into pay TV, and into capacity meant to pass Rival R. If step 5 stays aimed backward, step 1 will erode as solid state matures. Step 3 will then be squeezed from below by imports.

**Current push:**
1. Build a printed-circuit, solid-state color chassis with modular, swappable boards. It must beat the hand-wired chassis on field service calls and keep repairmen loyal.
2. Add color capacity in tranches tied to dealer sell-through, with a borrowing ceiling.

**Doom-loop check:**
- *Big leap to jump-start momentum:* the new color-tube plant and the push to be No. 1 in color fit this pattern if they are sized to a share target rather than demand.
- *Reaction without understanding:* the dismissal of imports and integrated circuits is the reverse problem, a refusal to react. That is still a doom-loop precursor.
- *New direction after new direction:* none visible.
- *Inconsistency:* none visible.

## 5. Stop-doing list (stewardship mode: temptations to refuse)

1. **Refuse the share race.** Do not size color capacity or debt to pass Rival R. Size them to dealer sell-through and profit per set.
2. **Stop treating hand-wiring as the identity.** Market *reliability and serviceability*, the outcomes. Let the method follow test results.
3. **Stop open-ended pay-TV funding.** Set a dated decision gate tied to the regulatory ruling and cap the spend. If it isn't approved by the gate, exit.
4. **Stop dismissing imports in public and in private.** Replace opinion with quarterly data. Do not answer them with a price war either, because that falls outside the Hedgehog.
5. **Stop pronouncing on unproven technology.** Settle integrated-circuit and solid-state readiness with reliability tests, not leadership statements.

**Non-negotiable disciplines:**
- Every model is measured on first-year service calls.
- Dealer inventory weeks are reviewed monthly.
- No capacity tranche is approved without sell-through evidence.

**Framework boundaries:**
- *Freedom:* engineering may pick any technology that beats the reliability benchmark.
- *Required:* dealers' economics come first, and profit per set is the scorecard.

**Bureaucracy check:** heavy end-of-line inspection may be compensating for a production method that can't build quality in. Test this before cutting anything.
