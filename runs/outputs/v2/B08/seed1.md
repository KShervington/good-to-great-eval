# Good to Great: Meridian Radio Corporation, as of late summer, Year 0

## Verdict

**Meridian is already great, and it shows early signs of decline.** This run is in stewardship mode.

- **Hedgehog Concept (confirmed and restated):** Meridian builds the American TV and radio that dealers and repairmen trust most to work, to last and to be fixed, and it earns more profit per dealer for it than anyone else.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** reliability currently comes from hand-wiring and inspection rather than from design. That approach is expensive and tied to an aging technology.
- **Temptations to refuse:**
  1. A debt-funded race to overtake Rival R in color units.
  2. Treating hand-wiring as the value itself when it is only a method.
  3. Widening pay TV before the regulators decide.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** leadership publicly dismisses integrated circuits and low-cost imports. Those are the two forces that attack cost per set.
- **Numbers that would change this:** cost and field-failure rates for hand-wired versus printed-circuit sets; import volumes and prices; debt and capital spending; color market share.
- **Confidence:** moderate on direction and low on magnitudes. The verdict rests on one quarter, the Year −6 figures and the stock record.

Greatness potential: 35/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

In its great version, Meridian is still the set the repairman recommends. The difference is that its quality is engineered in rather than inspected in. It builds solid-state, modular chassis that an independent repairman can diagnose and fix faster than any rival's. It proves its reliability with field data collected from its own service network instead of relying on the "hand-crafted" story. It grows color as fast as its dealers can sell through without inventory piling up, and it funds that growth mostly from internal cash, not a borrowing binge at the top of a boom. It knows what low-cost imports really cost to build, because it buys and tears them down, and it decides on purpose where it will compete and where it will not. Its top team includes a senior engineer with real authority, and a named succession plan exists. Its value comes from leadership in profit per dealer and in trust, not from being No. 1 in units.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence (working hypothesis):** *Meridian builds the American TV and radio that dealers and repairmen trust most to work, to last and to be fixed, and it earns more profit per dealer for it than anyone else.*

| **Economic engine** | **Denominator: profit per franchised dealer** `[ASSUMPTION: chosen because sell-through, inventory discipline and repairman referral all land at the dealer]`. The alternative is profit per set sold. | Unit share for its own sake. |
| **Deeply passionate about** | Quality that holds up in the home, and practical engineering firsts that make home life easier (portable radio, an early wireless remote). | Producing at the lowest possible cost. |

**The stewardship-mode twist.** Meridian already lives by this concept, but it has confused the concept with one way of carrying it out. The concept is *trusted and serviceable*, and hand-wiring is one method of achieving that. If a printed-circuit or solid-state chassis designed for serviceability matches or beats hand-wired field reliability, then that new chassis is the Hedgehog Concept, and the soldering iron was never the point.

**Confirmation tests (what would turn the CONDITIONAL into a PASS):**
1. Profit per dealer is flat or rising over Years −5 to 0. This needs dealer counts and full-year profit.
2. Field failure and service-call rates for Meridian sets are better than rivals' sets, including printed-circuit sets, as reported by the repairman network.
3. Dealers report that repairman recommendations drive a measurable share of Meridian sales.

**Uncomfortable implications:** the concept does *not* require being No. 1 in color units. It also requires putting hand-wiring on trial.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   ▼                                              │
[1] Reliable, serviceable sets                    │
   │                                              │
   ▼                                              │
[2] Repairmen recommend & dealers trust Meridian  │
   │                                              │
   ▼                                              │
[3] Fast dealer sell-through, tight inventory     │
   │                                              │
   ▼                                              │
[4] Price premium → profit per dealer (denominator)
   │                                              │
   ▼                                              │
[5] Reinvest in reliability engineering ──────────┘
    (solid-state, modular, service-first design)
```

**Weakest link: step 5 → step 1.** Reinvestment today pays for hand-wiring and inspection. About one production worker in six is an inspector. That spending protects step 1 at a cost that grows in line with volume. Meanwhile the industry is moving to printed circuits, transistors and eventually integrated circuits, so the method that feeds step 1 is getting more expensive just as its rivals' costs fall.

**Current push (the next two turns):**
1. **Instrument step 2.** Collect structured service-call and failure data from the repairman network covering Meridian, Rival R and imported sets. Today the reliability claim rests on reputation, not on measurement.
2. **Rebuild step 5 → step 1.** Start a serviceable solid-state chassis program whose acceptance test is field reliability and repair time, not whether the set is hand-wired.

**Doom-loop check:**
- *Big program to jump-start momentum:* there is a risk here. The goal of overtaking Rival R in color, possibly funded with debt, is a capacity-driven leap rather than a flywheel turn.
- *Long-running side bet:* pay TV has absorbed years of spending, and the outcome depends on regulators.
- *New direction after new direction:* not seen. The core has been consistent.
- *Reaction without understanding:* not seen yet. The opposite failure, refusing to react, is present (imports, integrated circuits).

**Verdict: PASS.** The loop closes.

## 5. Temptations to refuse (stewardship mode)

1. **A race to No. 1 in color units, especially if it is debt-funded.** Grow color capacity to match dealer sell-through and free cash, not to match Rival R.
2. **Making hand-wiring sacred.** Keep the promise (reliable and serviceable) and let the method change when field data supports it.
3. **Expanding pay TV before regulatory approval.** Cap spending at the current test until the regulators decide.
4. **Matching import prices by cutting reliability.** If Meridian enters the low end, it does so with a designed-for-cost set that still meets its reliability standard, or it doesn't enter at all.
5. **Scaling inspection along with volume.** Avoid adding inspectors in proportion to output as the way to protect quality.

## 7. Core ideology and the BHAG

**Core values:**
1. Products that keep working in the home.
2. Straight dealing with dealers and repairmen.
3. Practical engineering firsts that make home life easier.
4. Craftsmanship pride, meaning pride in the outcome, not attachment to a technique.

"U.S. manufacturing" is a candidate value, but it should be tested explicitly rather than assumed. If it is kept, it has to be paid for through automation, not through margin.

**Core purpose:** *To bring dependable home entertainment that people and the people who fix it can trust for years.*

**BHAG (about 20-year horizon):** *Meridian is the brand American households and repairmen name first for dependability on every screen in the home, whatever technology is inside it.*

**Clock-building mechanisms:**
- Field-reliability data from the service network, reviewed each quarter.
- A senior engineering seat with top-team authority.
- A board-owned succession plan for both top roles.
- A capital rule tying capacity additions to dealer sell-through.
