# Continental Provision Company: Good to Great Run

## Verdict

**A fallen great, deep in decline.** It is in Collins's Stage 4 (grasping for salvation). The path to great runs only through a smaller, rebuilt company.

**Hedgehog (working hypothesis):** *Be the #1 or #2 grocer in a handful of dense regions, in modern stores, winning on store-brand value made in our own plants, measured by profit per regional market.*

**Weakest flywheel link:** reinvesting in modern stores. The dividend-payout habit blocks it, and so does a foundation owner that relies on those dividends.

**Stop doing:**
1. The chainwide deep-discount price war.
2. Paying out most profit as dividends while the stores age.
3. Running small, old stores in markets where it isn't #1 or #2.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** on a 1-cent margin, cutting prices across an obsolete store base can't beat rivals who simply match the cuts. That is this year's ~$51M loss.

**Missing numbers that would change this:** profit by store size and age, market share by region, and results of any large-store tests.

Greatness potential: 8/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

The great Continental Provision is smaller, younger in its stores and older in its wisdom. It operates in a deliberately limited set of regions where it is the #1 or #2 grocer. Every store there is large enough to run efficiently, well staffed, and fully stocked with both national brands and its own store brands. Those store brands come from its own plants, sized to what the stores actually sell, and they give it a cost and quality edge rivals can't copy by matching a price list. It funds new stores from its own cash flow and its unused borrowing capacity, not from a one-time windfall. Its foundation owner backs a capital plan rather than an income stream. It doesn't win by being the cheapest everywhere. It wins by being the store neighborhoods in its regions would miss most.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence (working hypothesis):** *Be the #1 or #2 grocer in a handful of dense regions, in modern stores, winning on store-brand value made in our own plants, measured by profit per regional market.*

| Circle | Candidates considered | Kept | Ruled out, and why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Best in the world at** | National price leader; biggest chain; modern specialty superstores; vertically integrated store brands; dense regional operations | **Store-brand value from its own manufacturing**, applied **in regions where it has density** | *Price leader:* competitors matched immediately, and its costs are worse. *Biggest:* size without density is what broke it. *Specialty superstores:* Rival L is already ahead. |
| **Economic engine (denominator)** | Profit per store; per sq ft; per customer visit; **per regional market** | **Profit per regional market** | Profit per store would push toward cherry-picking stores. In grocery, distribution, advertising and supervision costs are shared across a region, so density is the real lever `[ASSUMPTION]`. |
| **Passion** | Heritage and pride in "the original"; making food; serving neighborhoods | Making and selling good food at fair value `[ASSUMPTION]`: the dossier is silent on what the people care about | "Being the biggest" is pride, not passion, and it is what got the company here. |

**Tests:**
- **Explains past wins?** Partly. The old dominance rested on buying scale and its own brands. What has changed is that scale spread thin no longer delivers the cost edge.
- **Rules things out?** Yes: whole regions, small stores, chainwide discounting, and plant volume for its own sake.
- **Uncomfortable?** Very. It means a much smaller chain, a dividend cut, and admitting that the biggest-chain identity is over.

**What would confirm it:**
1. Store and market P&L shows a profitable core of regions where the company has share.
2. Store-brand gross margin beats the national-brand equivalent at competitive shelf prices.
3. A modern-format pilot in a core region beats the old stores on profit per sq ft within 12 months.

```
        ▼                                              │

        │                                              │
        ▼                                              │
 [6] PROFIT PER REGIONAL MARKET ───────────────────────┘
        funds the next store replacement in the same region
```

**Weakest link:** [2], reinvestment. Today's cash goes to dividends, and the current price cuts consume the rest.

**Current push (next two turns):**
1. Stop the bleeding at [6]. End chainwide deep discounting, and use targeted pricing only in core markets.
2. Restart [2]. Redirect dividend cash, and some of the company's untouched debt capacity, into replacing stores in the strongest one or two regions.

**Doom-loop check:** the company is clearly in a doom loop. It converted to discount store by store four years ago, then went all in chainwide last year. It left the West Coast as a retreat rather than as part of a strategy. Now a takeover bid demands fast results. That is new direction after new direction, big programs meant to jump-start momentum, and reaction without understanding. The next "savior" move, whether a celebrity CEO, a bigger price cut or a merger, would continue the loop.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop the chainwide deep-discount program.** Price only where density makes it pay.
2. **Stop paying out most profit as dividends** while the store base ages.
3. **Stop operating stores under ~8,000 sq ft** that can't be relocated or enlarged, and stop staying in markets where the company isn't, and can't become, #1 or #2.
4. **Stop cutting store staff to fund price cuts.** Long lines and empty shelves undo whatever the low prices win.
5. **Stop pushing store brands at the expense of national-brand availability.** Store brands should win the shelf on value, not by default.
6. **Stop running the plants for volume.** Size production to what the core stores sell `[ASSUMPTION: plant fixed costs are adding pressure for volume]`.
7. **Stop making defensive moves aimed at the stock price.** Respond to the tender offer with a funded plan, or not at all.
8. **Stop treating "largest grocery chain in the country" as a goal.**

It is marked CONDITIONAL pending store and market P&L.

1. Profit per regional market sustained above rivals'.
2. In its regions, it is the store neighborhoods would miss most.
