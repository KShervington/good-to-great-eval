## Verdict

**Calder is good at growing sales but not at making money, and it is spread across too many fronts. The great version of Calder is narrower than the current one.**

- **Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** Calder becomes the best in the world at automating how banks and accounting departments handle money, with systems simple enough to run without large programming staffs. It measures itself by profit per installed financial customer account.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** converting the installed base into profit. Sales quadrupled, yet net earnings fell from about $12M to about $8M.
- **Top three stop-doing items:** (1) racing Rival I across the whole computer market; (2) buying entry into new fields; (3) promising margin recovery without a date.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** nothing in the evidence shows which business lines make money.
- **Missing numbers that would change this answer:** profit by line, earnings for Years −3 to −1, R&D spending in dollars, and market share in bank automation.

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

The great Calder no longer presents itself as a smaller, cheaper version of Rival I. It owns the money-handling back office of banks and businesses. Its machines read the magnetic ink on checks and sort them. It prints the checks and supplies the ink. Its bookkeeping machines and computers post the accounts. All of it works with plain business English rather than teams of programmers. Each bank or accounting department that installs one Calder system keeps adding more, and pays rent and buys supplies every month. That recurring profit per account pays for focused R&D, which makes the next system easier and harder to replace. Margins move back toward the double digits the company earned 16 years ago. The adding-machine line is run for cash, defense is contained, and general-purpose computing outside the financial back office is deliberately left to others. The company is run through a leadership team and a set of reporting mechanisms, not through one forceful executive, so it keeps working after he is gone.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence:** *Calder is the best in the world at automating how banks and accounting departments handle money, with systems simple enough to run without armies of programmers, and it measures itself by profit per installed financial customer account.*

| Circle | Candidates considered | Kept | Rejected, and why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Best in world at** | General-purpose computers; mechanical adding machines; business forms; military computing; bank and accounting automation; easy-to-program computers | **Money-handling automation for banks and accounting departments, plus ease of programming as the edge** | General computers: Rival I has a large lead in scale and technology. Adding machines: a shrinking 6% of sales. Forms: a commodity on their own. Defense: no evidence Calder is best-in-world there [ASSUMPTION] |
| **Economic engine denominator** | Profit per machine sold; profit per check processed; **profit per installed financial customer account** | **Profit per installed financial customer account** (rentals + magnetic ink + printed checks and forms + accounting systems per bank or department) | Per machine sold: it rewards one-time unit sales, which is the pattern that came with collapsing margins. Per check is a strong alternate; test both once data exists |
| **Passion** | Serving the people who keep the books (a 75-year identity); engineering pride in the easy-to-program machine; the EVP's drive to sell and expand | **Making money-handling accurate and simple for the people who handle it** [ASSUMPTION: the dossier has no evidence on culture] | "Being a computer company" for its own sake |

**Why it fits:**
- **It explains past wins.** The founding product served accountants. Calder was an early entrant in check processing. Its computer's selling point is that it cuts programming staff, which matters most to cost-conscious financial back offices.
- **It rules things out.** It excludes the whole-market computer fight, non-financial acquisitions and expansion in defense.
- **It is uncomfortable.** It gives up the "full-line computer company" ambition that both recent CEOs pursued.

**Confirmation tests (working hypothesis until these are met):**
1. Profit by line shows that bank automation and accounting systems earn above the company average.
2. Computer installations at banks and accounting customers show better margins and retention than other computer installations.
3. Accounts that hold two or more Calder product types (for example, sorter + ink + checks) earn materially more per account than accounts with one product.

**Current push (next one or two turns):**
1. **Make link 4 visible.** Build profit per account and profit by line. Today nothing in the evidence shows that this link works.
2. **Narrow link 5.** Redirect computer R&D toward financial and business-accounting users, and stop funding parity with Rival I across the whole market.

**Doom-loop check (signs already present):**
- **New direction after new direction.** Two successive CEOs reshaped the company away from its core.
- **Acquisitions to jump-start momentum.** Calder bought a computer maker and forms printers. Even so, observers still call the product line narrow, so diversification has not reached its own stated goal.
- **Reaction without understanding.** The company says profits will recover once R&D is written off, but gives no timeline and no line-level data.
- **Inconsistency at the top.** A computer partnership was agreed with the partner and then left to lapse at Calder's own board.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop competing with Rival I across the whole general-purpose computer market** (scientific and large-scale computing outside the financial back office).
2. **Stop acquiring companies to enter new fields** until the hedgehog's confirmation tests are met.
3. **Stop telling shareholders and staff that profits will recover once R&D is written off, without a date.** Replace that message with a dated margin plan.
4. **Stop investing in development of mechanical adding machines.** Harvest the line.
5. **Stop growing the defense division as a strategic pillar** unless profit data justifies it.
6. **Stop taking non-financial print work** that ties up capacity [ASSUMPTION].
7. **Stop letting strategic decisions lapse without a recorded yes or no** at board level.
