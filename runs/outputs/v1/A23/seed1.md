# Good to Great: Calder Business Machines Company

## Verdict

**Calder is good but not yet great, and it is drifting.**

- **Hedgehog (working hypothesis):** Be the world's best at automating how banks and accounting departments process money and records, measured by *profit per financial customer*.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** turning installations into profit. Nobody can show which line makes money.
- **Stop doing:**
  1. Racing Rival I across the whole computer market.
  2. Funding R&D with no payback date.
  3. Buying sales growth through acquisitions.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** sales quadrupled ($94M to $389M), but net earnings *fell*, from about $12M to about $8M. "Profits return once R&D is written off" is a hope, not a plan.
- **Numbers that would change this answer:** profit by line, earnings for Years −3 to −1, R&D dollars, and share in bank automation.

Greatness potential: 30/100

---

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Calder stops trying to be a smaller Rival I. It becomes the company every bank and corporate accounting department thinks of first when money and records have to move accurately and cheaply. It sells check sorters, magnetic ink, bank bookkeeping machines, and the checks and forms that pass through them. It also sells computers that bank and accounting staff can program in plain business language, so customers need fewer expensive programmers. Its 75 years with accountants, its early lead in check processing and its international sales network all feed one engine: each financial customer gets more profitable every year as rentals, consumables and upgrades build on each other. It reports profit line by line, gives every R&D dollar a payback date, and keeps a management bench strong enough that it does not depend on one forceful executive.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence:** *Calder will be the best in the world at automating how financial institutions and accounting departments process money and records, and will measure itself by profit per financial customer.*

| Circle | Candidates considered | Kept | Ruled out, and why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Best in the world at** | General-purpose computers; easy-to-program computers; military computing; financial-record automation; international distribution | **Financial-record automation** (bank automation, accounting systems, checks and forms, business-language programming) | General computers: Rival I has a 12-year scale lead dating from the air-defense contract. Military: a separate division with no evident edge `[ASSUMPTION]`. Distribution is an accelerator, not a hedgehog. Ease of programming is a real edge, but on its own Rival I can copy it. |
| **Economic engine** | Profit per machine sold; per rental installation; per check processed; per financial customer | **Profit per financial customer** (rentals + inks + checks/forms + upgrades) | Per machine sold rewards volume, and volume is what produced the margin collapse. Per check processed is a strong second candidate; test both once line data exists. |
| **Passion** | Accountant heritage; the large computer's ease-of-use breakthrough; global selling | **Making the money-keepers' work accurate and simple** `[ASSUMPTION: inferred from 75-year heritage, a 40-year-tenure chairman, and an EVP who built international sales]` | The dossier has no evidence of passion for defense or scientific computing. |

**Why it passes the three tests:**
- **It explains past wins.** These include 75 years with bookkeepers, early entry into check processing, and forms acquisitions that sell consumables to the same customers.
- **It rules things out.** It excludes a head-to-head general computer race and growth through unrelated acquisitions.
- **It is uncomfortable.** It means publicly giving up the "whole computer market" ambition and capping the military division.

**Confirmation tests (working hypothesis):**
1. Line-level profit shows that banking and accounting systems earn more per dollar of R&D than general-purpose computers.
2. Bank customers renew rentals and expand their consumables purchases.
3. Calder wins a rising share of new bank-automation installations.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   ┌─► 1. 75 years of trust with banks & accounting departments
   │        (+ international sales organization)
   │                     │
   │                     ▼
   │   2. Install systems that are easier to program and run
   │      (cuts customers' programmer & clerk costs)
   │                     │
   │                     ▼
   │   3. Recurring revenue: rentals + magnetic ink + checks & forms
   │                     │
   │                     ▼
   │   4. PROFIT PER FINANCIAL CUSTOMER rises   ◄── WEAKEST LINK
   │                     │
   │                     ▼
   │   5. R&D focused only on financial processing
   │      (better/cheaper than generalists' offerings)
   │                     │
   │                     ▼
   └── 6. Reputation as THE financial specialist, worldwide
```

**Current push:**
1. Build a profit-and-loss statement for each line, and set rental and consumables terms so that every bank customer is profitable. This is link 4.
2. Point the computer sales force at banks and accounting departments instead of the general market. This is link 2.

**Doom-loop check:** several warning signs are present.
- Growth has been bought through acquisitions: forms printers and the small computer maker.
- Direction has changed repeatedly, from adding machines to "diversified" to computers.
- The computer partnership lapsed because the board did not act.
- The future profit recovery is promised with no date.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. Competing across the whole general-purpose computer market against Rival I.
2. Funding any R&D project without an owner, a cost ceiling and a payback date.
3. Acquiring businesses to grow sales. Acquire only if a deal raises profit per financial customer.
4. Reinvesting in mechanical adding machines. Harvest them.
5. Expanding the military division beyond contracts that fund themselves.
6. Reporting only consolidated results. Report by line, internally, every month.
7. Treating scientific and engineering computing as a target market.
8. Cutting costs across the board to rebuild margins. Cut outside the hedgehog and protect service to bank customers.
