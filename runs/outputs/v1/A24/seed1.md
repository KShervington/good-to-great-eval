# Good to Great: Halstead Motors Corporation

## Verdict

**Halstead is not good yet. It is a declining incumbent in a survival crisis, so this run stops at Gate 1.**

- **Hedgehog Concept:** none yet. Writing one now would be strategy by slogan.
- **Weakest link:** there is no flywheel. The loop runs backward: product planning misses demand, inventory piles up, losses grow, quality and credibility fall, and the next plan misses again.
- **Stop now** (survival disciplines, not a Hedgehog list):
  1. Building cars to keep plants running instead of to demand.
  2. Treating aid, a merger, publicity or next year's launch as the rescue. The cash plan is the rescue.
  3. Shipping defects to protect volume.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** Halstead may run out of money by year-end, and private lenders have already said no.
- **Missing numbers that would change the answer:** cash and total debt, break-even volume, profit by vehicle line, defense-unit profit, third-quarter results.

`Greatness potential: 6/100`

```
Product plan misjudges demand
        ↓
Wrong mix in the field → 95 days of inventory
        ↓
Discounting / losses → capital markets close
        ↓
Cost pressure + regulatory load → quality and recalls suffer
        ↓
Reputation and share fall (21% → 9%)
        ↓
Smaller volume, less money to plan the next line → (back to top)
```

Breaking this loop is the job for the next year. Building a flywheel comes after that.

## 5. Stop now (survival disciplines)

These do not depend on any Hedgehog Concept. They are good practice for any automaker in Halstead's position.

1. **Stop building to plant capacity.** Schedule production to retail demand until dealer supply is normal. The normal level is `[ASSUMPTION]` at or below 60 days.
2. **Stop looking for one rescue.** Government aid, merger talk, a celebrity launch and a new car family can each buy time. None of them is a plan.
3. **Stop shipping defects to hit volume.** A quality hold on a car costs less than another recall headline.
4. **Stop swinging the whole lineup each time demand shifts.** Halstead has been caught on the wrong side three times running. That pattern points to the planning process, not to bad luck.
5. **Stop promising that every brand, plant and line will survive** before profit by line is known. Don't cut blind either: decide on the numbers.

## 9. Next 90 days (toward good)

Each action pushes on breaking the doom loop.

1. **Know the cash.** Build a 13-week cash forecast, a full debt and liability map, and a stated break-even volume, and share them with the board and prospective guarantors. *Owner: finance chief (seat undocumented; fill it first if empty).*
2. **Close survival financing tied to milestones.** Seek a loan guarantee whose conditions match the milestones below. Make concessions visibly shared across union, suppliers, lenders, *and management pay*. *Owner: CEO.*
3. **Clear the inventory.** Cut production to bring dealer supply to ≤60 days `[ASSUMPTION]`, and keep schedules tied to retail sales. *Owner: head of manufacturing, with sales.*
4. **Ring-fence the front-wheel-drive compact launch.** Protect its funding and schedule, and add hard quality gates. Treat it as the proof that Halstead can build the right car well, not as the rescue. *Owner: head of engineering and product.*
5. **Get profit by line, including defense.** Decide which brands, lines and assets to keep, shrink, sell or close. The defense business ($625M, about 4.6% of sales) could be a stable earner or a source of cash: decide on its numbers, not under pressure. *Owner: finance chief, with CEO.*
