# Good to Great: Marlow Discount Centers

## Verdict

**Good, not yet great, and drifting.** Marlow has a 35-year operating base but no Hedgehog Concept. "Lowest price, opened fast, in many places" is a tactic, not a strategy.

- **Hedgehog (working hypothesis):** Be the first-choice, lowest-price store for TVs, audio and major appliances in a few markets Marlow dominates, and grow only by deepening them, measured in profit per market.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** local density. Stores spread across distant regions cannot spread advertising cost.
- **Top three to stop:**
  1. Multi-store blitz entries into new regions.
  2. Opening stores before per-market profit is proven.
  3. Treating parent capital as free.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** Growth is paid for by a steelmaker for whom retail isn't core. Meanwhile Rival T is moving to a bigger format and buying its way into the largest market.
- **Missing numbers that would change this:** profit and share by market, same-store sales, and the parent's multi-year capital commitment.

Greatness potential: 15/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Marlow is not a national chain. It is the default destination for TVs, audio and major appliances in a small number of metro markets, starting with its home region. In those markets its name is known, its store density lets one advertising budget carry many stores, and its buying volume supports prices competitors can't easily match. It enters a new market only when it can reach density there within a few years. It reaches density mostly by filling in near existing strength, not by blitzing distant cities. It knows to the dollar which markets earn their keep. Its parent treats it as a disciplined investment with clear return hurdles, not as a growth experiment. And it would keep working without the current president, because the market-entry rules and per-market scorekeeping run the company. `[ASSUMPTION: the inferred economics of an advertising-heavy discount retailer]`

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Denominator:** operating profit per market (metro area).

**Confirmation tests** (all need data that isn't in the dossier):
1. Home-region markets earn more profit per store than the Rocky Mountain and Southwestern markets do.
2. Markets with more Marlow stores show lower advertising cost as a share of sales.
3. Markets where Marlow leads on share also earn the highest profit per market.

If these tests fail, the concept is wrong. The honest fallback may then be that Marlow can only be good, and the parent should treat it as a cash-yielding holding rather than a growth bet.

## 3. The flywheel

```
  Store density in a market
            │
            ▼
  Ad spend spread over more stores ──► lower ad cost per sale
            │
            ▼
  Sharper prices and more promotion per dollar
            │
            ▼
  Traffic, volume, local share
            │
            ▼
  Buying power + profit per market (denominator)
            │
            ▼
  Reinvest in fill-in stores in the SAME market ──► (back to density)
```

**Weakest link:** density. Today the footprint is the home region, a scattered set of Rocky Mountain and Southwestern stores, and new blitz markets. Every new region restarts the wheel from zero.

**Current push:**
1. Measure profit per market everywhere.
2. Redirect new capital into fill-in stores in the two or three best markets.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop** simultaneous multi-store entries into new, distant regions.
2. **Stop** opening in any market that can't reach target density within about three years `[ASSUMPTION: horizon to be set from data]`.
3. **Stop** sizing ad blitzes to buy awareness where the store count can't carry the spend.
4. **Stop** treating parent capital as free. No opening goes ahead without a per-market return hurdle.
5. **Stop** fighting price wars in markets where Marlow isn't, and can't become, the share leader.
6. **Stop** adding or dropping categories without per-market profit evidence.
7. **Stop** keeping isolated, orphan stores because of what has already been spent on them.
