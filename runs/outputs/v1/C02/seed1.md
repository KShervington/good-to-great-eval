# Good to Great: Meridian Surgical Corporation

**Verdict: Good, not yet great. Meridian has a real engine and a narrow lead, but it depends heavily on one founder and one technology.**

**Hedgehog Concept:** *Meridian turns surgeons from sutures to stapling and keeps them stapling, and it earns on every stapled procedure rather than every instrument sold.*

**Weakest flywheel link:** a trained surgeon becoming a routine user. This link broke 8 years ago, and the dossier shows no sign it is measured today.

**Top three stop-doing items:**
1. Placing staplers with surgeons who have no training plan.
2. Paying reps on instruments placed instead of cartridges consumed.
3. Adding unrelated instruments just to spread marketing cost.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** The moat has not been checked. Patents and a training lead are all that stand between Meridian and a large suture maker with far more surgeon relationships, and nobody has measured either one.

**Numbers that would change this answer:** profit per cartridge, patent expiry dates, the active-use rate among stapler owners, and rep turnover.

Greatness potential: 25/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Meridian is the company surgeons think of when they think of mechanical tissue closure. Stapling is taught in residency because Meridian built the curriculum. Its reps are the best-trained clinical salespeople in the operating room and stay for years rather than months. Its economics are measured and managed per stapled procedure, not per instrument shipped. It extends stapling into new procedures only where clinical evidence shows staples beat sutures. It runs with a professional management bench, controls that cannot be gamed by commission pressure, and a leadership succession that does not depend on the founder. When a large competitor enters, it meets a decade-deep installed base of trained surgeons, outcome data, and teaching-hospital relationships, not just a patent.

**Current push (next two turns):**
1. **Turn (2):** Measure and manage active use. Know which owners of a stapler actually staple, and send reps back to the ones who don't.
2. **Turn (1):** Lower rep churn so the $8,000 training investment compounds instead of walking out the door.

**Doom-loop check:**
- **Hire, train, fire, repeat** is a small doom loop inside the sales engine. Each rep let go quickly writes off about $8,000 and a set of surgeon relationships.
- **Channel-stuffing risk** `[ASSUMPTION]`: commission-only pay, high pressure, and a model where instrument sales are not revenue that sticks create an incentive to book shipments that are never used. This repeats the failure from 8 years ago in a new form.
- **Product sprawl:** the early logic of "develop other instruments to spread marketing costs" could turn into chasing breadth instead of depth.
- **No sign of** direction changes or big-bang acquisitions so far. Verdict: the loop closes, with leaks to fix.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop placing staplers without a committed training plan** with the surgeon and OR team.
2. **Stop paying and judging reps on instruments placed.** Pay on cartridges consumed over time.
3. **Stop developing non-stapling instruments to spread marketing cost.** Every new product must extend stapling to a new procedure.
4. **Stop firing new reps before their training has had time to pay back.** Set a defined ramp period and a coaching step first.
5. **Stop letting distributors own surgeon relationships.** Distributors deliver; Meridian converts.
6. **Stop recognizing success by shipments into the channel.** Report pull-through and in-hospital use alongside sales.
7. **Stop pushing staples into procedures where sutures are clinically equal or better.** Unearned conversions create detractors.
