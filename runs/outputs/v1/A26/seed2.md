# Good to Great: Brightwater Industries

## One-screen verdict

**Brightwater is good on assets and weak on economics. It can become great only by narrowing to toys.**

- **Hedgehog (working hypothesis):** Brightwater keeps a small stable of simple, affordable toy brands selling for decades, measured by *lifetime profit per brand*.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** brand renewal. The one proven hit is off the market, and the company's own brands are aging and depend on TV.
- **Top 3 stop-doing:** (1) chasing electronic games; (2) ventures outside toys, such as day care and cookware; (3) running pencils and toys as rival fiefdoms. Separate them.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** heavy debt and poor cash flow mean the bankers, not the family, currently set the pace.
- **Missing numbers that would change this:** toy versus pencil profit, debt level and loan terms, Year −1 results, and profit by brand. If toys lose money before interest, the answer may be "be a pencil company."

Greatness potential: 30/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Brightwater is a focused toy company. It does not compete with Rival M on every shelf. It owns a dozen or so brands that parents remember from their own childhood and children still ask for by name: preschool classics, the long-running build-a-face toy, licensed comic-strip characters, and a revived action-figure line re-engineered to cost less to make. Each brand is reviewed every year on its profit. Advertising goes to fewer brands, which get more of it. Every product passes a safety gate before it ships, and that gate can stop a launch. The pencil business runs separately under the person who built it, so the family dispute is over and the two businesses no longer compete for the same capital. Debt falls first. Growth is paid for by cash the brands generate, not by borrowing to buy the next hit. No single product makes up most of sales again.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop considering electronic games.** No cash, no capability, and much larger competitors. Revisit only if a licensing route requires no capital.
2. **Stop every venture outside toys.** No day care, cookware, or tie-ins driven by subsidies or celebrities.
3. **Stop running pencils and toys as rivals inside one company.** Decide the separation and finish it.
4. **Stop launching products without an independent safety gate.** The lawn-dart and water-gun episodes must not recur.
5. **Stop one-season novelty and fad products.** They work against lifetime profit per brand.
6. **Stop SKU proliferation.** Keep the president's pruning going. Every product must earn its shelf space each year.
7. **Stop using the TV show as an unacknowledged advertising vehicle.** Either make it real programming or exit it.
8. **Stop any pressure to restore the dividend before the debt plan is met.**

**Non-negotiable disciplines:**
- An annual brand review based on profit per brand. The bottom brands get cut or refreshed.
- Cash comes before growth: debt reduction targets are met before any new category.
- The safety gate has veto power.
- Concentrated advertising: fewer brands, more support behind each.

**Framework boundaries:** brand teams decide freely on design, refresh and marketing for their brands. They cannot enter a new category, take on debt, or skip the safety gate.

**Bureaucracy check:** the approval and politics layers between pencils and toys exist only because of the family conflict. They disappear with separation.

## 7. Core ideology and the BHAG

- **Core values:** safety first, without exception; simple, affordable play; keep good brands alive and don't chase fads; thrift and cash discipline; tell the truth with numbers.
- **Core purpose:** to give every generation of children toys they can afford and will remember.
- **BHAG (20 years):** be one of the two largest toy companies in the country, with at least a dozen brands that have each sold for more than 20 years, and no single brand above a quarter of sales.
- **Clock-building mechanisms:**
  - the annual brand review based on profit per brand
  - independent directors
  - the safety gate with veto power
  - a rule tying capital allocation to debt reduction
  - a documented succession bench
  - separation of family ownership from management roles
