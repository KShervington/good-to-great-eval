# Tolliver Company: Good to Great

## Verdict

**Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** Tolliver can be the best consumer-electronics superstore operator in regions where it builds dense store clusters, and it should judge itself on **profit per region**.

**Weakest flywheel link:** whether superstore profit can fund the next cluster. No one has shown profit by format or by region.

**Top three stop-doing items:**
1. Stop buying chains for their tax credits or their store count.
2. Stop running four formats as equal growth bets.
3. Stop entering markets with one or two isolated stores.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** capital is spread across four formats while a rival backed by a steel company enters markets in force. Tolliver cannot show which of its formats actually earns money.

**Missing numbers that would change the answer:**
- Profit and return on investment by format, and by store for the superstores
- Net income and return on equity for the last three years
- Debt level
- Market share outside the national capital area

If mid-sized stores turn out to beat superstores on return on capital, the Hedgehog Concept flips to mid-sized stores.

Greatness potential: 35/100

---

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Tolliver is one format, expanded region by region. It runs warehouse-showroom superstores that offer the widest selection and real service at low prices. They are clustered so that each region pays for its own advertising, distribution and management. The company enters a region only when it can open several stores in a short window, and it measures every region by its profit. Superstore cash flow, sheltered by the acquired tax losses, funds the next region, so expansion never depends on a bet the size of the company's net worth. Leased departments, isolated stores and opportunistic acquisitions are gone. Tolliver outperforms its market for fifteen or more years because the whole company pushes the same loop, not because one store or one deal paid off.

**Confirmation tests (all three must hold):**
1. Superstore return on investment is higher than mid-sized store return on investment, after a fair ramp-up period.
2. The four superstores opened last year in the neighboring state are on track to earn back their cost. If they are profitable as a cluster, the region denominator holds.
3. Mid-sized stores in the national capital area (11% share) earn more where store density is higher.

**Explains past wins?** Yes. The superstore bet came right after the loss year, and the company survived and grew.

**Rules things out?** Yes: leased departments, acquisitions for tax credits, new formats, and isolated stores.

**Uncomfortable?** Yes. It may mean converting or exiting mid-sized stores, and fixing or exiting last year's acquisition.

## 3. The flywheel

```
  ┌──► 1. Superstore value: widest selection + service + low price
  │                     │
  │                     ▼
  │        2. Traffic and volume per store
  │                     │
  │                     ▼
  │    3. Regional cluster density → ad + distribution cost spread
  │                     │
  │                     ▼
  │     4. Buying power + lower cost per sale → lower prices
  │                     │
  │                     ▼
  │      5. PROFIT PER REGION (denominator)
  │                     │
  │                     ▼
  │  6. Retained cash (amplified by tax-loss shield) funds next cluster
  │                     │
  └─────────────────────┘
```

**Current push (next two turns):**
1. Prove step 5. Build profit-by-region and profit-by-format reporting, using the national capital area and the new four-store cluster as test cases.
2. Write the cluster entry rule for step 6: the minimum number of stores per region, the opening window, the maximum share of net worth at risk per region, and the advertising budget per region.

**Doom-loop check:** **warning signs present.** Over three decades the company has moved through licensed departments, acquired chains, audio specialty stores, mail order, mid-sized stores, superstores, and another acquisition. Each one may have made sense on its own. Together they look like new direction after new direction. Buying a bankrupt chain partly for its tax credits is a classic attempt to jump-start momentum. The superstore line has been pushed steadily for about eight years, which is the opposite of the doom loop. That consistency is the thing to protect.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. Stop acquiring chains for tax-loss credits or store count. Every acquisition from here on must pass the Hedgehog test and the cluster rule.
2. Stop treating the four formats as equal growth bets. Superstores get the growth capital. Mid-sized stores are held or converted. The acquired chain is fixed or exited. Leased departments are exited.
3. Stop single-store entries into new markets. No region opens without a committed cluster.
4. Stop committing to any single store or region whose failure would endanger the company. The first superstore risked half of net worth, and that should not happen again.
5. Stop exiting the West Coast leased department slowly. Set an end date.
6. Stop expanding the big-city chain while it is struggling. Set a deadline for proving it can earn money.
7. Stop meeting Rival S head-on in markets where it is already dense. Win elsewhere first `[ASSUMPTION]`.
8. Stop adding categories outside consumer electronics and major appliances. Appliances stay as long as superstore data shows they help the economics `[ASSUMPTION]`.

**Non-negotiable disciplines:**
- A format-level and region-level P&L, reviewed monthly.
- Capital is allocated only by profit per region.
- One store prototype, copied consistently.

**Framework:** headquarters fixes the format, the region-entry rule and the capital limits. Store and region managers have freedom over staffing, service and local promotion inside those limits.

**Bureaucracy check:** no evidence either way. Watch for approval layers that pile up around the acquired chain to make up for weak local management.

## 7. Core ideology and the BHAG

**Core values** `[ASSUMPTION]`, to be confirmed with leadership:
1. Customers get selection, service and fair prices together, not traded off against each other.
2. Face the facts before deciding.
3. Discipline over opportunism.
4. Operators own their results.

**Core purpose:** to make new consumer technology accessible and understandable to ordinary households. This echoes the founding story of a TV store opened as TV arrived.

**BHAG (about 15 years):** become the leading consumer-electronics retailer in the country by number of dominant regions, with superstores in a clear majority of the large metro areas it chooses to enter.

**Clock-building mechanisms:**
- A format and region P&L system.
- A written cluster-entry rule.
- A store prototype manual.
- A management-development pipeline that supplies superstore general managers.
- Board-level capital limits per region.
- A CEO succession plan that does not rely on the family line.
