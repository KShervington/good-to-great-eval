# Tolliver Company: Good to Great

## Verdict

**Good, not yet great, and spread across four formats.**

**Hedgehog (working hypothesis):** Tolliver can be the best big-ticket electronics and appliance superstore in every region where it builds a dense cluster of stores, measured by profit per region.

**Weakest flywheel link:** turning store profit into the next cluster. One superstore once cost half the company's net worth, and superstore returns versus mid-sized stores are unproven.

**Stop doing (top 3):**
1. Buying chains for tax credits or store count.
2. Running leased departments in other retailers' stores.
3. Opening single stores in markets it can't cluster.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** Rival S has a steel company's balance sheet and enters markets in force. Tolliver is meeting it with capital split four ways.

**Numbers that would change this:** profit and return on equity by format, debt, and the big-city chain's losses against its tax-credit value.

Greatness potential: 30/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Tolliver runs **one format**: large superstores for big-ticket consumer electronics and appliances. They offer the widest in-stock selection in town, prices that are hard to beat, and salespeople who know the products. Stores open only in **regional clusters**, so that advertising, distribution, management and vendor volume are shared across many stores in one market. Each cluster is a profit unit. Each profitable cluster funds the next adjacent one, at a pace set by capital and by trained store managers, not by ambition or by bargains on bankrupt chains. It has left the leased departments behind. The big-city chain has either been converted to the format or exited. Its returns beat the market for a decade or more, and the system runs on mechanisms (site rules, format P&L, a manager pipeline) that will outlast the current chief executive.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence (working hypothesis):** *Be the best big-ticket electronics and appliance superstore in every region where we build a dense cluster, and grow by profit per region.*

| Circle | Candidates considered | Narrowed to | Not best-in-world at |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Best in the world at** | Mid-sized full-service electronics stores; big warehouse-showroom superstores; acquiring and turning around distressed chains; leased-department operation | **Superstore retailing of big-ticket electronics and appliances, combining selection, price and service, within regional clusters** | Turnarounds of acquired chains (the big-city chain is struggling). Running departments inside other retailers (already shrunk from many to one). Mail order, audio boutiques, tires, hardware (all exited). |
| **Economic engine** (denominator) | Profit per store; profit per square foot; profit per employee; **profit per region** | **Profit per geographic region (cluster)** `[ASSUMPTION]` | — |
| **Passion** | Consumer electronics as a category; the founder's original TV-store mission; the CEO's drive to find the "right" direction | **Putting the right home electronics in families' hands with confidence** `[ASSUMPTION: inferred from founding story and 80% electronics mix]` | — |

**Why profit per region.** The costs that make or break a superstore chain are shared at regional level: advertising, warehousing and delivery, management, and local brand awareness. Rival S's playbook (several stores at once plus heavy advertising) shows that competitors already treat the market, not the store, as the unit. Profit per store would reward isolated winners and hide the cost of thin coverage.

**Tests:**
- *Explains past wins?* Partly. The 11% local share in the capital area comes from a concentrated set of stores in one region. The superstore bet survived from the loss year onward, and management doubled down with four more last year. Neither proves profitability yet.
- *Rules things out?* Yes: leased departments, tax-motivated acquisitions, isolated stores, and new formats.
- *Uncomfortable?* Yes. It implies the mid-sized format and the big-city chain may have to be converted or shed, despite the capital-area share and the tax credits.

**Confirmation evidence needed:**
1. Format-level P&L showing superstore return on invested capital ≥ mid-sized stores.
2. Profit per region rises with store density.
3. The two original warehouse-showroom stores have earned their capital back.

If superstores return *less* than mid-sized stores, re-run Gate 5 with the mid-sized format as the vehicle. The cluster and big-ticket logic likely survives either way.

## 3. The flywheel

```
        ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────┐
        │                                              │
        ▼                                              │
 [1] Superstore: widest big-ticket                     │
     selection + low price + trained service           │
        │                                              │
        ▼                                              │
 [2] More traffic, higher ticket,                      │
     higher sales per square foot                      │
        │                                              │
        ▼                                              │
 [3] Add stores in the SAME region →                   │
     shared ads, distribution, managers                │
        │                                              │
        ▼                                              │
 [4] PROFIT PER REGION rises  ◄── denominator          │
        │                                              │
        ▼                                              │
 [5] Volume buying power + internally                  │
     funded capital + trained managers                 │
        │                                              │
        ▼                                              │
 [6] Enter next ADJACENT region with a                 │
     cluster, lower prices, better stores ─────────────┘
```

**Weakest link:** [4]→[6]. The link from regional profit to self-funded expansion is unproven because profit and debt are unknown. Each store is a large capital commitment.

**Current push (next 1–2 turns):**
1. **Densify existing regions:** the capital area and the neighboring state where four superstores just opened. Don't open a new region yet.
2. **Prove the unit:** get format-level and region-level P&L so that turn [4] can be measured, not assumed.

**Doom-loop check:**

| Sign | Present? | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| New direction after new direction | **Yes, historically** | Leased departments, tires, mail order, audio specialty and Midwest stores were entered and exited in turn. Four formats run today. |
| Big acquisition to jump-start momentum | **Yes** | Big-city chain, bought partly for tax credits, in the hardest market. |
| Reaction without understanding | Risk | No visible format P&L, and Rival S's blitz invites a me-too response. |
| Inconsistency | Yes | No stated expansion route, pace or region. |

The loop closes on paper, so the gate passes. But the company currently shows doom-loop *behaviors* alongside flywheel *ingredients*.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Buying chains for tax credits or store count.** A tax asset doesn't make a weak operation fit the hedgehog.
2. **Running leased departments in other retailers' stores.** Exit the last West Coast arrangement on the cleanest contractual terms.
3. **Opening isolated single stores** in markets where a cluster isn't planned within 2–3 years.
4. **Launching or keeping extra formats.** No new concepts, and no return to audio boutiques, mail order, tires or hardware.
5. **Opening new mid-sized stores** until format P&L shows they beat superstores. Existing ones stay and are converted or relocated over time.
6. **Matching Rival S's ad blitz** in markets where Tolliver lacks cluster density. Defend and deepen instead.
7. **Expanding faster than the manager pipeline and internally funded capital allow.**
