# Good to Great: Marlowe Drug Co.

## Verdict

**Good, not yet great.** Marlowe is the largest drugstore chain in the country. It has more than doubled sales across five businesses, but earnings have not grown with them.

- **Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** *Be the most convenient drugstore in every community we serve, and earn the highest profit per customer visit in the industry.*
- **Weakest link in the flywheel:** profit per customer visit. Nobody measures it, and net margin has fallen from 2.2% to about 1.5% before the LIFO charge.
- **Top three stop-doing items:**
  1. Opening restaurants. Cancel the 22 planned units and set a firm exit deadline.
  2. Running discount department stores.
  3. Pursuing "growth across all divisions."
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** Marlowe cannot see which division makes money. EPS two years ago ($2.11) was barely above EPS seven years ago ($1.95), on 66% more sales.
- **Missing numbers that would change this answer:** profit and return on capital by division, and profit per store and per customer visit.

Greatness potential: 42/100

---

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Marlowe is a **drugstore company and nothing else**. It is the pharmacy and health-and-beauty stop on the most convenient corner in each neighborhood it serves. It knows the profit of every store and every customer visit, and it funds only what raises that number. It closes the stores that can't earn it and clusters new stores densely, especially in the Sun Belt markets regional rivals are taking. Its house-brand laboratory exists to raise margin inside Marlowe stores and its agency network, not to be a business in its own right. Restaurants, discount department stores, the travel agency, and coffee and ice cream production have been exited on a published timetable. Sales growth is a by-product. The headline number is profit per customer visit, and earnings per share compound faster than sales.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence:** *Be the most convenient drugstore in every community we serve, and earn the highest profit per customer visit in the industry.*

| Circle | Candidates considered | Kept | Ruled out |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Best in the world at** | National drugstore scale; convenient neighborhood drugstores; house-brand manufacturing; family restaurants; discount general merchandise | **Convenient neighborhood drugstores** (pharmacy plus health and beauty), backed by unmatched scale and house brands | Restaurants (8% of sales, no evidence of superiority). Discount stores (25 stores in one region, hit hardest by recession, shrinkage "unacceptable"). Travel, coffee, ice cream (no basis for best-in-world). |
| **Economic engine (denominator)** | Profit per store; profit per square foot; profit per customer visit; profit per prescription | **Profit per customer visit** `[ASSUMPTION]` | Sales volume. It is the current implicit denominator, and it has failed: sales grew 2.3x while EPS fell. |
| **Passion** | Pharmacy heritage; "community centers for health and beauty service"; restaurant heritage (soda fountains) | **Pharmacy and health service.** Management says the company holds fast to its identity as drugstores. | Restaurant nostalgia. It is real, but it is attachment to the past, not passion for what the company can be best at. |

**Why profit per customer visit (hypothesis):**
- Drugstore trips are frequent and small, so convenience drives how often people come.
- Prescriptions create repeat visits.
- House brands and lower shrinkage raise the take per visit.
- This denominator rewards dense, well-sited stores. Profit per store alone would reward ever-bigger boxes, which pull the chain toward general merchandise.

**Tests:**
- **Explains past wins?** Yes. Marlowe became the leader through drugstores and the switch to self-service. Pruning 51 marginal stores is profit-per-unit thinking already at work.
- **Rules things out?** Yes: about 23% of corporate sales and several side businesses.
- **Uncomfortable?** Yes. Restaurants trace back to the founder's soda fountains, and 22 new units are already planned.

**What would confirm it (needed for PASS):**
1. Division P&Ls show drugstores earn most of the profit at a higher return on capital than restaurants and discount stores.
2. Store-level data shows profit per visit separates the best stores from the marginal ones better than sales per store does.
3. In-store lunch counters do not raise profit per visit enough to justify their space. If they do, revisit the counters only, not the free-standing restaurants.

## 3. The flywheel

```
  (1) Convenient sites: best corners, dense clusters per market
                 │
                 ▼
  (2) More customer visits: repeat prescriptions, quick health & beauty trips
                 │
                 ▼
  (3) Higher PROFIT PER CUSTOMER VISIT: pharmacy mix, house brands, lower shrink
                 │
                 ▼
  (4) Cash and capital focused only on drugstores: open dense, close marginal
                 │
                 ▼
  (5) Market density: buying, distribution and advertising leverage; trusted local name
                 │
                 └──────────► back to (1)
```

- **The loop closes.** Density improves site economics and brand, which funds more convenient sites.
- **Weakest link:** step (3). It isn't measured. Margin is falling, and shrinkage is a named problem.
- **Current push:**
  1. Measure profit per visit and lift it, through shrinkage, cost control and house-brand mix. Management already named these levers.
  2. Move capital from restaurants and discount stores into dense drugstore clusters in the Sun Belt markets where Rival E is expanding.
- **Doom-loop check: moderate warning signs.**
  - Growth "across all divisions."
  - A string of acquisitions: discount stores, the travel agency, 29 stores, the Mexico stake.
  - Launching a new restaurant chain while margins fall.
  - Sales growth celebrated while EPS stagnates.
  - **Not** a full doom loop. Store pruning shows discipline, and management names margin as its top opportunity.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Open no new restaurants.** Cancel or defer the 22 planned units. Announce a firm deadline (for example, five years) to exit food service, starting with the free-standing family restaurants.
2. **Exit discount department stores.** Sell the 25 Southwest stores, or wind them down. Redeploy the capital to Sun Belt drugstores.
3. **Drop "growth across all divisions" as the strategy.** Stop treating the $1 billion sales mark as a milestone.
4. **Sell the travel agency, coffee roasting and ice cream plant.** The ice cream plant mostly serves fountains, which go when the restaurants go `[ASSUMPTION]`.
5. **Stop expanding manufacturing ahead of evidence.** Run make-versus-buy on the second laboratory and the plastic-container plant. Keep house brands only where they raise margin in Marlowe and agency stores.
6. **Stop buying store packages for footprint.** Judge any acquisition, like the 29 Southeastern stores, store by store on profit-per-visit potential.
7. **Put no new capital into the Mexico stake.** Hold it as a passive asset and review it.

## 7. Core ideology and the BHAG

- **Core values:**
  - The pharmacist's trust: accuracy and care first.
  - Service to the community.
  - Thrift and fair prices.
  - Integrity in every transaction.
- **Core purpose:** to make staying healthy convenient for every community we serve.
- **BHAG (25 years):** a Marlowe drugstore within easy reach of most Americans, earning the highest profit per customer visit of any drugstore chain.
- **Clock-building mechanisms:**
  - Division and store P&Ls with the profit-per-visit metric.
  - A capital rule: new capital goes only to drugstores and their supply chain.
  - A standard method for deciding which stores to prune.
  - A majority-independent board.
  - A succession pipeline open to non-family executives.
