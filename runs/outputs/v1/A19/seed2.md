# Marlowe Drug Co.: Good to Great verdict

**Good, not yet great.** Marlowe is the largest drugstore chain in the U.S. Sales have doubled in nine years, but earnings per share have barely moved.

**Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** *Marlowe runs the most convenient neighborhood drugstores in the markets it chooses to dominate, and it manages every decision by profit per customer visit.*

**Weakest flywheel link:** turning customer visits into profit. Nobody currently measures profit per visit, per store or per division.

**Top three stop-doing items:**
1. Stop growing restaurants. Freeze the 22 planned units.
2. Stop expanding discount department stores, and explore exiting them.
3. Stop adding stores to raise the count. Add them only if they pass a per-store profit test.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** before the LIFO accounting change, net margin was about 1.5%, compared with 2.2% seven years ago. Growth is not paying.

**Missing numbers that would change this answer:** profit and return on capital by division, and profit per store and per customer visit.

Greatness potential: 42/100

---

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Marlowe is a drugstore company and nothing else. Every store sits on a high-traffic, convenient corner in a metro area where Marlowe is dense enough to be the obvious choice. The pharmacy is the reason people visit repeatedly. Health and beauty products and Marlowe's own house brands make each visit profitable. Capital flows only to sites that raise profit per customer visit, and marginal stores are closed or relocated as routine housekeeping, without drama. Restaurants, discount department stores, the travel agency and other side businesses are sold or wound down over a few years. The cash and management attention they free up go into store density and pharmacy convenience. Earnings per share grow at least as fast as sales, every year, through good economies and bad.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence:** *The most convenient neighborhood drugstores in the markets we choose to dominate, run on profit per customer visit.*

| Circle | Candidates considered | Selected | Ruled out |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Best in the world at** | Low prices; house-brand manufacturing; diversified retail; convenient pharmacy and health/beauty drugstores | **Convenient, dense, pharmacy-anchored drugstores in chosen metros.** Marlowe already does about twice the volume of its nearest drugstore rivals and has 75 years of pharmacy identity. | **Restaurants**, where specialist chains compete. **Discount department stores**: 25 stores in one region is too small to lead. **Travel, coffee roasting, ice cream.** **Manufacturing as a business in its own right**: it can feed the drugstores, but it can't be best-in-world on its own. |
| **Economic engine** | Profit per store; per square foot; per customer visit; per prescription | **Profit per customer visit** `[ASSUMPTION]`. Drugstore economics depend on many frequent, small visits. Convenience drives visit count, and pharmacy plus house brands drive profit per visit. Profit per store is the secondary check. | Total sales. Sales have doubled without the profit to match, so sales is the wrong denominator. |
| **Deep passion** | Restaurant heritage (soda fountains); the founder's pharmacy roots; "community centers for health and beauty service" | **The drugstore identity.** Management says the chain "holds fast" to being a drugstore. | Restaurants. They are a cherished heritage and management calls their outlook positive, but heritage is not the same thing as best-in-world. |

**Confirmation tests**, which would show the hypothesis is right:
- Drugstores show higher return on capital than restaurants and discount stores once division profit is measured.
- Profit per customer visit varies a lot across stores, and the variation tracks site convenience and pharmacy volume. That would show the denominator is something management can actually move.
- In-store lunch counters do *not* raise drugstore profit per visit. If they do, they stay as a drugstore feature, but they are not a growth line.

## 3. The flywheel

```
  ┌─► 1. Convenient sites, clustered densely in chosen metros
  │        │
  │        ▼
  │   2. More frequent customer visits (pharmacy refills drive repeat trips)
  │        │
  │        ▼
  │   3. Higher PROFIT PER CUSTOMER VISIT
  │      (pharmacy + health/beauty + house brands, low shrinkage)
  │        │
  │        ▼
  │   4. Cash freed, both from profits and from exiting non-core businesses
  │        │
  │        ▼
  │   5. Reinvest in better sites; close or relocate the bottom stores
  │        │
  │        ▼
  └── 6. Density strengthens the brand and convenience ──┘
```

**Weakest link:** step 3. Visits are not turning into profit. Underlying margin has fallen from about 2.2% to about 1.5% even as volume doubled, shrinkage is a named problem, and the denominator is not measured at all.

**Current push, the next two turns:**
1. Measure profit per customer visit and profit per store for all 616 stores, and rank them.
2. Point this year's 32 new stores, and the capital now planned for restaurants and discount stores, at the top-ranked metros. Put the bottom tier of stores on a relocate-or-close list.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop growing restaurants.** Freeze the 22 new units and stop expanding the new family-restaurant chain.
2. **Stop expanding discount department stores.** Begin a sale process or a staged wind-down.
3. **Stop opening or buying stores to raise the count.** Every new or acquired store must clear a profit-per-store threshold. Fifty-one closures in two years show that the old test was too loose.
4. **Stop "growth across all divisions" as the stated aim.** Replace it with the hedgehog.
5. **Stop entering unrelated businesses.** No more deals like the travel agency, and exit the ones already made.
6. **Stop building manufacturing capacity ahead of proof.** The second laboratory and the plastic-container plant must justify themselves through drugstore margin, not through volume.
7. **Stop celebrating total sales.** Report EPS growth and profit per visit before reporting "$1 billion."
