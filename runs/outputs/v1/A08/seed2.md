# Good to Great: Pacific Crest Financial Corporation

## Verdict

**Good, not yet great, and drifting toward the wrong fork.**

- **Hedgehog (working hypothesis):** Be the lowest-cost, lowest-risk adjustable-rate home lender in our home state, judged by profit per dollar of assets across a full rate cycle.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** *low cost → competitive deposit pricing.* Deposit insurance means a reputation for safety won't buy cheap funds. Only a real cost advantage will, and that advantage is unproven.
- **Top three stop-doing items:** (1) building the brokerage and real-estate services business; (2) growing by acquisition and measuring success by size rank; (3) taking the high-yield, development and speculative assets that deregulation now permits.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** the old fixed-rate mortgage book is still funded by deposits that reprice, and its size relative to capital is unknown.
- **Numbers that would change this answer:** net worth and capital ratio, size of the fixed-rate book, ROA against peers over 5+ years, G&A as a share of assets, and the brokerage unit's P&L.

Greatness potential: 24/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

The great Pacific Crest is a simple company. It takes household savings and makes small, conservatively underwritten adjustable-rate home loans in the state it knows best. Its operating cost per dollar of assets is lower than any large peer's, so it can match competitors' deposit rates and still earn a wide margin. Its new loans reprice with its deposits, and its legacy fixed-rate book is being deliberately worked down. When deregulated rivals chase growth into risky assets, Pacific Crest turns them down, and when those rivals stumble it is still standing with capital to spare. It is known for being *the most profitable thrift per dollar of assets through every rate cycle*, not for being the second-largest. It has no brokerage, no real-estate development arm and no trophy acquisitions. It grows only when growth meets the same underwriting and cost standards as its core.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence (working hypothesis):**
> *Be the lowest-cost, lowest-risk adjustable-rate home lender in our home state, judged by profit per dollar of assets across a full rate cycle.*

| Circle | Candidates considered | Kept | Ruled out (cannot be best at) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Best in the world at** | Rate-matched residential lending (ARM pioneer); conservative small-loan underwriting; low-cost branch deposit gathering; diversified financial services; national thrift scale | Rate-matched, conservative, small residential lending in one large state. The early ARM move (≈60% variable by the late 1970s, 100% of new loans adjustable for three years) is a real, demonstrated head start. | Brokerage, real-estate services and development, commercial and speculative lending, high-yield securities, out-of-state lending without local knowledge |
| **Economic engine (denominator)** | Profit per office; per household relationship; per employee; **per dollar of assets** | **Profit per dollar of assets, measured across a full rate cycle**, i.e., cycle-averaged ROA. This forces attention onto the three levers a thrift actually controls: spread stability, operating cost and credit losses. | "Profit per household relationship" was rejected. It is the logic behind diversifying into cross-selling and would argue *for* the brokerage. |
| **Deep passion** | Prudence and survival; helping households own homes; scale and prestige | Prudent home lending to ordinary households `[ASSUMPTION]`. The celebrity advertising and the "second-largest" framing hint at this pull. |

**Confirmation tests (what would turn this hypothesis into a PASS):**
1. Over the past 5–10 years, Pacific Crest's ROA and credit losses beat the large-thrift peer median. This would show the hedgehog explains past wins.
2. G&A as a share of assets is at or below the peer median. If it isn't, the "lowest-cost" claim is aspiration, not fact.
3. Management can name at least one attractive deal the Hedgehog Concept would kill, and is uncomfortable about it. The brokerage unit is the obvious candidate.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   ┌─► 1. Conservative underwriting of small residential ARMs
   │              │
   │              ▼
   │   2. Low credit losses + assets that reprice with deposits
   │              │
   │              ▼
   │   3. Stable spread through rate cycles
   │              │
   │              ▼
   │   4. Lowest operating cost per $ of assets  ◄── WEAKEST LINK (unproven)
   │      (one simple product, standardized, consolidated offices)
   │              │
   │              ▼
   │   5. Highest profit per $ of assets → strong capital
   │              │
   │              ▼
   │   6. Can pay competitive deposit rates and still earn margin;
   │      capital to lend (and to buy distressed franchises cheaply, later)
   │              │
   └──────────────┘  (more household deposits → more disciplined lending)
```

**Current push (next two turns):**
1. **Prove link 4.** Benchmark cost per office and G&A as a share of assets. Consolidate overlapping offices from the Year −2 merger (61 offices were added to 122).
2. **Protect link 2.** Measure and shrink the legacy fixed-rate exposure, for example through the Rival K loan-for-securities swap (see the tech triage), and stress-test the ARM book for payment shock.

**Doom-loop check: warning signs present.**
- Growth by acquisition: the Late-1950s buying spree, the Year −2 merger, and the brokerage purchase last year.
- A new direction (diversification) taken *before* the core's economics are documented.
- A big-program reflex. "How much to grow by acquiring other thrifts" and "whether to expand beyond the state" are being framed as strategy questions, when they are really questions about the flywheel's capacity.

The loop closes logically, so this is not a FAIL. But the signals are real.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop investing in the brokerage and real-estate services business.** Freeze new capital and start a divestiture review.
2. **Stop real-estate development and direct equity investments**, however attractive deregulation makes them look.
3. **Stop or refuse high-yield and speculative securities** as a way to lift yield.
4. **Stop growth in commercial, construction and land lending** beyond strict, small caps.
5. **Stop funding growth with high-rate or brokered "hot" deposits.**
6. **Stop holding new fixed-rate loans** in portfolio unless the funding is matched.
7. **Stop acquiring thrifts for size.** Any acquisition must be ROA-accretive and must meet core underwriting standards after diligence.
8. **Stop using "second-largest in the nation" as a goal** in communications or compensation.
9. **Stop out-of-state expansion by acquisition** until the cost and capital tests in the scorecard are met.

## 7. Core ideology and BHAG

- **Core values:** prudence before growth; thrift in our own costs; candor about risk; service to ordinary households.
- **Core purpose:** to help ordinary households save safely and own their homes.
- **BHAG (25 years):** *The most profitable home lender per dollar of assets in the country through every rate and credit cycle for a generation, never once needing help.*
- **Clock-building mechanisms:**
  - A board-set interest-rate gap limit with automatic escalation.
  - Credit policy owned independently of volume goals.
  - Executive pay tied to cycle-averaged ROA, cost ratio and credit losses, not to asset growth.
  - Acquisition gates written down in advance.
  - A named and developed CEO successor.
