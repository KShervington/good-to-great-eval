# Good to Great: Heart Failure Transitions Program (HFTP), St. Aldric Regional Medical Center

## Verdict

**HFTP works at day 30. Whether it works at day 90 is unproven. It is not ready to expand.**

**Hedgehog (working hypothesis):** *HFTP keeps heart-failure patients home for good, not just for 30 days, by finding the medication and home problems no one else sees. It measures this in hospital days avoided per patient at 90 days.*

**Weakest flywheel link:** whether the protocol leads to *durably* fewer hospital days. The 30-day gap (15.9% vs 21.8%) nearly disappears by day 90 (32.7% vs 33.9%).

**Top three stop-doing items:**
1. Taking the lead on Hospital at Home in Year 1.
2. Graduating every patient at day 30.
3. Presenting enrolled-vs-not-enrolled results, the hospital-wide rate and the penalty drop as proof that HFTP works.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** between days 31 and 90, enrolled patients were readmitted *more* often than non-enrolled patients (16.8% vs 12.1%). HFTP may be postponing readmissions rather than preventing them.

**Missing numbers that would change this answer:** risk-adjusted 90-day readmission and mortality, Finance's cost per HF readmission, and a breakdown of non-enrolled patients by reason.

Greatness potential: 30/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great HFTP is the team St. Aldric could not replace. Its patients spend measurably more days alive and at home in the 90 days after a heart-failure discharge than comparable patients do, and the advantage holds after risk adjustment and lasts past the penalty window. It reaches the patients most likely to bounce back, including those who are unreachable by phone today. It spends its scarce pharmacist and social-work hours on the highest-risk tier, and it keeps those patients until day 90 instead of graduating everyone on day 30. Every report leads with its hardest number. Finance can state its value in the hospital's own dollars. Because of that, its budget survives lean years without anyone writing a story to the CEO. It is a narrow program and a deep one: a transitions team, not a home-based care empire. If it ever moves into acute care at home, it does so from proven strength, not because the board was enthusiastic.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence (working hypothesis):** *We keep heart-failure patients home for good, not just for 30 days, by finding the medication and home problems no one else sees, measured in hospital days avoided per patient at 90 days.*

| Circle | Candidates considered | Kept | Ruled out, and why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Best in the world at** (narrow world: HF patients leaving one regional hospital) | 30-day transition logistics; cross-prescriber medication reconciliation; reaching socially complex patients at home; acute care at home | Cross-prescriber medication fixes (Mr. D, two diuretics from two specialists) plus home-reality problems found by CHWs and social workers | **Acute inpatient-level care at home.** The team has never run 24/7 acute response or daily acute visits. "Home-visit experience" from one visit at day 7 and a CHW visit at day 14 is a different capability. **Generic care coordination for all conditions** is too broad to be best at. |
| **Resource engine** (team inside an organization: budget and headcount leverage) | Penalty avoided; readmissions avoided; beds freed; patient experience | **Hospital days avoided per enrolled patient, measured at 90 days.** This captures cost, capacity and penalty exposure, and it can't be gamed by pushing readmissions past day 30. | **The 30-day readmission rate** as the denominator: it rewards postponement. **Enrollment volume:** more patients is not the same as more value. |
| **Passion** | Patients staying home; the "catch"; graduations; recognition | The catch: the pharmacist who found the duplicate diuretic, CHWs hand-delivering 214 scales | Recognition and titles (see Gate 10) |

**Denominator:** hospital days avoided per enrolled patient at 90 days, compared with the program's cost of **$1,274 per enrolled patient** ($1.46M ÷ 1,146).

**Tests:**
- *Does it explain past wins?* Partly. It explains the Mr. D outcome and the 7-day follow-up advantage (82% vs 46%). It does not yet explain any 90-day result, because none has been shown.
- *Does it rule things out?* Yes. It rules out leading Hospital at Home now, uniform day-30 graduation, and chasing enrollment volume for its own sake.
- *Would it be uncomfortable to commit to?* Yes. It means reporting a metric on which HFTP currently looks almost no better than no program at all.

**What would confirm it:** (a) a risk-adjusted 90-day difference of a few points or more in favor of HFTP, with mortality not worse; (b) a Finance-validated cost per HF hospital day; (c) a high-risk 90-day tier that lowers day 31–90 readmissions against a matched prior-year cohort.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop pursuing leadership of Hospital at Home in Year 1.** It is acute care, outside the Hedgehog. It adds 6 FTE, a vendor platform and a new title to a team whose core outcome is unproven. Offer a partnership instead (Section 9).
2. **Stop graduating every patient at day 30.** Day 30 is the length of the penalty window, not the length of patient need. Extend the high-risk tier to day 90 `[ASSUMPTION: roughly the top 25–30% by risk score]` and shorten the protocol for low-risk patients to pay for it.
3. **Stop presenting enrolled-vs-not-enrolled results, the hospital's 17.8% and the penalty drop as HFTP's effect.** The comparison group includes patients sent to nursing facilities, patients who declined and patients with no phone, so the two groups are not alike. The penalty covers several conditions and is calculated on lagged data. The hospital rate is mostly HFTP's own patients by arithmetic.
4. **Stop giving every patient the same six-touch protocol.** Uniform intensity spreads pharmacist and social-work hours thin on the patients who need them least.
5. **Stop treating the enrollment rate as a goal in itself.** The 67% figure is calculated over all HF discharges, including nursing-facility discharges who are never offered enrollment, so part of the gap to 75% can't be closed by design. Target reach in the high-risk tier instead.
6. **Stop using patient stories as the value case to executives.** Keep Mr. D as an illustration and put the numbers first.
7. **Stop waiting for Finance to assign a cost.** Build the value model with Finance, including the unfavorable 90-day scenario.

## 7. Core ideology and the BHAG

**Core purpose:** help people with heart failure live their lives at home, not in our hallways.

**Core values:**
1. Tell the truth about outcomes, especially the unflattering ones.
2. The patient's home is the unit of care.
3. Find the hidden problem; don't just complete the checklist.
4. No handoff without someone ready to receive it.

**BHAG (one line, 15-year horizon):** *St. Aldric's heart-failure patients have more days alive and at home in the year after discharge than those of any hospital in the state.*
