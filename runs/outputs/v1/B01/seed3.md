# Good to Great: Kestrel Stores, Inc.

## Verdict

**Kestrel is good and was great. It is now sliding, though the slide doesn't yet show in the headline numbers.**

**Hedgehog (working hypothesis):** *Kestrel is where households go for expert help choosing, buying and getting working the complex electronics that run their homes, delivered by retail's best store-and-distribution machine and measured by operating profit per square foot.*

**Weakest flywheel link:** the in-store experience. The stores are aging, and customers may now avoid commissioned selling.

**Stop doing:**
1. Chasing 800 stores and $15B in revenue.
2. Running used cars inside the electronics company. Separate it.
3. Building stand-alone appliance stores.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** Rival B, running a self-service model, has led Kestrel in sales, earnings, profit per store and share for four years. Leadership still describes the company as "in great shape."

**Numbers that would change this:** profit per square foot versus Rival B, the gross-margin trend, appliance profitability, and customer preference for assisted versus self-service selling by category.

Greatness potential: 22/100

---

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Kestrel stops measuring itself by store count and revenue headlines. It measures itself by how much profit each square foot of selling space earns. It runs fewer, remodeled stores that are each built around two zones. The first is self-service, low-pressure and fast, for commodity goods: music, games, accessories and boxed software. The second is expert-led, for the complex purchases where advice is worth paying for: home theater, home office and computing, and later installation and setup. Its operations and distribution machine, already one of the best in retail, feeds both zones. The used-car business runs as its own company with its own Hedgehog. Kestrel no longer tries to beat home-improvement chains on refrigerators or to own media formats. It wins on one thing that Rival B's self-service model is not built to do. The approach outlasts the current chairman because the measures and the capital filters are built into the board, not into any one person.

**Denominator:** operating profit per square foot of selling space. Baseline unknown; see the scorecard.

**Confirmation tests:**
- **Explains past wins?** Yes. Kestrel grew on wide selection plus knowledgeable salespeople at a time when products were complex. Its failures, the proprietary disc format and the stretched growth targets, sat outside this concept.
- **Rules things out?** Yes: appliances, owning media formats, used cars inside the electronics company, and store count as a goal.
- **Uncomfortable?** Yes. It means a smaller appliance footprint, retired growth targets, and a redesign of the commission system everyone grew up in.
- **Evidence that would confirm it:**
  - Remodel test stores with expert zones beat matched control stores on profit per square foot.
  - Expert-zone gross profit per counselor-hour exceeds fully loaded commission cost.
  - Customer research shows a willingness to seek out help for complex categories.
- **Evidence that would kill it:** customers don't value assistance enough to cover its cost, even in complex categories. In that case Kestrel has no best-in-world circle in electronics retail and can likely only be *good*. The honest strategy then becomes efficient operation, with no claim to greatness.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │                                                              ▼
 [1] Trained expert counselors          [2] Customers buy complete systems
     in remodeled complex-category  ──►     (home theater, home office),
     zones                                  plus setup; trust grows
   ▲                                                 │
   │                                                 ▼
 [6] Distribution and store-ops machine  [3] Higher gross profit per sq ft
     turns that data into better             (self-service zone keeps
     assortment and inventory turns          commodity cost low)
   ▲                                                 │
   │                                                 ▼
 [5] More repeat and referral traffic ◄── [4] Reinvest in remodels, counselor
     and richer purchase data                training and pay, delivery and
                                             installation
```

- **Denominator in the loop:** step 3, operating profit per square foot.
- **Weakest link:** the step from 1 to 2. Aging stores and a commission model that customers may experience as pressure could break the trust link before it starts. Rival B's share gains are the evidence.
- **Current push (next two turns):**
  1. Run the planned 30–35 store remodel test as a *Hedgehog test*. Use expert zones plus self-service zones, matched control stores, and success thresholds written down in advance.
  2. Build the profit-per-square-foot measure for every store and category so that the reinvestment step (4) can be aimed.
- **Doom-loop check: signs are present.**
  - **Big programs to jump-start momentum:** the disc format cost about $233M, with roughly $198.5M in charges over two years (68.5 + 16 + 114). The used-car expansion target was more than 80 stores.
  - **Grand targets:** $15B in revenue and 800 stores.
  - **New directions in progress:** stand-alone appliance stores, and a CEO change.
  - **The next doom-loop risk:** reacting to Rival B by copying its self-service model all at once. That would be reaction without understanding. The answer is to test, not to imitate.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop managing to store count and revenue targets.** Retire the 800-store and $15B goals. Open new stores only where projected profit per square foot beats the base.
2. **Stop running the used-car business inside the electronics company.** Begin the board process to separate it fully. This is to protect *both* businesses' focus, not to punish the venture.
3. **Stop the six stand-alone appliance stores.** Decide on appliances within 90 days using profit per square foot. The default is an orderly exit unless the data beats the electronics average.
4. **Stop owning formats or technologies.** No more proprietary-format bets like the disc venture. Kestrel sells technology; it doesn't invent it.
5. **Stop applying commissioned selling across every category.** Use self-service where the customer doesn't need help.
6. **Stop issuing 20–25% earnings-growth forecasts.** Report the scorecard instead.
7. **Stop treating last year's +8% comparable-store sales as proof the model works.** A strong year can coexist with structural share loss.

**Non-negotiable disciplines:**
- Every capital request states its expected profit per square foot and how it serves the Hedgehog.
- A monthly board dashboard compares Kestrel with Rival B.
- Every new store and remodel test has a matched control and success thresholds written in advance.
- Every closed venture gets a blameless autopsy.

**Freedom within a framework:**
- **Store managers** control staffing mix, local assortment within the category boundaries, and service offers.
- **Headquarters** owns category scope, format standards, the compensation framework and capital.
- **Bureaucracy check:** heavy controls around commission disputes and sales-pressure complaints `[ASSUMPTION]` are a symptom of a pay design that rewards the wrong thing. Fix the design rather than adding rules.
