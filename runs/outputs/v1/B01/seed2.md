# Kestrel Stores, Inc.: Good to Great

## Verdict

**Kestrel is good. It was once great, and it is now sliding. The slide can be fixed, but nothing has fixed it yet.**

- **Hedgehog (working hypothesis):** Be America's best at helping people choose, buy and get working the complex home electronics they can't sort out alone, measured in *profit per customer transaction*.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** customer trust in the commissioned sales counselor. Rival B's self-service model is winning. Kestrel's selling only earns its cost if customers feel helped, not pushed.
- **Top 3 stop-doing:** ventures outside electronics retail (finish separating used cars); major appliances, including the six stand-alone appliance stores; new stores in the old format before the existing base is remodeled.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** Rival B has led Kestrel on sales, earnings and profit per store for four years, and Kestrel still hasn't decided how to answer.
- **Numbers that would change this:** margin by category, profit per store versus Rival B, store age and remodel cost, and customer satisfaction.

Greatness potential: 18/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Kestrel is the electronics retailer people go to when the purchase is hard: a home-theater system, a first home PC, a networked home office. They go there because they trust it to get them the right thing and make it work. It runs fewer new stores and better existing ones. Every store is remodeled around demonstration, help and setup. Its counselors are paid for outcomes the customer would endorse, such as the right product, fully set up and not returned, rather than for pressure. Commodity products such as music, movies and games are sold efficiently, without spending expert labor on them. It owns no used-car business, no appliance price war and no proprietary format bets. It does not try to out-Rival Rival B on self-service. It wins the narrower field where help matters, and its distribution and store-operations systems make that help affordable at national scale. It measures itself on profit per customer transaction and profit per store against Rival B, and not on revenue targets or store counts.

**What Kestrel cannot be best at:** lowest-cost self-service retail, major appliances (home-improvement chains are in a price war there), used cars, and format invention.

**Tests:**
- *Explains past wins?* Mostly yes. The original superstore formula was selection plus knowledgeable counselors plus strong operations.
- *Rules things out?* Yes: appliances, used cars, ventures, commodity-heavy space, and unremodeled growth.
- *Uncomfortable?* Yes. It caps the store count well below 800 and gives up roughly 14% of sales.

**Confirmation evidence:**
1. Margin by category shows complex categories carry materially higher gross profit per transaction than commodity categories.
2. In the remodel test, stores with outcome-based counselor pay beat control stores on profit per transaction *and* customer satisfaction.
3. Category share data shows Kestrel holding or gaining share in complex categories even where Rival B is present.

If (2) fails, meaning customers don't value help enough to pay for it, the hedgehog is wrong. Kestrel should then consider whether it can only be a good #2.

## 3. The flywheel

```
  (1) Expert, outcome-paid counselors
      in modern, demo-led stores
              │
              ▼
  (2) Customers trust Kestrel for
      complex, high-ticket purchases   ◄── WEAKEST LINK
              │
              ▼
  (3) Complete solutions sold:
      product + setup + accessories + support
              │
              ▼
  (4) Higher PROFIT PER CUSTOMER TRANSACTION
              │
              ▼
  (5) Cash funds remodels, counselor training/pay,
      and systems (distribution, inventory, data)
              │
              ▼
  (6) Better availability, selection and price
      competitiveness in complex categories
              │
              └──────────► back to (1) and (2)
```

**Current push (next two turns):**
1. **Turn (1):** use the 30–35 remodel test to test counselor pay designs, not just store fixtures. Commission alone rewards pressure. The test should pay for things the customer would endorse, such as attach, low returns and satisfaction.
2. **Turn (5):** move capital from new stores to remodels. With 571 stores and only about 6% in the remodel test, the existing base is the flywheel's drag.

**Doom-loop check:** warning signs are present.
- **New direction after new direction:** the used-car venture, then the disc-rental format, then six stand-alone appliance stores, with a possible format swap still undecided.
- **Big programs to jump-start momentum:** the ~$233M disc venture, and a $415M tracking-stock raise for an 80-store used-car target that was halted.
- **Reaction without understanding:** the next risk is copying Rival B's self-service model wholesale. That would turn Kestrel into a weaker Rival B and would be a doom-loop move.

**Verdict:** `CONDITIONAL`. The loop closes on paper, but link (2) is unproven.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop launching ventures outside electronics retail.** No more format inventions or adjacent big-box experiments. Any new venture must pass an explicit hedgehog test at board level.
2. **Finish separating the used-car business.** Don't leave it half in and half out.
3. **Stop selling major appliances, and cancel the six stand-alone appliance stores.** Exit over 12–24 months, unless category margin data shows it beats the company average after allocated costs.
4. **Stop opening stores in the old format while the base is unremodeled.** Cut or pause the ~25 planned openings and redirect the capital to remodels.
5. **Stop managing to revenue and store-count targets.** Retire the $15B and 800-store goals publicly, and replace them with profit per transaction and profit per store.
6. **Stop paying counselors in ways that reward pressure over outcomes.** Redesign the commission plan; don't simply abolish it.
7. **Stop spending counselor labor on commodity categories** (music, movies, games). Serve these as self-service and shrink their footprint where space is better used for demonstration.

## 7. Core ideology and the BHAG

**Core values** `[ASSUMPTION: inferred, to be confirmed with leadership and counselors]`:
1. The customer leaves with the *right* thing, working.
2. Operational excellence in everything behind the counter.
3. Expertise is earned and kept current.
4. Tell the truth about results, especially about competitors.

**Core purpose:** to make home technology understandable and working for ordinary people.

**BHAG (15-year horizon):** be the retailer most Americans name first when asked where to buy something complicated for their home.

**Clock-building mechanisms:**
- Counselor pay tied to customer-endorsed outcomes: attach rate, return rate and satisfaction.
- A quarterly Rival B red-flag report to the board, comparing profit per store, share by category, and format changes.
- A hedgehog gate for any new venture or category, with a written fit test and blameless post-mortems.
- A remodel cycle funded *before* new-store capital.
- A succession bench at least two deep for CEO and for heads of stores and merchandising.
