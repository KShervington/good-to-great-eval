# Good to Great: Kestrel Stores, Inc.

## One-screen verdict

**Kestrel is good. It used to be great. Today it is the #2 player and still behaves like #1.**

- **Hedgehog (working hypothesis):** Kestrel is where American households go to choose, buy and get working the complex home technology they can't sort out alone. It measures itself by operating profit per store.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** commissioned counselors may be eroding customer trust instead of building it. Rival B dropped commission and now earns more per store.
- **Top 3 stop-doing:** (1) fully separate the used-car business; (2) stop chasing 800 stores and stop promising 20%+ growth; (3) exit major appliances, starting by cancelling the stand-alone appliance stores.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** Rival B has led on sales, earnings, profit per store and share for four years, yet management's public line is still "great shape."
- **Numbers that would change this:** profit per store and margin by category (appliances above all), Rival B's profit per store, and customer trust under each selling model.

Greatness potential: 25/100

---

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Kestrel is the most trusted place in America to buy home technology that is hard to get right: home theater, computers and home office, and whatever complex digital categories come next. It does not try to be the cheapest box-mover for commodity items, and it does not try to sell cars, refrigerators or proprietary disc formats. Its counselors are paid for whether customers leave with a setup that works and come back. They are not paid for pushing volume. The company judges itself by operating profit per store, not store count or total sales. It reinvests that profit in counselor skill and in remodeling the stores it already has. It no longer runs on growth announcements. It closes the profit-per-store gap with Rival B within three years and then holds the lead. The superstore operating system that took it from 69 stores to more than 600 becomes the engine behind one narrow promise instead of a platform for diversification.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

> **Kestrel helps households choose, buy and get working the complex home technology they can't figure out alone, and earns the highest operating profit per store in the category by doing it.**

*Status: working hypothesis. The test is whether matched-store experiments show that expert-assisted selling of complex categories beats self-service on profit per store and customer trust (see confirmation tests).*

| Circle | Candidates considered | Kept | Ruled out (cannot be best-in-world) |
|---|---|---|---|

These are what the 800-store and $15B targets chased. |
| **Deep passion** | [ASSUMPTION] Counselors who love technology and like solving customer problems; an operations culture proud of execution; leadership passion for growth | **Counselor expertise + operational craft** | Passion for growth as such. It produced the ventures and is a Stage 2 decline signal. |

**Why profit per store:** store growth is slowing (34 added last year, about 25 planned this year, and the company's stated ceiling is about 800). From here, growth has to come from making each store more productive. This is also the number on which Rival B currently wins.

**Tests of the intersection:**
- *Explains past wins?* Partly. The superstore format plus knowledgeable counselors drove growth from about $1B to $12.6B. It does not explain the last four years, which is the point: that is when the expertise edge appears to have stopped paying.
- *Rules things out?* Yes: used cars, format ventures, appliances, chasing store count, and commissioned pressure on commodity categories.
- *Uncomfortable?* Yes. It means giving up about 14% of store sales, the 800-store story and the growth narrative, and it puts the sales model itself on trial.

**Confirmation tests (to turn CONDITIONAL into PASS):**
1. In matched stores, the outcome-paid expert model earns higher profit per store in complex categories than a self-service control.
2. Customer trust scores ("I'd come back here for help") beat self-service stores, and "felt pressured" scores fall.
3. Gross profit per complex-category customer rises through accessories, installation and service, not price.

**If the tests fail:** the honest conclusion is that Kestrel's best realistic future is as an efficient, self-service #2, which is *good*, not great. That is still a better outcome than funding another diversification.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │                                                              │
   ▼                                                              │
1. Skilled counselors help customers choose complex tech          │
   (home theater, PCs/home office, new digital categories)        │
   │                                                              │
   ▼                                                              │
2. Customers trust the advice → buy complete working solutions    │
   (accessories, installation, service) → come back and refer    │
   │                                                              │
   ▼                                                              │
3. Higher gross profit per complex-category customer              │
   │                                                              │
   ▼                                                              │
4. Higher OPERATING PROFIT PER STORE  (denominator)               │
   │                                                              │
   ▼                                                              │
5. Reinvest: counselor pay/training on customer outcomes          │
   + remodel existing stores around focus categories              │
   │                                                              │
   ▼                                                              │
6. Better stores and counselors become known as "the place        │
   that gets it right" → more complex-purchase traffic ───────────┘
```

**Weakest link: 1 → 2.** Commission can turn "expert advice" into "sales pressure." Rival B's shift away from commission, followed by its lead in profit per store, suggests that many customers experience it that way. If customers don't trust the advice, the loop doesn't turn. [ASSUMPTION: the dossier has no customer-trust data.]

**Current push (next two turns):**
1. Measure profit per store and per category for every store, so the denominator is real.
2. Run a disciplined test that pairs the 30–35 planned remodels with compensation redesign: outcome-based counselor pay in complex categories and self-service for commodities. Run it in matched stores against control stores.

**Doom-loop check: signals present.**
- *Big programs to jump-start momentum:* the used-car venture, the disc-rental format (about $233M invested and roughly $200M in charges and operating losses over two years), and the stand-alone appliance stores now proposed.
- *Targets over understanding:* $15B and 800 stores were announced four years ago, and both were missed or are far away.
- *Coming risk:* a new CEO who reacts to Rival B by ripping out commissioned selling overnight, or by redesigning every store at once. Copying the rival without understanding why it wins is the classic doom-loop move. Test, learn, then roll out.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop running a second business.** Fully separate the used-car business, by spin-off or distribution to shareholders, on a fixed timeline.
2. **Stop launching proprietary formats and standards.** Kestrel is a retailer. Kestrel does not get to create formats.
3. **Stop chasing the 800-store target.** Replace store count with profit per store as the headline goal.
4. **Stop issuing aggressive growth forecasts.** The 20–25% earnings guidance and the $15B-style revenue targets drive the undisciplined pursuit of more.
5. **Stop building stand-alone appliance stores, and plan the exit from major appliances** (about 14% of store sales). [ASSUMPTION: appliances earn below the store average per square foot. Confirm with category margin data before the final call.]
6. **Stop putting commissioned counselors between customers and commodity products** (music, video games, small items). Let those categories be self-service.
7. **Stop measuring success against last year.** Measure it against Rival B.

### Appendix: gate working notes

**Gate 0, subject card.** Kestrel Stores, Inc. Type: business (public specialty retailer, consumer electronics). Steward: CEO, handing over to the insider CEO-designate this summer, under the board. Horizon: decades. Stage: mature national chain of 571 superstores, #2 in its category, in a leadership transition, carrying a separate used-car business.

**Gate 1, baseline sheet.**
- *Evidence of good:* sales +17% to $12.61B; electronics +13% to $10.60B with comps +8%; continuing earnings +55% to $327.8M; growth from about $1B to $12.6B and 69 to 600+ stores under the current CEO; shareholder returns far above the market for over a decade; distribution and store operations respected industry-wide; the transformation outlasted the departure of its original architect.
- *Three tests:* (1) Superior performance: yes against the market historically, **no** against the best competitor for four years. (2) Distinctive impact: **weak**, because Rival B could absorb most of Kestrel's customers. (3) Endurance: **partial**, because it survived one leadership transition, but the edge has eroded.
- *State:* **good, not (any longer) great.** Not stewardship mode: the flywheel is losing momentum, so protecting the status quo would be wrong.

**Gate 2, leadership read.** *Strengths:* fierce resolve; an extraordinary build; succession planned with an insider. *Gaps:* a window-not-mirror reflex (pointing to growth when asked about distraction); ambition aimed at growth figures more than the core customer; staying as chairman. *One behavior change:* the incoming CEO's first public statement names the Rival B gap and owns it.

**Gate 6, discipline framework.** *Framework:* focus categories, profit per store, a counselor conduct standard, and a hedgehog test for every new initiative. *Freedom within it:* store managers control local staffing, merchandising emphasis and service execution. *Bureaucracy check:* rules that police sales pressure (return disputes, scripted upsell controls) likely exist to offset commission incentives [ASSUMPTION]. Fix the incentive and drop the rules. *Fanatical consistency:* every complex-category customer gets problem-solving help; every store sees its profit per store monthly; no venture outside the hedgehog gets funded.
