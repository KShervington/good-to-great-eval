# Pacifica Bancorp: Good to Great

**Verdict: good, not yet great, and the rules that made it look great are ending.** Pacifica's size rested on deposits whose interest cost was capped by law and on lending growth approved out in the field. Both of those are now liabilities.

- **Hedgehog (working hypothesis):** Be the best-run, lowest-cost bank for the households and small businesses of our home state, judged by profit per employee and not by size.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** turning a branch network built for capped-rate deposits into a cost base that can pay market rates and still earn.
- **Top stop-doing:** (1) treating asset growth as success; (2) new developing-country, farm and construction lending under decentralized approval; (3) long fixed-rate mortgages funded with short deposits.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** no one can yet say how much of the fast-grown loan book is already impaired.
- **Numbers that would change this:** losses and reserves by loan category, country exposure, maturity gap, and expense ratio versus peers.

Greatness potential: 30/100

---

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Pacifica is no longer "the world's largest bank." It is the bank that ordinary households, farms and small businesses in its home state choose first, run at a lower cost per customer than any rival. It keeps core deposits because of convenience, service and price, now that a legal ceiling on deposit rates no longer holds them in place. Every loan it books is centrally underwritten and match-funded: it knows what each loan costs to fund and what it could lose. International work shrinks to supporting the trade of home-state clients. It uses its computing heritage to automate the branch network instead of trailing in-state rivals on ATMs. It reports one number above all others, profit per employee, and it is comfortable being smaller if that number rises. Its founder's purpose, banking for the little guy, is again the strategy and not just the origin story.

**Tests of the sentence**
- *Explains past wins?* Mostly. The home-state retail franchise and consumer innovation built the bank. The international and asset-growth era is what the sentence excludes.

**Confirmation tests (what would turn this into a PASS)**
1. Segment profitability, after realistic loss reserves, shows home-state retail and small business earning the highest risk-adjusted return.
2. Cost per account and expense ratio can reach parity with, then beat, in-state rivals within 2–3 years.
3. Core deposit retention holds up as rate ceilings phase out over six years. Track it in every annual step.

## 3. The flywheel

```

        ▼                                              │

        │                                              │
        ▼                                              │
 [4] Centrally underwritten, match-funded local loans  │
        │   at known risk                              │
        ▼                                              │
 [5] Lowest cost per account (automation, scale)       │
        │   → PROFIT PER EMPLOYEE ↑                    │
        ▼                                              │
 [6] Reinvest in price, service and technology ────────┘
     for the little guy
```

**Weakest link:** [3]→[5]. Deposits stayed cheap because the law capped their rate. Now they have to stay because customers prefer the bank, while the bank pays market rates. That only works if link [5], cost per account, falls fast enough.

**Current push (next one or two turns):**
1. **Stop pushing on the wrong flywheel.** Halt asset growth in high-risk categories and get the risk numbers.
2. **Turn link [5].** Set a cost baseline against peers, catch up on ATMs, and plan branch consolidation.

**Doom-loop check: warning signs are already present.**
- Growth was pursued for its own sake: 15% a year in assets, the only top-20 U.S. bank to do so.
- Many new fronts opened at once: developing-country, farm and construction lending, fixed-rate mortgages, 90+ overseas branches, venture capital.
- The board is openly considering a CEO who would "enter new businesses." That risks a new direction layered on new direction.
- Next risk: a new CEO trying to restart momentum with one big program, acquisition or tech bet.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop treating asset growth as success.** Remove it from targets, pay and board reporting. Replace it with profit per employee and risk-adjusted ROA.
2. **Freeze new loans to developing countries.** Let the existing book run off under central monitoring of country exposure.
3. **Pause growth in farm and construction lending** until loss experience and reserve adequacy are reviewed. After that, lend only under central approval.
4. **Stop originating long-term fixed-rate mortgages funded with short-term deposits.** Originate adjustable-rate loans, or match-fund or sell fixed-rate ones.
5. **Stop opening overseas branches.** Shrink international to what serves home-state clients.
6. **Exit venture-capital direct investing.**
7. **Stop adding branches.** Consolidate where ATMs and automation can carry the volume.
8. **Retire "action beats indecision" as the credit creed.** In lending, declining a loan is a decision, and most bank losses come from approvals that should have been declined.

**Non-negotiable disciplines:** one denominator, reported monthly. Central credit limits by country, sector and size. A monthly maturity-gap report against board-set limits. An expense ratio benchmarked to peers every quarter. Blameless loan-loss post-mortems.

**Framework boundaries:** branch and regional managers keep wide freedom on service, local pricing within bands, and small-business relationships. Credit concentration, interest-rate gap and capital are set at the center.
