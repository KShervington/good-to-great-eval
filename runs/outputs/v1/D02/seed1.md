# Corvane: Good to Great

**Verdict: Good, not yet great. The Hedgehog Concept holds. The cheap machine supply it runs on is about to end.**

**Hedgehog Concept:** *Corvane keeps independent restaurants' dish lines running, fast and for one monthly price, by packing machines so densely into each metro that a technician is never far away.*

**Weakest flywheel link:** low-cost machines. 71% of all units came through Delmar from Harrow's Grill remodels, and that program finishes next fiscal year. Replacement units cost 1.7 to 2.2 times as much per placement.

**Stop doing:**
1. The third metro in spring.
2. Larchmont as offered (three-year conveyor contract, 2-hour response, service credits).
3. Quoting a 63% margin that leaves technician labor out.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** the supply cliff. None of the four offsite agenda items addresses it.

**Missing numbers that would change this answer:** fully loaded technician cost, account churn and lifetime, and what Delmar can still deliver from the roughly 118 remaining Harrow's locations.

Greatness potential: 22/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Corvane is the obvious dish-machine answer for independent restaurants in a small number of very dense metros. Every customer sits within a short drive of a technician. The 4-hour promise is met at least 95% of the time everywhere, and the east territory's new unannounced inspections become a reason to buy, not a liability. It owns a repeatable sourcing channel for chain-remodel machines instead of depending on one liquidator and one chain. It knows its fully loaded contribution per technician and expands only when an existing metro clears clear density and response thresholds. Revenue comes from restaurants on standard terms, not from large service-only contracts that compete with restaurants for the same technicians.

**Denominator:** contribution per field technician, after machine capital. Today's proxy is 351 machines ÷ 17 technicians = **20.6 machines per technician**.

**Tests:**
- *Does it explain past wins?* Yes. The dense home metro is where both the speed and the reviews come from.
- *Does it rule things out?* Yes. It rules out the third metro now, Larchmont as offered, conveyor work, and scattered accounts outside route zones.
- *Is it uncomfortable to commit to?* Yes. It means turning down a signed $412k letter of intent and the CEO's expansion plan.

**Status:** working hypothesis. It is confirmed if:
1. Fully loaded contribution per technician in the home metro is positive and rises as density rises.
2. The east territory reaches ≥92% response as its account count grows.
3. Churn proves low enough that 20–25 month machine payback is acceptable.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   ┌─► 1. Fast, reliable service (4-hr promise, inspection-ready machines)
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │    2. Chef word of mouth + "fast" reviews
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │    3. New accounts INSIDE existing route zones
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │    4. More machines per technician  ◄── DENOMINATOR: contribution per tech
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │    5. Lower cost per visit; cash per machine rises
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │    6. Funds low-cost machine sourcing + refurb + response slack  ◄── WEAKEST LINK
   └────────────┘
```

**Weakest link: #6.** The cheap-machine source ends within about 12–18 months. The second-weakest link is #4 in the east territory, where response is not yet good enough to start the loop.

**Current push (next one or two turns):**
1. Lock in the remaining Harrow's units and open a second chain-remodel source.
2. Grow density in the east territory only inside its route zones, until response there reaches ≥92%.

**Doom-loop check:** There is no history of lurching between strategies. But the offsite is set up to start one: a new metro, a new customer class (hotels), a new machine class (conveyors), new capital (the bay) and a new price, all at once. That is the "big program to jump-start momentum" pattern. Choose one push.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Planning the third metro for spring.** Replace it with threshold gates (see Section 1).
2. **Pursuing Larchmont as offered:** conveyor units, a 2-hour response promise with service credits, three years, about a quarter of revenue in one customer.
3. **Quoting 63% gross margin** without technician labor. Report fully loaded contribution per metro and per technician.
4. **Dev closing most large accounts personally.**
5. **Buying refurb capacity before supply is locked in.** Defer the $64k bay.
6. **Signing accounts outside route zones** just because they want to buy.
7. **Treating technician slack as waste to be filled.** At 63% utilization, some of that slack is what keeps the 4-hour promise.
8. **Competing on sticker price with the national chemical bundler.** Compete on speed and on the absence of lock-in.
