# Halvard Strings: Good to Great (stewardship run)

**Verdict: Halvard is already great.** For 14 years it has grown in a market that grows 2% a year. Operating margins have held at 24–29% in ten of the last eleven years, return on capital averages 34%, and its share of professional orchestra players has risen from 9% to 31%. The job now is protecting what works, not inventing change.

**Hedgehog:** *Halvard makes the strings professional violin-family players choose when they listen blind, and sells them through the specialist shops those players trust.*

**Weakest flywheel link:** winding capacity and know-how. Cello and bass lead times slipped to five weeks, and the head of winding retires in two years.

**Temptations to refuse:**
1. Halvard Six as proposed.
2. Buying the student brand.
3. Spending winding capacity on anything before cello and bass delivery is secure.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** the board is about to consider re-entering both markets Halvard left 14 years ago, at half its gross margin.

**Missing numbers that could change this:** winding utilization, Halvard Six's fully loaded contribution, full-set share among Halvard users, and the student brand's margins.

Greatness potential: 74/100

---

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Halvard looks like today's Halvard, carried past Annelie Brandt. Every string still passes a blind test by working professionals. The company still sells only through specialist shops at a protected price, and it still earns roughly a dollar of operating profit for every four of revenue. It grows by winning the 69% of orchestral players who don't yet use any Halvard string. It also grows by moving current users from one Halvard string to full sets, and by reaching specialist shops in regions it doesn't yet serve. It doesn't grow by pointing its winding machines at bigger markets where it has no judges and no right to win. Its greatness becomes institutional: the Listening Room's veto is written into governance, every critical role has a trained successor, and cello and bass players never wait more than two weeks.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence:** *Halvard makes the strings professional violin-family players choose when they listen blind, and sells them through the specialist shops those players trust.*

| Circle | What it is | What it is *not* |
|---|---|---|
| **Best in the world at** | Strings professionals prefer in blind tests, strongest in cello (44% of orchestral cellists). The proof is the Listening Room, which has vetoed three products in five years, and 78% three-year loyalty. | Student strings, guitar strings, mass-retail volume. Winding technology on its own is not the best-in-world claim. It is the *means*. "We have the best winding technology in the world" is a lab claim. "Professionals pick us blind" is a market fact. |
| **Economic engine (denominator)** | **Profit per loyal professional player.** Professionals pay a 15–25% premium and stay. Advanced players and teachers follow them. Shops earn steady margins under minimum advertised price, so they push the brand. | Profit per unit shipped, which would reward volume and favour Halvard Six. |
| **Passion** | Players and sound: the CEO's acoustics background, a panel with real veto power, crediting the players publicly. `[ASSUMPTION]` Staff passion sits here too, inferred from the panel culture and employee ownership. | Retail growth stories. |

**Confirmation tests:**
- **Does it explain past wins?** Yes. The 14-year-old exit plus the testing program produced the margin and share curve.
- **Does it rule things out?** Yes. It excludes Halvard Six and the student brand.
- **Is it uncomfortable?** Yes. It says no to a $19M plan and a market six times larger.

**Status: working hypothesis.** It is confirmed if all three hold for the next three years:
- orchestral share keeps rising by about 1.5 points a year;
- revenue growth stays well above the 2% market;
- loyalty holds at or above 78%.

## 3. The flywheel

```
 ┌─► 1. Listening Room blind-tests every prototype (approves or kills)
 │        │
 │        ▼
 │   2. Strings professionals genuinely prefer
 │        │
 │        ▼
 │   3. Orchestral adoption (31%) and loyalty (78% at 3 years)
 │        │
 │        ▼
 │   4. Advanced players follow; specialist shops pull demand at protected margins
 │        │
 │        ▼
 │   5. Premium price → PROFIT PER LOYAL PROFESSIONAL PLAYER (61% gross margin)
 │        │
 │        ▼
 └── 6. Reinvest in own winding machines, R&D, and the player panel
```

**Weakest link: between 2 and 3.** Players must be able to *get* the strings they prefer. The autumn slip to five-week lead times hit cello and bass, the strongest segments. The winding knowledge base is also about to lose its head.

**Current push (next one or two turns):**
1. Find the root cause of the lead-time slip and protect cello and bass capacity. Any second shift goes here first.
2. Grow profit per loyal player inside the hedgehog. The levers are full-set conversion among existing Halvard users and share among the 69% of players who don't use Halvard.

**Doom-loop check:** no doom loop so far. Direction has been consistent for 14 years. The risk is prospective. Halvard Six and the acquisition are both big moves meant to restart momentum that hasn't actually stalled: growth was 9%, 7% and 8% against a 2% market.

## 5. Temptations to refuse (stewardship mode)

1. **Halvard Six as proposed: guitar strings on mass-retail end-caps under the Halvard name.** Five problems:
   - **Margin.** Year 3 gross profit would be about $5.9M on $19M, against about $28.5M on the core's $46.8M. Blended gross margin would fall from 61% to about 52%, before chain terms, promotion, four sales hires and a second shift.
   - **History.** Guitar would become about 29% of revenue. That nearly reverses the exit of about 22% of revenue from mass distributors 14 years ago.
   - **Capacity.** It would compete for winding machines that already let cello and bass customers down.
   - **Channel trust.** Specialist shops value that Halvard isn't in mass retail.
   - **No judges.** Halvard has no blind-test panel for guitar. A 2.3× corrosion result in the lab is not proof that guitarists will choose it. `[ASSUMPTION]` Long-life coated guitar strings are an established category, so that advantage may already be partly matched.
2. **Buying the student brand at about 1.1× revenue.** It is a business Halvard exited, in a channel it exited. `[ASSUMPTION]` A private-equity consolidator selling one piece of its roll-up is more likely selling the weaker asset. Its margins are unknown.
3. **Asking the Listening Room to judge guitar prototypes.** The CEO's own principle is that the question matters less than who answers it. Violin-family professionals are the wrong judges for guitar, and this would quietly widen the panel's mandate. Answering her question properly would mean building a second Listening Room of professional guitarists. That cost shows how far outside the hedgehog this is.
4. **Giving any new winding capacity to non-core work** before cello and bass lead times stay at two weeks or less all year.
5. **Treating the revenue growth rate as the goal.** The goal is profit per loyal professional player. Growth of 7–9% in a 2% market is outperformance, not stagnation.

*Already refused, keep refusing:* general online marketplaces and selling below minimum advertised price.

*If the board insists on doing something with guitar:* the only shape consistent with the hedgehog is a small option that doesn't use the Halvard brand on end-caps and doesn't take core capacity, such as licensing the corrosion process. Evaluate it, but at low priority.

**Faith statement:** *Professional players will keep choosing what sounds best, and nobody asks them as rigorously as we do.*

## Appendix: gate notes

- **Method note.** There was no memory, past-chat search or web research in this session, so no prior run or market data were used. Everything rests on the CFO's dossier. Inferences are marked `[ASSUMPTION]`.
- **Subject card.**
  - Name and type: Halvard Strings, business (privately held manufacturer).
  - Steward: CEO Annelie Brandt and the board.
  - Time horizon: decades (family and employee owned).
  - Stage: mature, already great, at a strategic fork.
- **Baseline (all three tests of great):**
  - *Superior performance:* revenue up 4.2× over 14 years in a 2% market; operating margin 24–29% in ten of eleven years; ROIC about 34%; no debt.
  - *Distinctive impact:* 31% of orchestral players and 44% of cellists use Halvard; no competitor runs comparable blind testing.
  - *Endurance:* 14 years, including a metal-price shock. Endurance beyond one CEO is the least-proven test.
- **Leadership read.** Strengths: window and mirror ("lucky to find the right players early"), declined the cover profile, credits the panel, puts the decision process above her own opinion. Gaps: the successor is untested in big decisions, and she may be the main guardian of the panel's power. One behavior change: let the COO lead the Halvard Six and acquisition evaluations and present the recommendation.
- **Discipline framework.**
  - *Non-negotiables:* panel approval for every product; minimum advertised price; the annual 61-orchestra survey; metal hedges; no debt and a cash cushion; lead times of two weeks or less.
  - *Freedom:* R&D may prototype anything for the violin family, and commercial staff choose their own specialist-channel tactics.
  - *Bureaucracy check:* no compensating rules found. The Listening Room is a mechanism, not red tape.
- **Tech triage.**

  | Technology | Classification |
  |---|---|
  | Own winding machines | Accelerator |
  | Corrosion-resistance process | Accelerator *if* applied to violin-family strings and approved by the panel; potential pioneer use for touring professionals |
  | Survey and shop-panel data | Accelerator; pioneer opportunity in blind-test data infrastructure |
  | General marketplaces and mass-retail online placement | Irrelevant; drop |
