# Halvard Strings: Good to Great (stewardship run)

**Verdict: Halvard is already great.** It has grown for fourteen straight years. Operating margin has stayed between 24% and 29% in ten of the last eleven years, return on invested capital averages about 34%, and pro-orchestra share rose from 9% to 31%. The job now is to protect what works, not to reinvent it.

**Hedgehog (confirmed):** *Halvard makes the violin-family strings that professional players choose in a blind test, and sells them at full price only through the specialist shops that serve those players.*

**Weakest flywheel link:** delivery through a shrinking shop channel. Cello and bass lead times reached five weeks in the autumn, and North American independent shops fell 3% last year.

**Temptations to refuse:**
1. Halvard Six as proposed.
2. Buying the private-equity student brand.
3. Treating a Listening Room verdict on guitar strings as the answer to a strategy question.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** growth now comes from taking share in a market that grows about 2% a year. Until the board has a growth answer that stays inside the hedgehog, proposals like Halvard Six will keep coming back.

**Numbers that would change this:** share by instrument and region, the revenue split between professional and advanced players, winding-machine utilization, and how concentrated sales are among shops.

`Greatness potential: 72/100`

*Evidence basis: this run had no search, memory or earlier runs to draw on. It is built only on the CFO's dossier, which is dated the start of Year 0. Inferences are marked `[ASSUMPTION]`.*

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

The great version of Halvard is mostly the Halvard that exists today, kept honest. Its Listening Room still decides which strings exist. It keeps winning professional string sections one chair at a time. It keeps those players: 78% are still with Halvard three years after switching. It sells at full price through the specialist shops those players trust, and it reinvests the profit into the winding process and the panel. Its growth comes from extending that loop, not from leaving it. That means winning more violin, viola and bass chairs, more advanced players, and, if the evidence supports it, professional players outside North America and Europe. It survives the move from Annelie Brandt to her successor without a "new direction." In five years the company is still recognizably itself, only larger. It has said no to at least two attractive things, and it is visibly better for having done so.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept (confirmed, not invented)

**Sentence:** Halvard makes the violin-family strings that professional players choose in a blind test, and sells them at full price only through the specialist shops that serve those players.

| Circle | What it is | What it is *not* |
|---|---|---|
| **Best in the world at** | Tone and playability as judged blind by professional violin-family players. It combines a winding process with a testing discipline (the Listening Room) that kills its own products. Evidence: 31% orchestra share, 44% among cellists, three panel kills in five years. | Mass-market volume. Student price points. Consumer retail marketing. Guitar strings, where it has a durability claim from a lab test but no proof that players prefer it. |
| **Economic engine** | A premium of 15–25%, never broken minimum advertised pricing, high loyalty, and steady margins for shops. Together these produce a 61% gross margin and roughly 28% operating margin. | Volume at 31% gross margin. |
| **Passion** | How orchestral strings sound under a professional's bow. The CEO is an acoustics engineer, and the panel's verdict outranks everyone's. | "Pointing the technology at more string players" for its own sake. |

**Denominator:** *operating profit per retained professional player.* This combines share (the annual orchestra survey), retention (the 78% three-year figure) and full-price discipline. It also explains why every past win happened.

**Confirmation tests:**
- *Explains past wins?* Yes. Exiting the mass-market channel, building the testing program and the winding machines, holding MAP, and staying off marketplaces all follow from it.
- *Rules things out?* Yes. It excludes both items on the session agenda.
- *Uncomfortable to commit to?* Yes. It means turning down $19M of projected revenue and a cheap acquisition.
- *Working hypothesis, confirmed if* share keeps rising in violin, viola and bass (not just cello), three-year retention stays at or above about 78%, and operating margin stays within the 24–29% band through the leadership succession.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   ┌─► 1. Listening Room blind-tests every prototype (and kills the losers)
   │            │
   │            ▼

   │            │

   │   3. Professionals switch and stay (31% share; 78% retained at 3 yrs)
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │   4. Advanced players, teachers and shops follow the pros' choice [ASSUMPTION: mechanism]
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │   5. Specialist shops earn steady full-price margin → stock, recommend, reorder
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │   6. Operating profit per retained pro player → funds winding R&D, the panel, cash
   └────────────┘
```

**Weakest link: step 5, delivery through the shops.** The channel is shrinking (North American independents −3%), and the autumn lead-time slip hit cello and bass, the segment where Halvard is strongest. A professional who can't get their usual set for five weeks is the cheapest customer a competitor will ever win.

**Current push (next one or two turns):**
1. Make cello and bass lead times of two weeks or less hold all year round, not just "back to two."
2. Extend step 3 beyond cello. Overall share is 31% and cello is 44%, so the other three instruments average below 31%. That gap is the in-hedgehog growth runway.

**Doom-loop check:** Halvard is not in a doom loop today. Both session items are classic entry points, though. Halvard Six is a new direction meant to restart growth, and the student brand is an acquisition meant to buy momentum. Approving either does not make Halvard bad, but it is how the slide starts.

## 5. Stop-doing list (stewardship: temptations to refuse)

1. **Halvard Six as proposed.** It is a mass-retail launch of guitar strings at 31% gross margin, sold on end-caps. It fails all three circles:
   - *Best in world?* The 2.3× corrosion advantage comes from a lab test. It says nothing about whether guitarists prefer the strings. Long-life guitar strings may already be an established category `[ASSUMPTION]`.
   - *Engine?* If core revenue reaches about $60M by Year 3 `[ASSUMPTION: ~8% growth]`, adding $19M at 31% gross pulls blended gross margin from about 61% to about 54%. That is before four new salespeople, a second shift, and whatever the chain charges for placement. The proposal does not give an operating margin.
   - *Passion?* Nobody inside Halvard tunes a guitar for a living.

   It also reverses the decision that created the company fourteen years ago, and it puts Halvard on mass-retail end-caps next to the specialist shops that carry its premium.
2. **Buying the private-equity student brand at about 1.1× revenue.** This re-enters the segment Halvard deliberately left. It is an acquisition meant to buy growth, from a seller who chose to sell this brand.
3. **Using the Listening Room as a verdict on guitar strings.** The CEO says the question is "who answers it." For product quality inside the hedgehog, the answer is the panel. For whether Halvard should be in guitar at all, the answer is the board, applying the Hedgehog Concept. Forty violin-family professionals cannot judge guitar strings meaningfully. If they approved one, that approval would be treated as permission anyway.
4. **Online marketplaces, or price cuts below MAP, to make up for losing shops.** Protect the channel instead of routing around it.
5. **Adding product lines that compete for winding capacity** before cello and bass lead times are reliably two weeks or less. Halvard Six specifically asks for a second shift on two winding machines.

**Non-negotiable disciplines:** Listening Room approval for every product. MAP, every time. Specialist shops only. The annual 61-orchestra survey. Metals hedged nine months forward. About nine months of operating cash and no debt.

**The framework:** inside these boundaries, the commercial, R&D and plant teams have wide autonomy. The boundaries are violin-family strings, professional and advanced players, and specialist shops. The bureaucracy check finds no rules that look like compensation for having the wrong people.
