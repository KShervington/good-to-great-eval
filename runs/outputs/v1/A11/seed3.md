# Lindqvist Company: Good to Great

## Verdict

**Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** *Be the #1 or #2 food-and-drug superstore in every regional market we serve, let what shoppers actually buy decide what goes in the store, and judge every move by profit per household in that market.*

**Weakest flywheel link:** whether the larger combination stores earn more per household than the small conventional stores. The test stores exist, but their economics are unknown.

**Top three stop-doing items:**
1. Stop putting capital into small conventional stores.
2. Stop staying in markets where Lindqvist cannot reach #1 or #2.
3. Stop treating manufacturing and the family-center division as engines of growth.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** the core format, the small conventional supermarket, is being outgrown. Rival A's price war punishes exactly that format.

**Missing numbers that would change the answer:** margins and ROE against Rival A; market share by metro area; test-store versus conventional-store economics; the consumer-research findings.

Greatness potential: 50/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Lindqvist is a **regional** company, not a national one. In every metro area it serves it is the #1 or #2 grocer. Its stores are large food-and-drug combination stores, with specialty departments chosen by what shoppers buy, not by what the company likes to make. It has left the markets it cannot lead without sentiment and put that capital into the ones it can. Its plants serve as quiet suppliers to a store brand that shoppers trust, not as a second business. Its checkout scanners make it the best in the industry at reading shoppers' "votes" item by item and store by store. It does not try to win the price war by matching Rival A everywhere. It wins by being the most-visited store in fewer places, which makes its distribution, advertising and buying cheaper per household than any rival's in those markets.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

| Circle | Candidates considered | Narrowed to |
|---|---|---|
| **Best in the world at** | Grocery manufacturing; national grocery scale; general merchandise (family centers); low-price leadership; **food-and-drug combination stores**; **consumer-research-driven store design** | Combination food-and-drug superstores, designed from shopper "votes," run at leading share in chosen regions |
| **Can *not* be best at** | n/a | National low-price leadership (Rival A is roughly twice the size of anyone else); food manufacturing as a business; general-merchandise retail; coverage of every market |
| **Economic engine (denominator)** | Profit per store; profit per square foot; profit per plant; **profit per household in a market** | **Profit per household in each regional market.** Share and density lower distribution, advertising and buying costs per household, and larger baskets raise revenue per household. `[ASSUMPTION]`: this has to be checked against market-level P&Ls. |
| **Passion** | Manufacturing pride; store-brand pride; consumer research as a way of working | A 40-year habit of treating purchases as shoppers' votes `[ASSUMPTION]`: the dossier shows the practice exists, not how deeply people care about it. |

**Hedgehog sentence:** *Be the #1 or #2 food-and-drug superstore in every regional market we serve, let what shoppers buy decide the store, and judge every move by profit per household in that market.*

**Does it explain past wins?** Partly. The two moves that look like wins both followed customers: replacing neighborhood stores with supermarkets, and the consumer-research turn about 40 years ago. The pairing of drugstores next to supermarkets fits as well.

**Does it rule things out?** Yes:
- the family-center general-merchandise stores;
- manufacturing growth for its own sake;
- markets where Lindqvist is weak;
- a blanket price match with Rival A.

**Is it uncomfortable?** Yes. It means leaving more markets, slowing down the newest division, and demoting what the last leader spent a decade building.

**Confirmation tests (working hypothesis until these come back):**
1. Test-store economics show higher profit per household than conventional stores after conversion cost.
2. Market-by-market data shows that the markets where Lindqvist ranks #1 or #2 earn clearly more per household than the others.
3. Consumer research and scanner data show shoppers want one-stop food and drug.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   ┌─► (1) Shopper votes: research + scanner data show what households want
   │          │
   │          ▼
   │   (2) Convert/build larger food-and-drug combination stores
   │       with the departments shoppers chose
   │          │
   │          ▼   ◄── WEAKEST LINK (unproven economics)
   │   (3) More trips and bigger baskets per household
   │          │
   │          ▼
   │   (4) #1 or #2 share in the regional market
   │          │
   │          ▼
   │   (5) Density: cheaper distribution, ads, buying; fuller store-brand plants
   │       → higher PROFIT PER HOUSEHOLD
   │          │
   │          ▼
   │   (6) Profit + proceeds from exited markets fund the next conversions
   │       and sharp prices where they matter
   └──────────┘
```

**Current push:**
1. Prove link 2→3 with the test stores' real numbers.
2. Rank every market by share and by its potential to reach #1 or #2. Then commit conversion capital only to the winners.

**Doom-loop check (warning signs found):**
- **New direction after new direction.** Each era has had a different big idea: brand consolidation, then manufacturing, then drugstores and family centers.
- **Big programs to jump-start momentum.** The family-center division, the 56-store West Coast acquisition, and the world's largest dairy plant all fit this pattern.
- **Reaction without understanding.** The price war invites a panicked matching of Rival A.

The loop itself closes. The risk is that leadership treats the combination store as just the next big idea instead of the one it pushes for 15 years.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **No new building or major remodeling of small conventional stores.** Maintain them for cash only until each is converted or closed.
2. **No continued investment in markets with no credible path to #1 or #2** within about five years `[ASSUMPTION on horizon]`. Exit them deliberately, not reactively.
3. **Freeze the family-center division.** No new stores, and a decision on its sale within 12 months.
4. **No new or expanded manufacturing plants.** Keep a plant only if it beats outside supply on store-brand cost and quality. Sell or close the rest.
5. **Do not match Rival A's deep discount across the board.** Price sharply on the items shoppers use to judge stores (identified from research and scanner data) in the markets you lead.
6. **No growth by acquisition.** The antitrust settlement restricts it anyway, and buying share hides weak formats.
7. **No new standalone drugstores outside food-store markets** until the combination model is proven.
8. **No product formulation that trades quality for weight or margin.** This rules out a repeat of the water-and-filler finding.

**Non-negotiable disciplines:**
- Every capital request names its market rank and its effect on profit per household.
- Every store decision cites shopper data.
- Quality audits are done on a fixed schedule with no exceptions.
- Every market is reviewed every year.

**Freedom within the framework:**
- *Inside the boundary:* store managers and regional heads choose the department mix, local pricing and staffing.
- *Outside it:* nobody adds formats, markets or plants.
