# Lindqvist Company: Good to Great Run

## Verdict

**Hedgehog Concept:** Lindqvist runs the best large one-stop food-and-drug stores in the country, designed around what shoppers actually buy, and only in regional markets where it can be first or second.

**Weakest flywheel link:** Capital to build the large stores. A price war is squeezing margins, and money is tied up in plants and in small stores.

**Stop doing:**
1. Remodeling and defending small conventional stores that can't become large combination stores.
2. Competing in metro markets where it can't reach #1 or #2.
3. Letting manufacturing drive capital decisions. Plants exist to serve the stores.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** Its main asset base, small conventional supermarkets, is becoming obsolete during a price war led by a rival twice its size.

**Missing numbers that would change this:** Economics of the test stores versus conventional stores, market share by metro, margins and ROE, and plant returns compared with outside supply.

Greatness potential: 30/100

---

*Evidence basis: this run used only the dossier. Memory, past runs and web research were not available, so the gates use no outside market data. Every inference is marked `[ASSUMPTION]`.*

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Lindqvist is a regional grocer, not a national empire. It holds first or second place in a deliberately chosen set of metro markets, and it leaves the others in an orderly way instead of retreating under pressure. Nearly every store is a large combination store: a full supermarket with specialty departments and a full pharmacy under one roof. Each store is sized, stocked and priced using the company's long-standing habit of treating purchases as shopper "votes." Scanner data now feeds that habit store by store and item by item. Manufacturing is a quiet supplier that keeps the store brand cheaper and more trustworthy than outside supply, and plants that can't meet that bar are closed or sold. Because Lindqvist is dense in every market it serves, its advertising, distribution and plant costs per household fall below those of rivals. That lets it match any price war locally and still earn more per household than its competitors.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

| Circle | Candidates considered | Narrowed to |
|---|---|---|
| **Best in the world at** | (a) national scale: no, Rival A is more than twice the size of any competitor; (b) vertical integration and manufacturing: plants are a cost position, not a reason shoppers choose a store; (c) lowest prices: no, it can't outspend Rival A everywhere; (d) **combination food-and-drug stores run on shopper data**: rare, because few grocers own a drugstore division, and it has decades of consumer research plus first-mover scanners | **(d)**, limited to markets where it leads |

**Hedgehog sentence:** *Be the best at large one-stop food-and-drug stores driven by shopper data, only in markets where we can be first or second, measured by profit per local household.*

**Tests:**
- *Does it explain past wins?* Partly. The consumer-research culture, the drugstore pairing and the scanner pilot all fit.
- *Does it rule things out?* Yes: small stores, markets where it can't lead, the general-merchandise push, and plants as a strategy in themselves.
- *Is it uncomfortable to commit to?* Yes. It means more market exits, closing many stores, and questioning the new dairy plant.

**Confirmation tests (working hypothesis):**
1. Combination test stores beat conventional stores on profit per square foot and on profit per household within 12 months.
2. Profit per household is higher in markets where it is #1 or #2 than in markets where it is #3 or lower.
3. Store-brand products made in its own plants beat outside supply on cost or on quality as measured by shoppers.

## 3. The flywheel

```
 ┌─► 1. Shopper data (research + scanners) tells us what to stock, price, and build
 │                        │
 │                        ▼
 │   2. Build/convert large combination food-and-drug stores
 │      in markets where we are #1 or #2
 │                        │
 │                        ▼
 │   3. More trips + bigger baskets (food + pharmacy + specialty)
 │                        │
 │                        ▼
 │   4. Local density → lower cost per household
 │      (ads, distribution, plant output) → profit per local household ↑
 │                        │
 │                        ▼
 │   5. Cash funds more conversions; weak stores and markets sold
 │      and capital redeployed
 │                        │
 └──── 6. More stores on scanners → richer data ◄──┘
```

**Current push:**
- *Turn 1:* Read the test-store economics honestly and turn the result into a store-format standard.
- *Turn 2:* Rank every market and commit capital only where Lindqvist leads or can reach the lead.

**Weakest link:** the step from 4 to 5. Price-war margins and capital tied up in plants may not produce enough cash to fund conversions quickly. Selling off markets where it can't lead becomes the funding source, not just a defensive retreat.

**Doom-loop check:**
- *Warning signs present.* The company has made a series of market exits with no stated rule, so they look like reactions rather than choices. Three successive emphases have run under three leaders: store closures, then manufacturing, then drug and family-center formats. There is a temptation to match Rival A's discounting everywhere.
- *Not present.* Antitrust limits block acquisitions, which closes off the "big acquisition to jump-start momentum" trap.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. Stop remodeling or extending small conventional stores that can't be converted to the large combination format.
2. Stop operating in metro markets where there is no credible path to #1 or #2 within about five years `[ASSUMPTION: horizon]`. Exit on purpose and on Lindqvist's timetable.
3. Stop treating manufacturing as the strategy. Plants get no new capital unless they beat outside supply on store-brand cost or quality.
4. Stop matching Rival A's discount program everywhere. Defend price only in leading markets, funded by density.
5. Stop expanding the general-merchandise family-center format.
6. Stop letting cost pressure override product quality in processed foods.
7. Stop exiting markets one by one without a stated rule. Every exit should come from the same portfolio test.
