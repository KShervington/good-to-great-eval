# Good to Great: Harlan Pacific Corporation

## Verdict

**A good bank that is slipping. It needs to narrow hard and get cheap to run before deregulation removes its cushion.**

- **Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** *Harlan Pacific will be the lowest-cost, best-run major bank in its home state and region, lending to the businesses, property markets and households it knows best, and it will judge every activity by profit per employee.*
- **Weakest flywheel link:** turning lower costs into *profitable* home-market lending without loosening credit standards, especially in real estate.
- **Top three stop-doing items:**
  1. Overseas expansion and minority stakes.
  2. The branch-count race with Rival B.
  3. New deregulation-era ventures outside core banking.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** the regulated cheap-deposit subsidy is ending. Cost of funds will rise while the bank's overhead is still built for the old world.
- **Numbers that would change this answer:**
  - Profit by business line.
  - Profit per employee and cost-to-revenue ratio versus peers.
  - Size of the Latin American exposure.
  - Real estate share of the loan book.

Greatness potential: 30/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Harlan Pacific has stopped trying to be a little of everything: a global bank, a consumer giant, a financial supermarket. It has become the best-run bank in the markets it knows. It treats every dollar of overhead as a dollar taken from shareholders. It operates fewer, more productive branches and has pulled back from overseas ventures that produced losses rather than customers. It lends with discipline to home-state businesses, property developers and households it understands better than distant competitors do. As deregulation forces every bank to pay market rates for deposits, Harlan's low cost base becomes its competitive weapon. Banks that grew fat under rate ceilings get squeezed, and Harlan uses its earnings and capital to absorb their customers and branches in its home markets at low cost. It is measured relentlessly by profit per employee. It does not depend on one CEO, because cost discipline, line-level profit visibility and independent credit judgment are built into how it runs.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence:** *Be the lowest-cost, best-run major bank in our home state and region, serving the businesses, property markets and households we know best, and measure everything by profit per employee.*

| Circle | Candidates considered | Kept | Ruled out, and why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Best in the world at** | Global/international banking; dominant state consumer bank; financial supermarket; large-corporate lending; home-state real estate and middle-market lending; lowest-cost operator among major Western banks | **Lowest-cost, best-run operator in home markets**, with real estate and business lending where it has proven skill | **International:** a record of losses (the West German stake, the failed London joint venture, currency losses). **State consumer leadership:** Rival B is far larger and long ahead. **Supermarket:** no evidence of capability. **Large-corporate:** likely a price-taker against money-center banks `[ASSUMPTION]` |
| **Economic engine** | Profit per branch; profit per customer; spread per deposit dollar; **profit per employee** | **Profit per employee** | Profit per branch is too narrow. Spread per deposit dollar is being squeezed by deregulation and is partly outside the bank's control. Profit per employee captures the cost and overhead problem directly. |
| **Passion** | The century-old franchise and its home-state roots; operator pride in running a tight, profitable bank (the new CEO's real estate record) | **Pride in being the best-run bank: frugality as a craft** `[ASSUMPTION]` | Glamour of global reach: the evidence suggests it fed ambition, not results. |

**Denominator:** profit per employee. The alternative to test is profit per branch.

**Confirmation tests:**
- **Does it explain past wins?** Mostly yes. Real estate lending, a focused business in a known market, became a strong contributor. Most losses came from far-flung ventures and costly expansion. The shared credit card was a home-market consumer play, and its profitability is unknown.
- **Does it rule things out?** Yes: overseas growth, new ventures, the branch race and big-corporate price-taking.
- **Is it uncomfortable to commit to?** Yes. It means closing branches, retreating overseas, cutting headcount and telling a 16-year narrative of expansion that it was wrong.
- **Evidence that would confirm it:**
  - Line-level profit shows home-state real estate and business lending beating the cost of capital, and international and parts of large-corporate not doing so.
  - Peer data shows Harlan's cost-to-revenue ratio is high enough that a top-quartile cost position is a large, achievable earnings lever.

```
    households) ── disciplined credit                             │
   │                                                              │
   ▼                                                              │
[4] Higher earnings + stronger capital                            │
   │                                                              │
   ▼                                                              │
[5] Absorb weaker home-market banks' customers/branches and       │
    strip their cost (in-market only, cost-integration skill)     │
   │                                                              │
   └────────── scale on a low cost base ──────────────────────────┘
```

**Current push (next one or two turns):**
1. **Turn 1:** cost and focus. Freeze overseas expansion, start branch consolidation, and build line-level profit reporting so cuts are made with facts, not across the board.
2. **Turn 3:** credit discipline. Make sure lower costs show up as better pricing and profitable growth, not as a reach for yield in real estate.

**Weakest link:** the step from [2] to [3]. Lower costs only become greatness if the bank grows profitably with them. The risk is that it either just shrinks, or loosens credit to grow. The CEO's own real estate background makes real estate the tempting place to push, and the most dangerous one.

**Doom-loop check:**
- **The predecessor era shows classic doom-loop signs:** new direction after new direction (card consortium, Asian and Latin American offices, German stake, London JV, statewide branch build-out), earnings decelerating, "time to slow down," then an abrupt exit.
- **The risk for the new CEO:** a large, announced "transformation program," or a big acquisition to jump-start momentum before turns 1–3 are working.
- **The rule:** acquisitions only after the cost flywheel is visibly turning, only in-market, and only where Harlan's cost discipline is the reason the deal works.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop overseas expansion.** No new foreign offices, joint ventures or minority stakes. Wind down or sell those that don't serve home-state customers.
2. **Stop new foreign sovereign and cross-border lending.** Run off Latin American exposure as recoveries allow.
3. **Stop the branch-count race with Rival B.** Close or consolidate the least profitable branches, and judge the network by profit per branch and per employee, not by coverage.
4. **Stop chasing deregulation-era new ventures** (non-bank financial businesses) unless one passes the three-circle test.
5. **Stop large-corporate lending where Harlan is a price-taker** with no relationship edge `[ASSUMPTION, pending line-profit data]`.
6. **Stop paying up for rate-sensitive deposits that don't bring a profitable relationship.**
7. **Stop treating overhead as fixed.** Every staff function and executive perk re-justifies its cost each year `[ASSUMPTION about current practice]`.

**Non-negotiable disciplines:**
- Monthly line- and branch-level profit review.
- Every expense justified against profit per employee.
- One credit standard, enforced by an independent credit function.
- Dual control and audit in every branch.

**Freedom within a framework:**
- **The framework:** geography (home markets), the cost ceiling, credit policy and the three-circle test for any new activity.
- **The freedom:** line heads own their budgets, staffing and how they hit their profit targets.

**Bureaucracy check:** the fraud points to too little control in the right places, not too much. Add controls at the point of cash and credit. Cut layers of headquarters review that exist only because line profit isn't visible.
