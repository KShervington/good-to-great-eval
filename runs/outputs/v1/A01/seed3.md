# Good to Great: Halvorsen-Pike Corporation

## Verdict

**Halvorsen-Pike is good but not yet great, and it is built around the wrong core.**

**Hedgehog:** Be the best in the world at paper-based consumer products that people know by brand, judged by profit per consumer brand.

**Weakest flywheel link:** product superiority. The diaper leaks, and the company has little nonwoven expertise.

**Top three stop-doing items:**
1. Stop funding the printing-paper and newsprint mills, and sell them, including the namesake mill.
2. Stop spreading capital across every business by tradition.
3. Drop the plan to run the air fleet as a charter business.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** the company cannot pay for mill modernization and a consumer war with Rival P at the same time. Today's habits send the most money to the slow-growing half.

**Missing numbers that would change this answer:**
- Return on capital by segment. If consumer products don't earn more than the mills, this verdict should be revisited.
- Blind-test leakage results for the diaper against Rival P.
- Growth rates for each category.

Greatness potential: 35/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Halvorsen-Pike is a consumer-products company that happens to make paper, not a paper company that happens to sell some consumer products. It has sold the commodity printing-paper, newsprint, pulp-for-sale, sawmill and timberland businesses. The proceeds have gone into brand advertising, absorbent-product research and consumer distribution. It keeps only the manufacturing that feeds its own brands. It holds the category it invented, facial tissue, as an unshakable base. It competes head-to-head with Rival P in diapers and personal care, and wins on product superiority rather than ad budget. A plain-spoken, self-critical leadership team runs every business on a brand-level P&L. Its total returns beat the market for fifteen-plus years and keep doing so after the current CEO.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence (working hypothesis):**
> *Halvorsen-Pike will be the best in the world at paper-based consumer products that people know by brand, and it will run the company on profit per consumer brand.*

| Circle | Candidates considered | Kept | Ruled out, and why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Best in world at** | (a) low-cost commodity milling; (b) timber and land management; (c) creating and leading branded consumer paper categories; (d) every absorbent category at once, head-on with Rival P | **(c)**, with diapers and feminine care inside it only once the product is superior | (a) The mills only reach cost parity, and Rival S is larger and more profitable. (b) The land is valuable but gives no best-in-world edge. (d) Rival P outspends the company and currently has the better product. |
| **Economic engine (denominator)** | profit per ton; profit per mill; profit per household; **profit per consumer brand** | **Profit per consumer brand** `[ASSUMPTION, to be confirmed by segment data]` | Profit per ton rewards the commodity cycle. Profit per household is too hard to measure at Year 0. |
| **Passion** | papermaking craft; the mill town; everyday products people use | **Fiber craft applied to products people touch every day** | Pure mill identity can't survive the pivot. The passion has to move from the mill to the product. This is the shakiest circle. |

**What it cannot be best at:** commodity coated and printing papers, newsprint, market pulp, lumber, aviation services, and outspending Rival P in every category at once.

**Tests:**
- **Explains past wins?** Yes. The company's one undisputed triumph is inventing facial tissue and still leading it, which is a branded, category-creating consumer product. Its mill businesses have produced twenty years of below-market returns.
- **Rules things out?** Yes: about half the company.
- **Uncomfortable?** Very. It means selling the namesake mill.

**Confirmation evidence that would turn this into a PASS:**
1. Segment return on capital shows that consumer products earn more than their cost of capital, or clearly could at scale, and that the printing, newsprint and pulp businesses don't over a full cycle.
2. A reformulated diaper matches or beats Rival P on leakage in blind tests within roughly two product cycles.
3. Consumer categories keep growing faster than printing papers, which is the dossier's direction. Exact rates are needed.

**Fallback if test 2 fails:** narrow the Hedgehog to sanitary tissue (facial, bath and towel) and exit diapers. That is a safer and smaller company, and it fights on Rival S's home ground. It would be a good result, but probably not a great one.

## 3. The flywheel

```
        ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────┐
        ▼                                              │
 [1] Superior paper-based products                     │
     (cellulose + nonwoven know-how)                   │
        │                                              │
        ▼                                              │
 [2] Consumer preference and brand trust               │
        │                                              │
        ▼                                              │
 [3] Shelf space, distribution and category share      │
        │                                              │
        ▼                                              │
 [4] PROFIT PER CONSUMER BRAND  ◄── denominator        │
        │                                              │
        ▼                                              │
 [5] Reinvestment in R&D, brand advertising and talent ┘
        ▲
        └── one-time boost: divestiture proceeds
            (mills, timberlands)
```

**The loop closes:** better products build preference. Preference wins shelf space and share. Share raises profit per brand. That profit funds the research and advertising that make the next product better.

**Weakest link: component 1, product superiority.** The diaper has leakage complaints and has fallen behind on improvements. The company's research is all in cellulose, while absorbent products are moving toward synthetic nonwovens. Advertising spend can't make up for a product that leaks.

**Current push, the next two turns:**
1. Fix the diaper before buying more advertising for it. Get nonwoven capability by hiring, partnering or making a narrow acquisition.
2. Defend facial tissue, which is the cash-and-credibility base, while the sale of the mills is prepared.

**Doom-loop check:** warning signs are already present.
- The company has made a string of specialty-paper acquisitions over 20 years with no unifying logic.
- It has no stated strategy.
- It allocates capital by tradition.
- It launched a diaper and then let it fall behind.

Those are signs of reaction without understanding. The planned divestiture is a single large move, but it is **not** a doom-loop move as long as it is made once, in service of the Hedgehog, and followed by years of steady pushes. It becomes a doom-loop move if it is followed by more reinventions.

**Leadership read (Gate 2):**

He intends to keep working through cancer treatment, which is fierce resolve. His ambition appears directed at the company, not at himself, and he is not a charismatic leader who suppresses bad news.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop reinvesting in coated and printing-paper and newsprint mills.** Sell them, including the namesake mill. Sell to operators who will keep running them, not to buyers who will close them `[ASSUMPTION that buyers exist]`.
2. **Stop allocating capital by tradition or across every business evenly.** Capital goes only where it raises profit per consumer brand.
3. **Kill the proposal to run the air fleet as a charter and maintenance business,** and shrink the fleet.
4. **Sell the western timberlands and sawmills.** They are valuable assets outside the Hedgehog, and they are the main funding source for the pivot.
5. **Stop marketing products that aren't superior.** No major increase in diaper advertising until the leakage problem is fixed and blind tests prove it.
6. **Stop acquiring specialty papers** (envelope, cigarette and similar). Sell or harvest the ones the company owns.
7. **Exit the Canadian newsprint joint venture.**
8. **Stop measuring success at board level in tons, mill efficiency or capacity.** Replace those measures with brand-level profit and share.
9. **Stop selling pulp on the open market.** Keep pulp capacity only where it feeds the company's own brands more cheaply or at better quality `[ASSUMPTION: to be tested with cost data]`.

**Non-negotiable disciplines:**
- Every capital request answers one question: does this raise profit per consumer brand?
- Every business has a monthly P&L at brand level.
- A product must win a superiority test before it gets a significant advertising budget.
- A quarterly brutal-facts review is held with share and complaint data on the table.

**Framework boundaries (freedom and responsibility):**
- *Inside* paper-based consumer products, brand teams have wide autonomy over product, pricing and marketing.
- *Outside* it, the answer is no unless the board changes the Hedgehog.

**Bureaucracy check:** the even spread of capital is a political truce between mill businesses rather than a rule anyone believes in. It disappears once the portfolio is focused and the right leaders run each brand.

## 7. Core ideology and BHAG

**Core values (preserve):**
1. Face reality honestly, starting with ourselves.
2. Make products a family can trust every day.
3. Stay plain and frugal, with no status trappings.
4. Practice craft in fiber.

**Core purpose:** to make everyday personal care cleaner, healthier and more comfortable for families.

**BHAG (20-year horizon):** *Outsell Rival P in the categories Rival P created.*

**Mechanisms that let this run without the current CEO (clock building):**
- A succession bench with a named number two.
- A brand-level P&L.
- The capital-allocation rule.
- The product-superiority test before advertising spend.
- A quarterly brutal-facts review.
