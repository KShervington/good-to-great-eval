# Good to Great: Meridian Radio Corporation (run as of late summer, Year 0)

## Verdict

**Meridian is already great, and it is showing the first stage of decline.** It passes Collins's performance test: its stock rose more than 100x, beating the market by more than 10x; it is No. 1 in black-and-white; it has no debt; and it kept performing after the founder died. This is therefore a stewardship run. The job is to protect the flywheel, and the flywheel is under threat.

- **Hedgehog:** Build the television that dealers and repairmen trust most, and earn more profit per dealer than anyone in the industry.
- **Weakest link:** Meridian buys its reliability with hand labor and inspection, so that reliability costs more with every set it sells.
- **Temptations to refuse:** a debt-funded race to beat Rival R in color; open-ended pay-TV spending; selling "hand-wired" when the real promise is "doesn't break."
- **Most urgent fact:** Leadership has publicly dismissed both solid-state circuits and low-cost imports. Those are the two forces aimed at its core.
- **Numbers that would change this:** cost and failure rates for hand-wired versus printed-circuit sets; the import price trajectory; color market share; current debt and capital spending.

Greatness potential: 30/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

The great Meridian treats quality as an outcome, not a method. Its promise is the lowest field-failure rate and the easiest service in the industry. It does not promise hand-wiring. It moves to printed circuits and solid-state electronics deliberately and ahead of the curve. It designs modular chassis that repairmen can service faster than they could service the old hand-wired sets, so the technology shift strengthens Meridian's moat instead of dissolving it. It adds color capacity in stages, paid for from earnings and tied to dealer sell-through, not to a share race against Rival R. It watches the low-priced imports as a future rival moving upmarket, not as a curiosity. A technical leader sits at the top table, a named succession plan exists, and pay TV is either a licensed side bet or ended. Meridian is then the brand dealers want to stock and repairmen want to recommend, in color and solid-state as it was in black-and-white.

**Why profit per dealer.** Meridian's two clearest strengths outside the product itself both act at the dealer: repairmen recommend the sets, and its control of inventory and dealer stock is among the best in the industry. Dealers who make money on Meridian push Meridian. A dealer who moves few sets gains little from a higher margin on each one.

**Tests:**
- **Explains past wins?** Yes. The No. 1 position in black-and-white was built on quality reputation and channel strength.
- **Rules things out?** Yes: pay TV, stripped-down sets to fight imports on price, a capacity race for color unit share, and marketing the method instead of the result.
- **Uncomfortable?** Yes. It means giving up "hand-crafted" as Meridian's identity, and accepting second place in color units if that is what disciplined growth produces.

**Confirmation tests (12 months):**
1. A printed-circuit or solid-state chassis matches or beats the hand-wired field-failure rate at lower unit cost.
2. Profit per dealer holds or rises while color volume grows.
3. Repairmen's preference for Meridian holds when they service the new chassis.

## 3. The flywheel

```
  (1) Reliable, easy-to-service sets
        (lowest field-failure rate)
               │
               ▼
  (2) Repairmen and dealers recommend Meridian
               │
               ▼
  (3) Full-price sell-through → PROFIT PER DEALER
               │
               ▼
  (4) Tight inventory keeps dealers profitable and loyal
               │
               ▼
  (5) Earnings (not debt) fund engineering and production
               │
               ▼
  (6) Designed-in reliability at FALLING unit cost ──► back to (1)
```

**Weakest link: step 6.** Today Meridian gets reliability from hand-wiring and inspecting one set in six, not from design. As volume grows and price pressure rises, step 6 runs backwards: more reliability costs more money, and that drains steps 3 and 5.

**Current push:**
1. Measure field-failure rates and unit costs for hand-wired versus printed-circuit chassis.
2. Engineer a modular solid-state chassis whose serviceability repairmen can verify.

**Doom-loop check (signs already present):**
- **Reaction without understanding:** the goal of being "No. 1 in color like we were in black-and-white" is set by reference to Rival R, not by the Hedgehog Concept.
- **Big program to jump-start momentum:** the new color tube plant, and possibly borrowing to fund more capacity `[ASSUMPTION: scale and financing not in dossier]`.
- **A multi-year bet outside the core:** pay TV.
- **A record of lateness at transitions:** color was held back for years, and the move to transistors has been slow. Neither is a doom loop yet, but together they make one likely.

## 5. Stop-doing list (stewardship mode: real temptations only)

1. **Stop advertising "hand-crafted" as the promise.** Advertise reliability and serviceability, the outcome customers actually buy.
2. **Refuse a debt-funded race for color unit share.** Add capacity in stages tied to dealer sell-through, paid for from earnings.
3. **Stop open-ended pay-TV investment.** Set a spending cap and a decision date tied to the regulatory ruling.
4. **Stop dismissing low-priced imports in public and in private.** Replace opinions with tracked prices, volumes and teardowns.
5. **Refuse a panic response to imports** with stripped-down, low-margin sets that dilute the brand. The answer is lower cost at Meridian quality, not lower quality.
6. **Stop inspecting quality in as the main method.** Design it in.

**Non-negotiable disciplines:**
- Field-failure rate is reported every month.
- No borrowing for capacity without a dealer sell-through trigger.
- Every new chassis is serviceability-tested with repairmen before launch.

**Framework:** Engineering is free to choose any technology, including hand-wiring, transistors and ICs, provided field-failure and serviceability targets are met at lower cost.

**Bureaucracy check:** inspecting one worker's output in six suggests inspection is making up for process design, not for the wrong people.
