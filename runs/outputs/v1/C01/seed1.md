# Good to Great: Wexley Medical Corporation

## Verdict

**Wexley is good but not yet great, and its position is fragile.** It has a 35-year reputation for practical, surgeon-designed products, and the trade still calls its turning frame by the founder's name. It also has no working sales force, no budget, and no source of new products other than an 80-year-old founder.

- **Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** Wexley makes surgeon-designed tools that make caregivers faster and care cheaper. It competes only in narrow niches it can lead, and it measures itself by **profit per niche**.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** a results-paid sales force working close to surgeons. Today there is virtually none.
- **Top three stop-doing items:** (1) chasing implants to answer Rival H; (2) paying salespeople flat salaries unconnected to results; (3) routing every decision through the founding family.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** nobody can say which product lines make money.
- **Missing numbers that would change this answer:** margins by product line, bed versus instrument profitability, market share by niche, and recent earnings history.

Greatness potential: 35/100

---

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

In its great version, Wexley is the company orthopedic surgeons and hospital caregivers call first for **practical tools that save time and cost**. Those tools are powered surgical instruments, plus specialized patient-handling equipment where Wexley already leads. It holds the #1 or #2 position in each narrow niche it enters and leaves niches where it can't. A results-paid specialist sales force lives in operating rooms and wards. The reps bring clinicians' problems back to engineers, the engineers turn them into products that meet the founder's design rule, and the profit from each niche funds the next turn. Product-line managers own their budgets and goals. The company no longer depends on one inventor or one family, and it has a long record of steady, compounding earnings growth before any public offering.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence (working hypothesis):** *Wexley builds surgeon-designed tools that make caregivers faster and care cheaper, and competes only in niches it can lead.*

| Circle | Candidates considered | Narrowed to |
|---|---|---|
| **Best in the world at** | Hospital beds broadly; implants and bone cement; powered orthopedic instruments; specialized patient-handling equipment; clinician-originated practical design | **Powered orthopedic and surgical instruments, plus specialized patient-handling niches it already leads.** It **cannot** be best at implants: it has no capability there, and a pharma-backed rival has a head start. It probably cannot be best at general-purpose hospital beds either `[ASSUMPTION: no share data]`. |
| **Economic engine** | Profit per bed sold; profit per hospital account; profit per sales rep; profit per surgeon customer; profit per niche | **Profit per product niche** in which Wexley is #1 or #2. Profit per field sales rep is the runner-up and should be tested once real numbers exist. |
| **Passion** | Clinician problem-solving; craftsmanship; family legacy | **Solving the practical problems clinicians face at the bedside and in the operating room.** This is the founder's design rule. Whether employees share it is `[ASSUMPTION]`. |

**Confirmation tests:**
1. **Profitability:** a line-level P&L shows the instruments and specialty patient-handling niches earn above-average margins.
2. **Market position:** Wexley holds #1 or #2 share in at least two of those niches.
3. **Pipeline:** non-founder engineers can produce a commercially successful product that meets the design rule within about three years.

**Why it fits:**
- **It explains past wins.** The turning frame, cast saw, bone saw and micro saw all came from a clinician fixing a practical problem.
- **It rules things out.** Implants, commodity beds and unrelated diversification fall outside it.
- **It is uncomfortable.** It may mean shrinking part of the 70% bed and stretcher base, and it means watching Rival H grow in implants without following.

**Current push (next two turns):**
- **Rebuild component 4**, starting in the instrument line, where surgeon contact matters most. Pay reps on results, not salary alone.
- **Make component 5 measurable** by installing the first budget and a P&L for each product line. Without that, nobody can tell whether the wheel is turning.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Chasing implants or bone cement** to keep pace with Rival H.
2. **Paying salespeople flat salaries** unconnected to results.
3. **Routing every decision through the founding family.** Delegate within budgets and goals.
4. **Selling general-purpose beds in segments where Wexley isn't #1 or #2.** Act on this only after line-level data confirms it.
5. **Pursuing an IPO** before at least two clean budget cycles and audited line-level earnings.
6. **Expanding the international sales build-out** ahead of a working domestic sales force.
7. **Launching any product that fails the founder's rule** of making caregivers more efficient or reducing the cost of treatment.
8. **Buying companies to jump-start growth** during the transition.

## 7. Core ideology and the BHAG

**Core values:**
1. Every product makes caregivers more efficient or reduces the cost of care.
2. Clinicians define the problem.
3. Practical over flashy.
4. Every manager owns measurable results.
5. Honest dealing with hospitals and with each other.

**Core purpose:** to help caregivers treat patients better, faster and at lower cost.

**BHAG (10–30 years):** to be the world's leading maker of powered surgical instruments, holding #1 or #2 in every niche Wexley serves.

**Clock-building mechanisms:**
- an annual budget plus quarterly goal reviews for each manager;
- a P&L for each niche, with a manager who owns it;
- a clinician advisory panel that feeds the engineering pipeline;
- results-based pay for sales;
- a written successor for every key seat, including the CEO;
- a product-review step that applies the founder's design rule.
