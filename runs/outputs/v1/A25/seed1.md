# Good to Great: Dunmore-Kessler Corporation

## Verdict

**Good, not yet great, and drifting toward a doom loop.**

- **Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** Dunmore-Kessler takes communications technology proven in space and defense programs and makes it the most dependable equipment in a few chosen commercial communications markets. That includes *electronic* printing systems. It excludes mechanical printing.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** turning government-funded technology into commercial *profit*. So far the launches have cost earnings instead of adding to them.
- **Top three stop-doing items:**
  1. No new acquisitions until each existing one passes a returns review.
  2. No more investment in hot-metal typesetting or mechanical-only printing products.
  3. Stop promoting managers for launching products. Promote them for earning commercial profit.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** earnings have been weak for five years while the company keeps buying businesses, and nobody can yet show which business earns its capital.
- **Missing numbers that would change this answer:** profit and return on capital by segment, printing market-share trend, and the size of the government backlog.

Greatness potential: 38/100

---

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

The great Dunmore-Kessler is a focused communications-electronics company, not a printing company with electronics attached. Its engineers do leading-edge work on government programs: tracking, telemetry, satellite links and data systems. A disciplined, small number of those technologies move into commercial markets where reliability earns a premium: broadcast transmission, microwave and radio links, data communications, and electronic composition and controls for printing customers. Every unit reports return on capital. Managers are rewarded for commercial profit, not for launches. The company stops buying growth and starts compounding it. Mechanical printing is either made electronic or managed for cash and eventually separated. The company is run from where its engineers are, and it no longer depends on one chairman or on the federal budget cycle.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

| **Economic engine** | Sales growth. Profit per press shipped. Profit per contract. | **Passion** | Printing heritage (headquarters, long-tenured staff). Engineering and frontier problems (the acquired aerospace company's founders and staff). | **The engineers' passion for hard communications problems.** The dossier says the company bought the aerospace firm "for its engineering talent as much as its earnings." `[ASSUMPTION: that talent is still in place and engaged]` |

**Tests:**
- *Does it explain past wins?* Partly. Electronics grew to about half of sales by Year −3, and the acquired aerospace company grew quickly on exactly this capability.
- *Does it rule things out?* Yes. It rules out mechanical printing R&D, hot metal, corrugated machinery, and acquisitions made to add sales.
- *Is it uncomfortable to commit to?* Very. It demotes the founding business.

**Confirmation tests (evidence that would make this a PASS):**
1. Segment data shows electronics and communications earning at least as much on capital employed as printing.
2. At least one transfer pipeline, from government technology to a commercial product, earns margins above the company average within 24 months.
3. Electronics applied to printing, as promised in the merger thesis, wins paying orders from printing customers within 2–3 years. If it doesn't, printing has no place in the Hedgehog Concept and should be separated.

## 3. The flywheel

```
 ┌──► 1. Government programs fund leading-edge communications/data engineering
 │                │
 │                ▼
 │    2. Engineers produce proven, high-reliability technology
 │                │
 │                ▼
 │    3. A FEW technologies are transferred into chosen commercial products   ◄── WEAKEST LINK
 │       (broadcast, microwave/radio links, data comms, electronic printing systems)
 │                │
 │                ▼
 │    4. Commercial products earn premium margins and repeat customers
 │                │
 │                ▼
 │    5. Rising profit per government-funded development dollar funds internal R&D
 │       and retains and attracts top engineers
 │                │
 │                ▼
 └─── 6. A stronger engineering bench wins better government programs
```

**Current push (the next one or two turns):**
1. **Make link 3→4 earn money.** Choose two transfer pipelines, not ten, and run each to a margin target with a go/no-go date.
   - *Candidate A:* electronic composition and editing systems for newspaper and printing customers. This combines the data-terminal acquisition, the typesetting customer base, and the merger thesis.
   - *Candidate B:* satellite and microwave know-how applied to broadcast transmission and point-to-point links.
2. **Fold last year's two acquisitions into those pipelines.** Don't run them as freestanding businesses.

**Doom-loop check: warning signs present.**
- Five acquisitions in about 15 years, two of them last year for $25.5M cash, while earnings stagnated. That looks like using acquisitions to jump-start momentum.
- Several new product lines are dragging on profit at the same time.
- The integration problems listed in the dossier show the company reacting without fully understanding what it has bought.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **New acquisitions:** none until each existing acquisition has a written returns autopsy. Minimum 24 months. `[ASSUMPTION on the period]`
2. **Hot-metal typesetting:** no new development. Harvest for cash and plan the exit.
3. **Mechanical-only printing products:** no development spending unless electronics is the differentiator.
4. **Corrugated-paper machinery:** stop investing and evaluate a sale.
5. **Rewarding launches:** stop promoting managers for starting commercial product lines. Promote them for commercial profit and return on capital.
6. **Commercial markets without a technology edge:** don't enter markets where large conglomerates have scale and Dunmore-Kessler has no government-derived advantage. This includes stand-alone commodity computer terminals.
7. **Government work for volume only:** stop bidding programs that neither earn good margins on their own nor feed a commercial pipeline.
8. **Running the company from two centers** with strategy in one city and the engineering core in another.
