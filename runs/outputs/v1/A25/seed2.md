# Good to Great: Dunmore-Kessler Corporation

## Verdict

**Good, not yet great, and drifting toward the doom loop.**

- **Hedgehog (working hypothesis):** Turn government-funded communications and information technology into the best commercial communications equipment, earning more profit per engineering dollar than anyone.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** converting government technology into commercial products that customers will pay for.
- **Top three stop-doing items:**
  1. Stop acquiring until the current integrations pay.
  2. Stop investing in hot-metal typesetting and other purely mechanical lines.
  3. Stop promoting managers for the number of products they transfer. Reward commercial profit instead.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** Revenue keeps climbing through acquisitions (four deals in six years), but earnings have stalled for five years. Growth is being bought, not earned.
- **Numbers that would change this answer:** segment profit and return on capital (printing vs. electronics), the size of the government backlog, and commercial market shares.

`Greatness potential: 30/100`

---

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

The great Dunmore-Kessler is a focused communications and information-equipment company, not a two-headed conglomerate. Government space and defense programs pay for frontier engineering. A disciplined, market-led process then turns the best of that engineering into commercial products: broadcast, radio and microwave links, data communications, and, only if it earns its place, electronic printing systems. Each engineering dollar therefore earns twice. Earnings grow faster than sales without acquisitions. Every unit reports its return on capital. Leadership sits close to where the engineering happens. The 80-year printing heritage is honored by turning that business into an electronic information-handling business, or by passing it to an owner who will invest in it. It is not kept on out of sentiment.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence (working hypothesis):** *Turn government-proven communications and information technology into the best commercial communications equipment, measured by profit per engineering dollar.*

| Circle | Candidates considered | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **Best in the world at** | (a) High-reliability communications and data systems that move signals and information, from satellite to broadcast to point-to-point links. (b) Mechanical printing presses. (c) Defense data systems as a standalone business. (d) General electronics and computers. | **(a)** is the only candidate with best-in-world potential in a narrow enough world. **(b)** leads at home but is mechanical, in a shift its own management calls electronic. **(c)** puts it head-to-head with larger primes. **(d)** means facing bigger conglomerates with no edge. |
| **Cannot be best at** | General computing, broad consumer or commercial electronics against large conglomerates, hot-metal typesetting, and mechanical printing as a long-term growth engine. | Ruled out. |
| **Economic engine** | Profit per acquisition dollar (this rewards deal-making, so rejected). Profit per sales dollar (generic). Profit per installed system (too narrow). **Profit per engineering dollar**, counting both company-funded and customer-funded engineering. | **Profit per engineering dollar.** The company's distinctive asset is engineering talent, and the government funds much of it. The engine works when that engineering is paid for once by a contract and monetized again in commercial markets. |
| **Passion** | Engineering culture of the acquired aerospace company, which was founded by two engineers. Printing-craft pride in the original business. | Communications engineering `[ASSUMPTION: passion inferred from founding history and the merger rationale; not evidenced in the dossier]`. |

**Tests:**
- **Does it explain past wins?** Mostly yes. The fastest-growing piece, the aerospace electronics company, and the broadcast and radio acquisitions all sit in communications. It does not explain the printing franchise, which is the uncomfortable point.
- **Does it rule things out?** Yes: mechanical printing expansion, general computing, and acquisitions outside communications.
- **Is it uncomfortable?** Yes. It demotes the founding business.

**On printing:** mechanical printing sits outside the Hedgehog. Printing comes back inside only if it becomes an information-handling business, meaning electronic typesetting, editing and press control. That would be the management thesis made real. There should be a 12–18 month decision gate: either an electronic printing line wins paying customers, or the company prepares to separate printing and pass it to an owner who will invest in it.

**Confirmation evidence needed:**
1. Segment data shows that commercial products derived from government technology earn higher margins than the rest.
2. Profit per engineering dollar can be measured by unit, and the communications units lead on it.
3. Printing's return on capital, set against its reinvestment needs, settles the decision gate.

## 3. The flywheel

```
 │              │

 │       a paying commercial customer  ◄── WEAKEST LINK
 │              │
 │              ▼
 │    4. Commercial communications products (broadcast, radio/
 │       microwave links, data comms, electronic printing if it earns it)
 │              │
 │              ▼
 │    5. ↑ Profit per engineering dollar; earnings less tied
 │       to federal budget swings
 │              │
 │              ▼
 └── 6. Reinvest in engineers + credibility wins bigger programs
```

**Current push (next one or two turns):**
1. Fix link 3. Put a commercial marketing leader beside the engineers, and pick transfers by customer evidence rather than by technology availability.
2. Make link 5 visible. Measure profit per engineering dollar and return on capital by unit, so the loop can be steered.

**Doom-loop check:** several signs are present.
- Acquisitions are being used to jump-start momentum: four deals in six years, including $25.5M in cash last year.
- Five years of weak earnings have been explained by integration and launch costs.
- Multiple integrations are running at once.

The technology-transfer direction itself has been consistent, which counts in its favor. The risk is that buying units substitutes for turning the wheel.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop acquiring** until open integrations hit their milestones and segment return on capital is reported. Suggested moratorium: about 18–24 months `[ASSUMPTION]`.
2. **Stop new investment in hot-metal typesetting** and in mechanical-only printing product development.
3. **Stop rewarding the count of transfers.** Reward commercial profit from transferred technology instead.
4. **Stop competing broadly against the electronics conglomerates** where there is no engineering edge. That includes general computing and terminals sold as computers rather than as communications.
5. **Stop treating printing and electronics as equal claimants on capital.** Allocate by return on capital and fit with the Hedgehog.
6. **Stop bidding government programs that build nothing reusable.** Favor programs that deepen the communications and data base.
7. **Stop running the core business from a distance.** Senior leadership is several states from the main electronics operations. Review where executive presence should sit.

## 7. Core ideology and the BHAG
