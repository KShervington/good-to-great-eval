# Good to Great: Dunmore-Kessler Corporation

## Verdict

**Good, not yet great.** Dunmore-Kessler is two companies sharing one name and one flat earnings line.

- **Hedgehog (working hypothesis):** Turn government-proven electronics into the most reliable commercial systems for moving information (broadcast, radio links, data), and judge every unit by profit per engineer.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** turning government technology into commercial products that earn money. The whole strategy rests on it, and nothing in the dossier shows it working yet.
- **Top three stop-doing items:**
  1. Acquisitions, for 24 months.
  2. Growth investment in mechanical printing, hot-metal typesetting and corrugated machinery.
  3. Promoting managers for converting defense work, rather than for commercial profit.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** Sales rose past $380M while earnings growth stalled for five years. The company is buying revenue, not returns.
- **Numbers that would change this answer:** segment profit and return on capital, the size of the government backlog, and printing's market share and trend.

Greatness potential: 28/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

The great Dunmore-Kessler is a **communications-technology company**, not an industrial conglomerate. Government space and defense programs pay for it to solve the hardest problems in transmitting and handling data. A disciplined, staffed process then turns those solutions into commercial broadcast, radio-link and data-communications products that customers choose because they are the most reliable available. It measures itself by profit per engineer. It has stopped acquiring to grow. Its printing business has either been genuinely transformed by electronics, with proof, or has been run for cash and separated on good terms. Its leadership sits where its engine is. Over 10 to 15 years it compounds earnings ahead of the market and no longer depends on federal budget swings for its health, because commercial communications has become the larger and steadier half.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence (working hypothesis):**
> *Dunmore-Kessler takes electronics proven under the hardest government programs and turns them into the most reliable commercial systems for moving information, measured by profit per engineer.*

| **Passion** | Engineers solving hard communications problems; printing craftsmen's pride in machines; management's enthusiasm for diversification | **Engineers on hard, real-world communication problems** `[ASSUMPTION — no culture data in dossier]` | Passion for diversification itself is a decline signal, not a hedgehog circle. |

**What it cannot be best at:**
- Commodity printing machinery.
- Corrugated-paper machinery.
- Hot-metal typesetting `[ASSUMPTION: a mature or declining technology]`.
- Broad-line competition with the giant electrical conglomerates across every product they make.

**Tests of the hypothesis:**
- *Does it explain past wins?* Partly. Electronics grew to about half of sales, and the aerospace firm's growth came from exactly this kind of engineering. It cannot yet explain earnings wins, because there haven't been any for five years.
- *Does it rule things out?* Yes: corrugated machinery, hot metal, unrelated government work, and further acquisitions outside communications.
- *Is it uncomfortable to commit to?* Yes. It demotes the founding business and the headquarters city.

**Evidence that would confirm it within 12 to 24 months:**
1. Segment data show communications earning above the company's average return on capital.
2. Commercial revenue from transferred technology rises as a share of electronics sales.
3. Profit per engineer rises year over year.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop acquiring for 24 months.** No new deals until last year's two purchases are integrated and earning.
2. **Stop investing growth capital in mechanical printing equipment.** Fund maintenance only, plus the electronics-in-printing test.
3. **Stop making corrugated-paper machinery.** Prepare it for sale.
4. **Stop investing in hot-metal typesetting** `[ASSUMPTION: declining technology]`.
5. **Stop promoting managers for converting defense work into commercial products.** Promote them for commercial profit and profit per engineer.
6. **Stop bidding government work that has no communications or data carry-over.** Government work earns its place by feeding the flywheel.
7. **Stop paying in stock at premium multiples.** The aerospace deal valued the target at about twice Dunmore-Kessler's own multiple.
8. **Stop running the company from a headquarters far from its engine** `[ASSUMPTION: the distance slows decisions]`. Move key decision-making toward the electronics operations.
9. **Stop competing across the full product range of the giant electrical conglomerates.** Compete only where reliability is the deciding factor.
