# Good to Great: Continental Provision Company

## One-screen verdict

**Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):**
> *Be the #1 or #2 grocer in a deliberately small set of dense regional markets, running modern large stores where our own-made brands sit beside fully stocked national brands at the best value, and judge every store, market and dollar by profit per household served.*

**Weakest link in the flywheel:** **reinvestment capital.** Rebuilding the store base needs the dividend redirected into stores. The largest shareholder (a charitable foundation that held more than a third of the stock at IPO) and the takeover deadline both push the other way. If the capital step doesn't turn, the flywheel doesn't move.

**Top three stop-doing items:**
1. **Stop the chain-wide deep-discount price war.** Competitors matched the cuts. A high-cost operator cannot win a price race it started.
2. **Stop paying out most of the profit as dividends** while the store base goes obsolete.
3. **Stop being a national chain.** Exit or sell markets and stores where a top-two position isn't reachable. This includes many of the stores under 8,000 sq ft.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** the company is trying to buy volume with low prices through the wrong stores, on the highest cost base in the industry. The result is a loss of about $51M, a stock down 76% from its peak, and a $20 tender offer. The current strategy is losing money faster the harder it is pushed.

**Missing numbers that would change this answer:**
- Market share and trend by region.
- Profit by store size, age and market.
- Results of any large-format tests.
- Labor-contract terms, especially closure and severance costs and work rules.
- The foundation's current stake and how much it depends on dividends.
- Current sales and plant utilization.

If no core of markets turns out to be profitable, the Hedgehog Concept fails and the honest answer may be that a sale beats independence.

Greatness potential: 10/100

*Evidence note: this run had no research tools, so no market or competitor research was possible. Every fact comes from the dossier. Every inference is marked `[ASSUMPTION]`. Collins's method was retrospective, so treat everything below as disciplined hypotheses to test, not guarantees. The subject is a fallen leader, currently in Stage 4 of decline ("grasping for salvation"). Recovery from Stage 4 is possible but uncommon, and that is the main reason the score is low.*

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

The great version of Continental Provision is smaller, denser and modern. It has left every region where it cannot be first or second. In the regions it keeps, it runs large modern supermarkets with specialty departments, scanner checkouts, short lines and full shelves. Its century-old store brands, made in its own plants, sit next to fully stocked national brands, so customers choose them rather than being forced into them. It no longer measures itself by total sales or by being "the largest." It measures profit per household in each market it serves, and it reinvests that profit into the next modern store before paying dividends. Its leadership mixes long-tenured insiders who face facts with a few outside builders. Its board sees store-level economics every month. It would survive any single executive's departure.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence:** *Be the #1 or #2 grocer in a deliberately small set of dense regional markets, running modern large stores where our own-made brands sit beside fully stocked national brands at the best value, and judge every store, market and dollar by profit per household served.*

| Circle | Candidates considered | Kept | Ruled out (cannot be best in world at) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Best in world at** | National scale; lowest price chain-wide; store-brand quality and cost through owned manufacturing; dominance of selected home regions | **Store-brand value from owned manufacturing, delivered through dense, dominant regional positions** `[ASSUMPTION: some regions still support a top-two share]` | Lowest-cost operator nationally (the labor base is the most senior and costly); lowest price (competitors matched immediately); national coverage (the West Coast exit already shows this) |
| **Economic engine (denominator)** | Profit per store; profit per sq ft; total sales; **profit per household served in a market** | **Operating profit per household served in core markets.** Grocery economics are local: density lowers distribution, advertising and overhead cost per sale and raises plant utilization. | Total sales volume, which is the metric that produced the price war |
| **Passion** | Century of grocery heritage; pride in own products; long-tenured, loyal workforce `[ASSUMPTION]` | **Feeding ordinary families well, at honest value, with products we make ourselves** `[ASSUMPTION]` | Nostalgia for being "the biggest." Passion for the past is not passion for the cause. |

**Confirmation tests:**
- *Explains past wins?* Partly. The company's rise rested on vertical integration and value. Its fall coincides with spreading thin into small, aging, under-invested stores.
- *Rules things out?* Yes: national footprint, chain-wide discounting, small-store formats and the current dividend policy.
- *Uncomfortable?* Very. It means closing many stores, shrinking total sales, cutting the dividend and hiring from outside.

**Evidence that would confirm it:**
1. Store and market P&Ls showing that a coherent set of markets with top-two share earns at least the traditional ~1% net margin before price-war losses.
2. Large stores in those markets outperforming small ones on profit per household.
3. Large-format pilots reaching target economics within 12 months.

**Known tension:** the 1.5M-sq-ft plant needs volume, and shrinking the footprint cuts that volume. The plant could become the reason to keep bad stores open. Rule: **manufacturing serves the Hedgehog Concept, not the reverse.** If the retained footprint can't keep it efficiently used, rightsize the plant.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop the chain-wide deep-discount program.** Price competitively only where the cost structure supports it, in core markets.
2. **Stop distributing most of the profit as dividends.** The dividend becomes what is left after a funded store plan.
3. **Stop operating in markets with no realistic path to #1 or #2.** Close or sell those stores (pending the market-share data).
4. **Stop running sub-scale stores** (many of the stores under 8,000 sq ft) where a large-format replacement isn't planned.
5. **Stop cutting store labor to hit cost targets.** Cut overhead, distribution waste and unprofitable markets instead. Long lines and empty shelves destroy the traffic the strategy depends on.
6. **Stop forcing store brands at the expense of national-brand in-stock.** Own brands must win the customer's choice.
7. **Stop measuring success by total sales or by "being the largest."**
8. **Stop running the plant for volume.** Don't keep stores open to feed manufacturing.
9. **Stop filling every senior seat from inside.**
10. **Stop reacting to the takeover with quick big moves** such as a defensive acquisition or a deeper price cut.

**Non-negotiable disciplines (fanatical consistency):** monthly P&L for every store and market; in-stock and checkout-wait standards in every core store; every capital dollar judged by profit per household; no new market entry until existing core markets are #1 or #2.

**Framework boundaries (freedom within a framework):** headquarters sets which markets to serve, the capital rules, the store standards and the brand architecture. Regional leaders own their P&L, staffing, local assortment and pricing tactics within that frame.

**Bureaucracy check:** any central price or staffing mandates that exist because regional leaders aren't trusted should go once the right regional leaders are in seat `[ASSUMPTION that such mandates exist]`.
