# Corvane Systems, Inc.: Good to Great run

## Verdict

**Good, but only thinly, and not yet great.** Corvane is a one-year-old defense-electronics company assembled mostly by acquisition. It is profitable, with about $10M of backlog, but almost none of its track record is its own.

- **Hedgehog (working hypothesis):** *Corvane owns only businesses where digital and solid-state electronics give a decisive edge, and runs each one for return on capital, never for size.*
- **Weakest flywheel link:** unit earnings feeding capital allocation. Return on capital by unit isn't measured, so capital is deployed blind.
- **Top 3 stop-doing:** (1) paying for acquisitions with stock that isn't richly valued; (2) acquiring outside the digital-edge niche; (3) treating revenue, headcount or deal count as goals.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** it is buying businesses, partly with its own shares, without knowing which units earn their capital.
- **Missing numbers that would change this:** return on capital by unit, organic growth, customer concentration, and the true net income.
- **Confidence: low.** The verdict rests on one fiscal year and the dossier alone.

Greatness potential: 15/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Corvane is a federation of technical businesses. Each one wins a narrow niche because digital or solid-state electronics gives it a real edge. Each unit is run by a general manager who owns its profit and loss. Headquarters does one thing fanatically well: it moves capital to whatever earns the highest return. That means reinvesting in strong units, buying only at prices that clear a fixed hurdle, using stock only when the shares are richly valued, and starving or selling units that don't earn their keep. It grows per-share value, not size. Its defense work is a profitable base rather than a dependency, and the capital-allocation system is written down well enough to outlast the founders. It is great if it compounds per-share value well above peers for decades (superior performance), if no one else combines this technical judgment with this capital discipline (distinctive impact), and if the system survives the chairman (endurance).

**Denominator:** profit per dollar of capital employed, measured unit by unit.

**Why this candidate:**
- It explains the only evidence available: a profitable first year and a $10M backlog built on digital and solid-state work.
- It rules things out: deals outside the digital edge, dilutive stock deals, low-return contracts, and growth for its own sake.
- It is uncomfortable to commit to. It slows the founders' stated plan to build a major company by acquisition, and it forces them to measure units they may prefer to judge by instinct.

**Confirmation tests (12–24 months):**
1. Unit return-on-capital figures exist and show a clear spread between units, so allocation decisions become possible.
2. Next year's acquisitions clear a written return hurdle, and per-share earnings rise despite any shares issued.
3. At least one unit grows organically on its digital edge, with no new acquisition behind the growth.

If units turn out to have similar, low returns and the edge is only defense contract access, fall back to candidate (a). That would be a narrower defense and space systems hedgehog measured by profit per engineer.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop paying with stock unless the shares are richly valued** relative to what you get, and unless the deal adds to per-share earnings.
2. **Stop acquiring outside fields where digital or solid-state electronics gives a decisive edge.** No "wide range of industries" for its own sake.
3. **Stop treating revenue, headcount, backlog or deal count as goals.** Report per-share and per-unit returns first.
4. **Stop doing the next deal before the last one reports monthly unit returns.**
5. **Stop bidding low-return contracts to fill capacity or inflate backlog.**
6. **Stop competing head-on in commodity semiconductors.** Point the devices division at Corvane's own systems and defensible niches.
7. **Stop routing operating decisions through the chairman.** Centralize capital decisions; decentralize operating ones.
8. **Stop funding research programs with no path to a business that earns back its capital.** This includes reviewing the experimental learning-computer program as an option to keep, not a commitment to expand.

**Non-negotiable disciplines:**
- Monthly unit profit and return on capital.
- One written acquisition hurdle, applied to every deal.
- A post-deal review at 12 months.

**Framework boundaries:**
- **Units have freedom over:** operations, hiring, pricing and bids within the return target.
- **Headquarters owns:** capital, the hurdle, and the decision on when to use stock.
- **Bureaucracy check:** add no head-office approval layers. If a unit needs them, its manager is in the wrong seat.
