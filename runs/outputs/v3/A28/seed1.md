## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Corvane is not the biggest electronics company. It is the best compounder of per-share value in technology. It is a federation of small, autonomous businesses, and each one is a leader in a narrow corner of digital and solid-state systems: rugged digital computers, fire-control and navigation processors, satellite data processing, and specialty devices. Each unit is run by a strong general manager who owns its profit and loss. Headquarters does three things: it allocates capital against a published hurdle, it holds every unit to its return on capital, and it keeps a shared solid-state technical edge. Acquisitions happen only when they fit the technology scope and raise per-share earning power. They are paid in stock only when the math says the shares are worth more than what they buy. Defense remains a core customer but no longer the only one, because the same technology is sold into industrial and commercial uses. The company has come through at least one defense-budget downturn with its returns intact. Its capital-allocation discipline is written down and institutional, so it would survive the founders.

**Denominator:** return on capital employed **per business unit**, rolled up to **per-share earning power**. Total sales is not the denominator. Neither is headcount.

**Tests:**
- *Explains past wins?* Only partly. Year 0 was profitable, but there is no unit data to show where the profit came from. `[ASSUMPTION]` The $5M fire-direction computer contract and the navigation and satellite programs reflect real niche technical strength.
- *Rules things out?* Yes. It excludes acquisitions in unrelated industries, size for its own sake, a commodity semiconductor business, and stock-funded deals that dilute.
- *Uncomfortable?* Yes. It caps the founders' stated ambition to build a major company mainly by buying, and puts that ambition under a per-share hurdle.

**What would confirm it within 12–24 months:**
1. Unit P&Ls show that most acquired units earn above the hurdle. If they don't, the Hedgehog collapses to the narrower operating version: "best-in-world solid-state digital systems for government niches."
2. Earnings per share grow faster than the share count.
3. At least one non-defense customer buys an existing Corvane technology without heavy custom work.

**Honest tension:** Collins's research found that acquisitions *accelerate* a flywheel that already turns and almost never *start* one. Corvane is trying to start its flywheel with acquisitions. This Hedgehog bets that strict per-share discipline can make that work. Treat it as a hypothesis, not a finding.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop acquiring outside digital and solid-state technology.** The "much wider range of industries" option is the most attractive way to wreck the Hedgehog.
2. **Stop paying with stock without a per-share test.** Treat stock as owners' capital, not as free currency. Use it only when the deal raises per-share earning power.
3. **Stop buying revenue for size.** Drop total sales and headcount as success measures in reports and in the founders' language.
4. **Stop new acquisitions** until every existing unit has reported return on capital for two quarters `[ASSUMPTION: two quarters is the proposed minimum]`.
5. **Stop chasing volume semiconductors** against established makers. Devices serve Corvane's own systems and specialty niches.
6. **Stop bidding as a prime contractor** on large systems against big aerospace firms and Rival L. Win the specialized subsystems instead.
7. **Stop running orphan research.** The adaptive computer and the study contracts continue only if they are externally funded or feed a unit's product line.
8. **Stop centralizing operating decisions at headquarters.** Headquarters allocates capital and measures. Units operate.

**Non-negotiable disciplines (fanatical consistency):**
- Every unit reports return on capital monthly, in the same format.
- Every deal is checked against the written hurdle and the per-share test before it closes.
- Every deal gets a blameless post-mortem at 12 months.

**Framework boundaries:**
- *Unit GMs have freedom over* products, pricing, hiring and operations.
- *Headquarters holds* capital, scope, the hurdle and the measurement system.

**Bureaucracy check:** headquarters controls should stay minimal. If a rule exists only because a GM can't be trusted, change the GM, not the rulebook.
