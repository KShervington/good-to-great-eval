# Good to Great: Brennard Steel Corporation

## Verdict

**Already great, so this is stewardship mode.** Brennard has 30 years of compounding behind it. Return on equity has been about 22% for two years running. It was the first company to make sheet in a minimill using thin-slab casting, and it has almost no debt. The open question is whether it stays great now that the founder-chairman no longer runs it.

- **Hedgehog:** Make steel at a lower cost per ton than anyone, by putting the newest proven minimill technology in the hands of lean, team-bonused, no-layoff plant crews.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** the *next* technology lead. Rivals are copying thin-slab. The successor bet, iron carbide, is late, over budget and still unproven.
- **Temptations to refuse:**
  1. Scaling iron-making before the first plant proves its cost and uptime.
  2. Building tons to protect the growth rate instead of profit per ton.
  3. Buying old mills or diversifying to keep growing.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** about 40% new industry capacity is coming in roughly three years, and sheet prices are already falling.
- **Missing numbers:** cost and profit per ton by mill compared with rivals, and the iron-carbide plant's actual cost and output.
- **Confidence:** moderate. It rests on four years of financials and 30 years of policy, with no per-ton data.

Greatness potential: 70/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

The great Brennard does the same few things that made it great, and it doesn't let a new CEO, a capacity glut or an exciting technology talk it out of them. It stays the lowest-cost steelmaker per ton in every market it enters. It only builds new plants, and only with technology rivals haven't adopted yet. It keeps a headquarters of about 25 people, four layers of management, weekly team bonuses and no layoffs. It is willing to grow more slowly than its history while rivals flood the market, because profit per ton matters more than tons. Its next technology lead is chosen and proven before it is scaled. The test of greatness this decade is endurance: whether the culture, and the willingness to stop doing things, survive the hand-off from the man who built them.

**Tests:**
- *Does it explain past wins?* Yes. It explains the first mill (low cost, captive joist demand), the thin-slab bet the integrated producers refused, and an ROE that climbed from 9.5% to 22%.
- *Does it rule things out?* Yes. It rules out acquisitions of old mills, diversification, overseas expansion, and growth for its own sake.
- *Is it uncomfortable to commit to?* Yes. It means accepting slower growth than history while rivals add 40% capacity.

**Working hypothesis. These results would confirm it:**
- Cost per ton at each mill sits in the lowest quartile among comparable minimills.
- Profit per ton stays above rivals' through the coming price decline.
- Fabrication units earn their per-ton keep on transfer-priced steel.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   ┌──► 1. Build a new plant with the newest proven technology,
   │       lean design, own cash
   │                     │
   │                     ▼
   │    2. Self-managing crews on weekly output bonus
   │       → highest tons per worker
   │                     │
   │                     ▼
   │    3. Lowest cost per ton  ◄── (input: scrap / scrap substitutes)
   │                     │
   │                     ▼
   │    4. Price to fill the mill and still earn
   │       the highest PROFIT PER TON
   │                     │
   │                     ▼
   │    5. Retained earnings, near-zero debt
   │       → fund the next greenfield
   │                     │
   │                     ▼
   │    6. Reputation (pay, no layoffs, tech-first)
   │       → best workers, best sites, willingness to take the next tech bet
   └─────────────────────┘
```

**The loop closes.** Component 6 feeds component 1.

**Weakest link:** the arrows from 1 to 3. The cost lead came from being first. With about ten copycat minimills on the way, being first with thin-slab is fading as an advantage. The input arrow into component 3 (scrap at about $155 per gross ton and rising) is under strain too. The only candidate for the next lead, iron carbide, is unproven.

**Current push (next one or two turns):**
1. **Start up the third sheet mill on time and on budget** (~$300M), with its per-ton cost demonstrated, and decide based on that cost, not on the tons it adds.
2. **Prove or kill iron carbide on explicit criteria** before committing any further iron-making capital.

**Doom-loop check:** no doom loop yet. Direction has been consistent for 30 years, and the company has made no acquisitions and no lurches. Watch for three early signals:
- Capex is nearly doubling to more than $500M (about 1.8× last year's earnings).
- Management describes iron-carbide failures as "expected."
- The question "how to keep its historic growth rate" frames growth in tons, not per-ton profit.

## 5. Stop-doing list (stewardship mode: real temptations only)

1. **Don't scale iron-making** (no second plant, no capacity increase) until the Caribbean plant meets its uptime and cost criteria.
2. **Don't build capacity to protect the historic growth rate.** Approve a mill only if its projected cost per ton is in the lowest quartile *at trough prices*.
3. **Don't buy older integrated mills or distressed capacity**, even when a glut makes them cheap.
4. **Don't diversify outside steel or go overseas** to replace growth that copycats take away.
5. **Don't add layers or a headquarters staff** during the CEO transition. Each new corporate role must be justified against the four-layer, 25-person standard.
6. **Don't touch weekly bonuses or the no-layoff policy** to protect a bad year's earnings. Cut hours, as before.
7. **Don't keep fabrication lines on loyalty.** If a line can't earn profit per ton, it goes on the review list.

## 7. Core ideology and the BHAG

**Core values** `[ASSUMPTION: inferred from practice; no formal statement exists]`:
1. **No class lines.** A lean headquarters, few layers, and the same rules for everyone.
2. **Pay for what the team produces.** Weekly output bonuses mean workers share in the results.
3. **Security through shared sacrifice.** Hours are cut instead of jobs.
4. **Decide at the plant.** The people closest to the work run it.
5. **Build new, build better, and send everyone home safe.** Safety is added explicitly, because the bonus system needs a counterweight.

**Core purpose** `[ASSUMPTION]`: to prove that steel can be made at home better and cheaper than anywhere, so that ordinary teams earn extraordinary pay.

**BHAG (25 years):** *Make the minimill the way most of the country's steel is made, and make Brennard's mills the lowest-cost, highest profit-per-ton steel plants in the world.*

**Clock-building mechanisms:**
- **Written rules:** the build-new policy, the stay-in-steel policy and the per-ton capital-approval rule, written down so they don't live only in the chairman's head.
- **Bonus formula:** with a safety gate built in.
- **Pipeline of future CEOs:** drawn from the plant general managers.
- **Independent directors:** they hold the CEO to the Hedgehog Concept.
- **Technology scouting process:** one person, a standing list of next-generation candidates and a pilot budget.
