## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Ashcombe is a shaving-technology company, not a diversified consumer-products company. It earns its returns by launching a demonstrably better shaving system every few years. Shavers move up to it and keep buying its refills for decades, and it runs the world's best high-precision blade manufacturing to make those refills at scale. It does not compete with Rival B on price anywhere. It keeps a disposable only as a flanker, so that it is never caught without one, but it never treats the disposable as its future. Cash from shaving goes back into shaving: R&D, manufacturing and global marketing. It does not fund the next diversification. The company is judged on one thing, profit per shaver, and that number rises year after year whoever the CEO is.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence:** *Ashcombe will be the world's best at the technology of a better shave, sold as premium repeat-purchase refills, earning more profit per shaver every year.*

| Circle | Candidates considered | Chosen | Explicitly NOT best at |
|---|---|---|---|

(c) Shaving technology and precision blade manufacturing. | **(c).** It is the 80-year core, has held 60–65% U.S. blade share for about 20 years, and has produced two twin-blade systems in about six years. | Low-price commodity plastics (lighters, cheap pens: Rival B's game). Electronics (watches, calculators: failed). Luxury fragrance (failed). |
| **Economic engine** | Profit per blade, per unit of sales, per razor, or **per shaver** | **Profit per shaver per year** | — |
| **Passion** | Consumer marketing broadly, or shaving | **Shaving.** `[ASSUMPTION]`: inferred from 80 years of focus and steady system innovation, with no direct evidence about the culture. | — |

**Why profit per shaver?**
- It makes the disposable dilemma visible. A shaver who moves from refills to disposables lowers it, and a shaver who trades up to a new system raises it.
- Unit share and total razor share hide that difference. That is why "defend total razor share" is the wrong goal.

**The concept answers the central strategic question.**
- Put resources into new premium systems.
- Keep the disposable as a priced, capped defensive flanker.
- "Do both equally" is not discipline. It means funding two opposite profit models at once.

**Candidate (b), personal-care consumables, was rejected.** It would explain the deodorant win, but it reopens the door to the diversification era (hair coloring, shampoos, perfumes) that shrank margins. Toiletries stay in a "prove it" seat (see the Bus section) rather than inside the concept.

**Confirmation tests (working hypothesis):**
1. Line-level data shows shaving systems earning well above the company average return. Disposables and lighters should earn below it.
2. The improved twin-blade system launched three years ago shows trade-up: its refill volume and price realization are higher than the first system's.
3. After disposable promotion is capped, profit per shaver holds or rises even if unit share dips.
4. **The test that would break the concept:** disposables becoming the majority of U.S. shaving units *and* premium refill volume falling for two consecutive years. If that happens, re-run Gate 5.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop the disposable-lighter price war.** Sell or close the lighter business within the year.
2. **Stop competing with Rival B on price** in any category: lighters, pens or razors.
3. **Stop promoting the company's own disposable for volume.** No growth targets and no promotional spend beyond what is needed to keep a presence in the category.
4. **Stop entering new categories.** No new categories or acquisitions unless they pass the Hedgehog test: shaving technology plus premium repeat purchase.
5. **Stop measuring shaving success by total razor unit share.** Report profit per shaver first.
6. **Stop investing for growth in pens.** Maintain them, harvest them, and sell if the price is fair.
7. **Stop treating U.S. electric-shaver entry as a strategic goal.** Let the antitrust suit run its course without making it a priority.
8. **Stop launching toiletry line extensions that have no technology edge.** Pump and roll-on reformulations for the aerosol problem are parity moves: do them, but don't chase.
