# Ashby & Crane, Inc.: The Good-to-Great Run

## Verdict

**Good, close to great. Do not buy the soft-drink company.**

- **Hedgehog Concept (working):** Take an under-positioned brand in a large adult-consumer category where image, more than distribution muscle, decides the winner. Make it the category leader with one durable image. Then scale it worldwide. *Denominator: profit per leading brand.*
- **Weakest flywheel link:** choosing where to apply the skill. Beer is one clear win. Razors and gum produced little.
- **Top 3 stop-doing:** (1) Buying the soft-drink company to escape tobacco. (2) Adding beer capacity ahead of profit evidence. (3) Buying small brands that have no path to #1 or #2.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** this month the government named the core product the nation's leading public-health enemy. "Liability contained so far" is not a plan.
- **Missing numbers that would change this:** profit by business and by brand, shareholder returns against the market, and the soft-drink target's financials and terms.
- **Confidence:** moderate. It rests on an 18-year strategic pattern, not on financials.

Greatness potential: 60/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

In its great version, Ashby & Crane is a disciplined brand-building company. It is not a diversified conglomerate. It owns a small number of very large brands, each #1 or #2 in its category, and it repeats one move. It finds a brand the market has underrated, or a segment no one has created yet, the way the low-tar and reduced-calorie launches did. It gives that brand one durable image and holds that image consistently for decades. Then it takes the brand worldwide. It turns down deals that look like "more" but can't become category-leading on the company's own terms, and the soft-drink company is the first of these. It treats the health case against smoking as a fact to plan around, not a story to manage. Its next generation of leaders runs the system without the current chairman.

**Sentence:** *Ashby & Crane takes an under-positioned brand in a large adult-consumer category where image decides the winner, makes it the category leader with one durable image, and scales it worldwide, measured by profit per leading brand.*

**Confirmation tests (working hypothesis):**
- **Does it explain past wins?** Yes. The flagship, beer, low-tar, and reduced-calorie all fit.
- **Does it explain the losses?** Yes. Razors and gum had no image-led path to leadership.
- **Does it rule things out?** Yes. It rules out the soft-drink company, where the target is already positioned and bottlers decide the shelf.
- **Is it uncomfortable to commit to?** Yes. It means continued dependence on tobacco in the near term, and diversifying only slowly.
- **What would confirm it:** per-brand profit data showing that leading brands carry the profits and the non-leaders don't.

```
 │      capacity, international rollout of the same image
 │                     │
 │                     ▼
 └── 6. Global leaders fund the next disciplined repositioning
```

**Weakest link: the arrow from 6 back to 1, which is choosing the next category.** The company can run steps 2 through 5 well. Its record at step 1 outside tobacco is one clear win (beer) and several weak results (razors, shaving, gum). A second weak point is step 5 in cigarettes: broadcast advertising is already banned, and further limits are likely `[ASSUMPTION]`.

**Current push (next 1–2 turns):**
1. **International cigarettes.** Take the flagship image to the markets growing faster than the U.S. Do it within the law and the health-disclosure norms of each market, and assume those norms will tighten.
2. **Beer.** Build profit per leading brand in the flagship beer and the reduced-calorie beer before buying more scale. Approve capacity in stages, gated on utilization and margin.

**Doom-loop check:** One warning sign is active. Buying a roughly $500M company to "diversify beyond tobacco" is a large acquisition meant to jump-start momentum, driven by fear about the core business rather than by an understanding of the new one. The beer and cigarette flywheels themselves show consistency, not doom-loop behavior.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop pursuing the soft-drink acquisition.** The target is already positioned as the cola alternative, so there is little repositioning upside. Two giants control the bottlers. The price is several times what the brewer cost. The motive is escape, not fit.
2. **Stop approving beer capacity ahead of evidence.** Expand in tranches tied to utilization and profit per barrel `[ASSUMPTION: beer margins are thin while ad spend is heavy]`.
3. **Stop making small acquisitions with no path to #1 or #2.** The razors and gum record already proves the point.
4. **Stop framing diversification as "getting out of tobacco."** Frame it as "finding the next category where the hedgehog wins." The first framing invites grasping.
5. **Stop treating health and liability as a communications problem.** Do not dispute the science. Plan the business on the assumption that the health case is correct and that regulation tightens.
6. **Stop spreading support across marginal brands.** Concentrate it on the brands that lead or can lead.

## 7. Core ideology and the BHAG

- **Core values (draft):**
  - Win by building brands, not by buying size.
  - Fanatical consistency of image.
  - Candor about our products and their risks.
  - Underdog drive against bigger incumbents.
  - Responsibility to adult consumers only `[ASSUMPTION: adult-only marketing is a non-negotiable]`.
- **Core purpose (draft, CONDITIONAL):** *To build brands that adult consumers choose over the incumbents.* A purpose that rests on cigarettes alone will not survive the health facts. It has to be honest about the product, and over time the company needs to earn a purpose beyond tobacco.
- **BHAG (20 years):** *Hold the #1 or #2 brand worldwide in every category we compete in.*
- **Clock-building mechanisms:**
  - A brand-repositioning playbook, documented and taught.
  - Per-brand profit reporting.
  - A capital-allocation gate that tests every deal against the hedgehog.
  - A formal succession process.
  - A standing brutal-facts review on health and regulation.
