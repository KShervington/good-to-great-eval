# Good to Great: Tillery Products Company

**Verdict: Good, not yet great. The core is strong; the new goal is dangerous.**

- **Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** Tillery makes the best-designed, longest-lasting everyday rubber and plastic products for home and commercial kitchens. It sells them through channels it already controls and judges itself by **profit per pound of resin**.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** pricing power. Design has to turn into profit per pound now that retail-price agreements are banned and resin prices swing.
- **Top three stop-doing items:**
  1. Party-plan selling against Rival T.
  2. Launching lines the company cannot distribute.
  3. Steering by a sales-quadrupling target.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** the evidence does not show which divisions actually earn their keep.
- **Numbers that would change this:** profit and resin volume by division, recent return on equity (ROE), housewares share by category, and who the main competitors are.
- **Confidence:** moderate-low. The verdict rests on 20 years of compounding and the brand-plus-distribution position, not on current profit data.

Greatness potential: 35/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Tillery is a narrower company than today's Tillery. It is the undisputed leader in molded rubber and plastic storage, organization and cleaning products for home kitchens and for restaurant, hotel and institutional kitchens. It wins shelf space because its products are visibly better designed and last longer, not because of price agreements. It owns direct distribution to supermarkets and commercial buyers and launches nothing it cannot push through those channels. Profit per pound of resin rises faster than resin costs, so commodity swings and recessions dent earnings without breaking the compounding. The current chairman built a bench strong enough that the next leader extends the run. It does not depend on him.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence:** *We make the best-designed, longest-lasting everyday rubber and plastic products for home and commercial kitchens, sell them through channels we control, and measure ourselves by profit per pound of resin.*

| **Economic engine** | Profit per item; per shelf foot; per customer; **per pound of resin** | **Profit per pound of resin.** Resin is the main cost and the main risk, and this denominator exposes whether brand and design beat the commodity. | **Passion** | Hometown heritage; 60-year brand; "growth" | Pride in a well-made, everyday product with the hometown name on it `[ASSUMPTION: no direct evidence of employee sentiment]` |

**Confirmation tests (the hypothesis holds if):**
1. Segment data shows housewares and commercial earn the highest profit per pound, and party plan, automotive and international earn the lowest.
2. It explains past wins (rubber kitchen products, the brand, commercial products growing to about 25% of sales) and past losses (recreational products, the slow party plan).
3. Committing to it is uncomfortable: it means exiting at least one existing division.

## 3. The flywheel

```
 ┌─► 1. Narrow, superior product design (kitchen/home + commercial)
 │            │
 │            ▼
 │   2. Retail and institutional sell-through, earned price premium
 │            │
 │            ▼
 │   3. PROFIT PER POUND OF RESIN rises  ◄── weakest link
 │            │
 │            ▼
 │   4. Reinvest: molding automation, design, resin buying scale
 │            │
 │            ▼
 │   5. Lower unit cost + steady stream of in-hedgehog new products
 │            │
 │            ▼
 └── 6. More shelf space and brand trust from supermarkets and commercial buyers
```

**Current push (next two turns):**
1. **Measure link 3.** Build profit per pound by product line and division. Without it, the flywheel has no feedback.
2. **Protect link 3 before the recession.** Set up resin purchasing contracts and a disciplined price-adjustment policy with retailers. Margins have to be earned through value now that the minimum-price agreements are gone.

**Doom-loop check (warning signs already present):**
- **Copying a leader instead of understanding the market:** the party plan was modeled on Rival T.
- **Diversifying without the right channel:** the recreational line was launched with no distribution for it and then abandoned.
- **A new leader framing the inherited company as "stodgy," plus a big public number:** this invites new direction after new direction.
- **The open acquisition question:** acquisitions used to jump-start growth are the classic doom-loop move.

Rule: **no acquisitions until the flywheel is visibly turning.** After that, acquire only to accelerate a proven flywheel within the Hedgehog Concept.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop competing in party-plan selling** against Rival T. Divest or wind down, subject to the profit data.
2. **Stop launching or buying lines that need distribution Tillery doesn't own** (the recreational-products lesson).
3. **Stop steering by sales volume.** Restate the quadrupling goal in terms of profit per pound and ROE. Sales are an outcome, not the target.
4. **Stop acquisitions outside the Hedgehog Concept,** however attractive, and make none until the flywheel turns.
5. **Stop adding SKUs by default.** Prune the roughly 240-item line to items that clear a profit-per-pound threshold.
6. **Stop treating automotive and international as permanent.** They need to earn their place every year.
7. **Stop absorbing resin swings passively.** No more surprises like the Year −6 profit drop without a pre-agreed response.

## 7. Core ideology and the BHAG

**Core values** `[ASSUMPTION: inferred, to be validated with employees]`:
1. Products built to last.
2. Prices earned through value, never fixed.
3. Respect for the people and the town that built the company.
4. Steady compounding over flashy growth.

**Core purpose:** To make everyday household and commercial kitchen work easier and more orderly.

**BHAG (20 years):** A Tillery product in nearly every American home and commercial kitchen, earning the highest profit per pound of resin in the industry.

**Clock-building mechanisms:**
- Divisional profit-per-pound reporting.
- A repeatable new-product design process for core categories.
- A resin hedging and price-adjustment policy.
- An annual stop-doing review by the board.
- A formal succession bench, reviewed by the board every year.
