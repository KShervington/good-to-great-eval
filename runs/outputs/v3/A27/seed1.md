# Good to Great: Tillery Products Company

## Verdict

**Tillery is good but not yet great. It becomes great by narrowing, not by growing to a number.**

- **Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** Tillery makes the best-made, most useful everyday rubber and plastic products for kitchens and households, at home and in commercial kitchens, and earns a branded premium on every pound of resin it converts.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** turning brand strength into a protected margin over resin cost. This link broke in Year −6.
- **Top three stop-doing items:** (1) the party-plan line; (2) treating automotive mats as a growth platform; (3) chasing the quadruple-sales goal through acquisitions or new categories outside the served markets.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** the new outsider CEO has set a public *sales-size* goal heading into a recession. That combination invites undisciplined pursuit of more.
- **Missing numbers that would change this:** profit and ROE by division, share in core categories, resin-spread history, and management bench depth.
- **Confidence:** low to moderate. It rests on a 20-year earnings record and qualitative facts, with no segment profit data.

Greatness potential: 25/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Tillery is a narrow, deep company. It owns the everyday durable kitchen and household storage and cleaning categories on retail shelves, and it supplies the same tough, well-designed products to restaurants, hotels and institutions. It is not a portfolio of housewares, auto mats, party plans and recreational products. Each year it designs a small number of products that are clearly better than those of its competitors. It prunes weaker items so that every shelf foot and every pound of resin earns more. It manages resin cost as a core discipline rather than as weather. Retailers want more Tillery on the shelf because Tillery items earn them the most. Its home-town workforce is a partner in quality, not an adversary. It compounds earnings and return on equity ahead of its peers through recessions and resin spikes. It does not depend on one leader, so it would stay great after the current chairman leaves.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   ┌─► 1. Design a few clearly better everyday products
   │      in core kitchen/household categories
   │                     │
   │                     ▼
   │   2. Brand trust + quality → shoppers choose Tillery
   │      at the shelf; commercial buyers specify it
   │                     │
   │                     ▼
   │   3. High sales and profit per shelf foot → retailers
   │      give more space; direct distribution gets denser
   │                     │
   │                     ▼
   │   4. Higher GROSS PROFIT PER POUND OF RESIN
   │      (brand premium + scale in resin buying, molding)   ◄── weakest link
   │                     │
   │                     ▼
   │   5. Prune weak SKUs; reinvest in design, molds,
   │      people and the commercial line
   └─────────────────────┘
```

The loop closes: step 5's reinvestment funds step 1's better products.

**Weakest link: step 4.** Brand strength has not reliably turned into a protected spread. Resin prices and price controls broke it in Year −6 and earlier in this decade. The cease-and-desist order means Tillery cannot steer retail prices, so its spread has to come from cost discipline and from the value it gives retailers, not from controlling price.

**Current push (the next one or two turns):**
1. **Measure and protect the spread.** Build spread-per-pound by segment and by SKU, put a resin-purchasing and price-adjustment discipline in place before the recession, and rank all of roughly 240 housewares items by spread and shelf productivity.
2. **Prune to fund design.** Cut the weakest items and exit the businesses outside the Hedgehog Concept. Redirect the freed capital and people into steady, disciplined new-product design in the core categories, not a burst of launches.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop the party-plan line.** Sell it. Tillery will not beat Rival T at its own model.
2. **Stop investing in automotive accessories for growth.** Harvest it, then sell or convert the southern plant.
3. **Stop entering categories where Tillery lacks distribution or best-in-world potential.** The recreational-products failure is the standing lesson.
4. **Stop acquisitions outside the served markets.** Acquisitions should be small tuck-ins inside the Hedgehog Concept, made only after the flywheel is turning. They are an accelerator, not an engine.
5. **Stop managing to a sales-size target.** Replace "quadruple sales" with earnings, ROE and spread-per-pound goals.
6. **Stop adding SKUs without removing weaker ones.** Use a one-in, one-out rule judged on spread and shelf productivity.
7. **Stop treating resin cost swings as uncontrollable.** Put in place contracting, multiple sources and disciplined price adjustments `[ASSUMPTION: no current hedging or pricing mechanism]`.
8. **Never return to minimum-retail-price arrangements.** Earn shelf space through retailer economics, not price control.

**Truth climate and charisma check:** this is the central risk. A strong, celebrated outsider with a public goal can become the "genius with a thousand helpers," with bad news filtered before it reaches him. Countermeasures:
- Run a management committee that debates real decisions.
- Hold a standing "brutal facts" agenda item each quarter.
- Hold blameless reviews of every failed product or acquisition.
- Track spread per pound and segment ROE as red flags that go to the board automatically.

**Faith statement:** Tillery has a trusted national brand, direct shelf access and 20 years of compounding. If it narrows to what it does best and masters its resin spread, it will outlast the recession and outgrow its peers.

## 7. Core ideology and BHAG

**Core values** `[ASSUMPTION: inferred, to be validated with employees and the board]`:
1. Products that are genuinely useful and built to last.
2. Fair dealing with retailers, customers and workers, with no shortcuts on pricing or the law.
3. Respect for the people who make the products, starting in the home town.
4. Thrift and discipline: every pound and every shelf foot has to earn its place.

**Core purpose:** to make everyday household and kitchen work easier with products people trust for years.

**BHAG (10 to 30 years):** *Become the brand that households and commercial kitchens reach for first in everyday storage and cleaning, and the one retailers can't run the category without.* This replaces "quadruple sales in ten years." A financial ambition can stay, but it should be stated as earnings and ROE compounding at or above the historical rate, not as a target for size.

**Clock-building mechanisms:**
- An internal succession pipeline, with two or more candidates for president or COO developed inside within 3 to 5 years.
- Segment and SKU reporting on spread per pound, reviewed monthly.
- A one-in, one-out SKU rule.
- A resin committee with set contracting and pricing playbooks.
- A joint labor-management council at the home-town plant.
- An acquisition charter: inside the Hedgehog Concept only, sized as tuck-ins, and approved only after a red-team review.
