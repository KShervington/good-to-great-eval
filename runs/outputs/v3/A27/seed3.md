# Tillery Products Company: Good to Great

## Verdict: good, not yet great. The great version is narrower, not bigger.

**Hedgehog (working hypothesis):** Tillery makes the best everyday rubber and plastic products for kitchens, homes and workplaces, sold under its own brand through channels it controls, and judged by **profit per pound of resin** `[ASSUMPTION: denominator]`.

**Weakest flywheel link:** turning the brand into pricing power when resin costs rise. Year −6 showed this link breaking, and minimum-price agreements can no longer prop it up.

**Stop doing:**
1. Party-plan selling against Rival T.
2. Entering categories without distribution the company owns.
3. Chasing "quadruple" through acquisitions outside the hedgehog.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** nothing in the evidence shows which division actually makes money.

**Missing numbers that would change this:** profit by division, ROE, share by category, depth of the management bench.

**Confidence:** moderate to low. It rests on 20 years of earnings compounding and the company's channel history, not on current profitability.

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Tillery is a focused maker of branded everyday rubber and plastic goods. Families reach for these products in the supermarket aisle, and restaurants, hotels and plants buy them for the back of the house. It earns more profit on each pound of resin than anyone in its categories. It does that through useful design, a trusted name, and direct-to-store distribution that competitors can't easily copy. It doesn't sell door to door, doesn't make boats, and doesn't buy companies to hit a size target. It compounds through recessions and resin spikes because it prices on product value rather than on agreements. It prunes weak items as steadily as it launches new ones. Its results don't depend on the chief executive who started the change, because a bench of insiders is ready to succeed him.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   ▼                                                          │
1. Useful, well-designed everyday products                    │
   (home + commercial, same materials & tooling)              │
   │                                                          │
   ▼                                                          │
2. Shoppers choose the brand → retailers grant more shelf     │
   │                                                          │
   ▼                                                          │
3. Volume through Tillery's own direct-to-store distribution  │
   │                                                          │
   ▼                                                          │
4. Scale in molding and resin buying lowers cost per part     │
   │                                                          │
   ▼                                                          │
5. Higher PROFIT PER POUND OF RESIN  ◄── weakest link         │
   │   (brand-based pricing must offset resin swings)         │
   ▼                                                          │
6. Reinvest in design, tooling and new products ──────────────┘
```

**Current push (the next two turns):**
1. **See the engine.** Build profit per pound by division and by product family, so that pushes go to the parts of the loop that actually spin.
2. **Strengthen link 5 before the recession arrives.** Set a resin pass-through and brand-pricing discipline that doesn't rely on retail-price agreements. Then prune the items that can't hold margin.

**Doom-loop check:**
- **Past signs:** a recreational line launched without understanding the channel, a copy of the party-plan leader, and a spread into automotive and international.
- **Forward signs:** an outsider CEO, a public goal of 4× sales, and a diagnosis of "stodgy and complacent." This combination tends to produce big programs, reorganizations and acquisitions meant to jump-start momentum.
- **Result:** the loop closes (PASS). The doom-loop risk is logged in the Risk Register.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Party-plan direct sales.** Exit them. Tillery can't be best in a channel owned by an entrenched specialist, and the line may also compete with its own supermarket shelf `[ASSUMPTION]`.
2. **New categories without distribution Tillery owns.** The boats and sleds are the lesson.
3. **Acquisitions made to hit a size target.** Allow only tuck-ins that share the same material, channel and brand.
4. **Defending margin through retailer price arrangements.** Win on product and brand. The cease-and-desist order closed the other route anyway.
5. **Adding items without retiring weak ones.** Run an annual prune, and never treat the count of new items as a goal.
6. **Measuring success publicly by sales quadrupling.** Reframe the goal around earnings and profit per pound.
7. **Putting new capital into automotive and international** until each passes the hedgehog test.

**Non-negotiable disciplines:**
- Every new item clears a profit-per-pound hurdle and a shelf-velocity hurdle.
- Bottom-tier items are pruned every year.
- Resin cost and pricing are reviewed every quarter.
- Every acquisition proposal must show the same channel, material and brand.

**Framework boundaries (freedom and responsibility):**
- **Fixed:** what Tillery makes and which channels it sells through.
- **Free:** product teams own design, pricing within the hurdles, and their own launch calendars.

**Bureaucracy check:** don't import conglomerate-style control systems from the CEO's previous employer as a substitute for getting the right people in the right seats.
