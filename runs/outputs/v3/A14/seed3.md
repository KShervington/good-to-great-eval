# Good to Great: Ironbridge Steel Corporation

## Verdict

**Ironbridge is good and sliding. It is not great, and it is not on a path to great today.**

- **Hedgehog (working hypothesis):** Ironbridge makes the demanding flat-rolled and plate steel that minimills can't, from a few world-class plants near its customers, at a delivered cost no importer can beat.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** cost per ton at the core plants. It is unproven and probably behind foreign mills `[ASSUMPTION]`.
- **Top three to stop:** (1) raising prices instead of cutting cost; (2) spreading capital across old plants; (3) defending bars against minimills.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** last year's record profit came from the end of price controls and customer stockpiling, not from performance. Shipments may fall about 25% this year.
- **Missing numbers that would change this:** cost per ton against foreign mills and minimills, three-year return on capital, and pension obligations.
- **Confidence:** low to moderate. It rests on qualitative facts in the dossier and none of the core cost numbers.

Greatness potential: 10/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

The great Ironbridge is **smaller, cheaper and harder to replace**. It no longer competes on being the biggest. It runs a few world-class plants, anchored by the Lake Michigan works and the tidewater plant `[ASSUMPTION: these are the best assets]`. Those plants make the steels that minimills can't yet make and that importers can't deliver as reliably: auto and appliance sheet, heavy plate, and high-spec structural steel. They sit close enough to customers that the delivered cost and the service win the contract. Executives are paid on cash profit per ton across the whole cycle, not on volume or price. Pay is modest when results are modest. Workers share in productivity gains instead of receiving guaranteed raises that ignore the market. The company invests in a plant only when it can prove that plant will be cost-competitive with the best mills in the world. It closes or sells capacity that can't meet that test, does so early, and supports the workers affected. It earns its return in down years as well as booms.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence (working hypothesis):** *Ironbridge makes the demanding flat-rolled and plate steel that minimills can't, from a few world-class plants near its customers, at a delivered cost no importer can beat.*

| Circle | Candidates considered | Kept | Ruled out, and why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Best in the world at** | Lowest-cost commodity steel; bars and simple shapes; shipbuilding; vertical integration; high-spec flat-rolled and plate for nearby industrial customers | **High-spec flat-rolled and plate, delivered reliably to Great Lakes and East Coast manufacturers** | Bars: minimills are already cheaper. Commodity steel: foreign mills have newer equipment. Shipbuilding: no evidence of an advantage. Owning mines, ships and railroads is a habit, not a strength. |
| **Economic engine** | Profit per ton shipped; profit per employee; return on capital; **cash profit per ton of capacity** | **Cash profit per ton of capacity, measured over the full cycle** | Profit per ton *shipped* hides idle mills. Per ton of *capacity* penalizes excess capacity and rewards cost and utilization together. |
| **Deep passion** | Making steel that holds up bridges, ships and cars; engineering pride; heritage of scale | **Making the hard, critical steels well** `[ASSUMPTION: no direct evidence on what people care about]` | Pride in size and empire. That is ego, not passion for the work. |

**Confirmation tests.** The hypothesis holds only if all four pass:
1. Delivered cost per ton at the core plants, on target grades, is at or below the landed price of comparable imports. *This is the decisive test.*
2. Margins on those grades held up better than the company average through the last downturn.
3. Customers in the target segments say proximity, quality and reliability are worth a price premium.
4. Metallurgical judgment suggests minimills are years away from making these grades `[ASSUMPTION]`.

**If test 1 fails,** there is no real intersection. In that case Ironbridge can probably be only good, and the honest plan is managed contraction to its best assets, not a push for greatness.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   ┌─► 1. Put capital and the best people into the core plants only
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │   2. Lower cost per ton, higher yield and quality   ◄── WEAKEST LINK
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │   3. Win long-term contracts with demanding sheet and plate buyers
   │      who value proximity and reliability
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │   4. Higher utilization at core plants through the cycle
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │   5. Higher cash profit per ton of capacity  (denominator)
   │            │
   │            ▼
   └── 6. Reinvest in core plants, share productivity gains with workers,
          retire capacity that fails the test
```

**Current push (the next one or two turns):**
1. **Measure link 2 honestly.** Get plant-by-plant cost per ton against foreign and minimill benchmarks.
2. **Stop feeding the old plants.** Freeze capital outside the core until the numbers are in.

**Doom-loop check.** Many signs are present:
- **Leadership churn:** four presidents in 15 years.
- **Big programs to force momentum:** a $3 billion modernization program and a $500 million expansion "to win sales."
- **Growth by acquisition** is in the company's DNA.
- **Reaction without understanding:** price increases instead of cost work.
- **No decision:** the company hasn't chosen among modernizing, seeking protection and cutting capacity.

The loop closes in logic but is not yet proven in practice.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop using price increases as the main profit lever.** The market now has importers and minimills that cap price.
2. **Stop spreading modernization capital across every plant.** Fund only plants that pass the cost test.
3. **Stop defending bars and simple shapes against minimills.** Give up that share in an orderly way.
4. **Stop adding capacity to "win sales."** In a falling market, capacity is a liability until it is proven competitive.
5. **Stop treating import protection as the strategy.** Trade defense can buy time but does not lower cost.
6. **Stop paying executives on anything other than full-cycle cash return.** End the perquisite culture visibly, starting with the tower.
7. **Stop owning mines, ore ships, railroads and shipyards by habit.** Keep each one only if it lowers delivered cost per ton or earns its own return.
8. **Stop promoting only insiders to the top.** Make outside hires and directors normal.

## 7. Core ideology and the BHAG

**Core values** `[ASSUMPTION: inferred, to be confirmed with the company's people]`:
1. Steel that holds: quality the customer never has to doubt.
2. Honest numbers, especially bad ones.
3. Capital is held in trust and earns its keep.
4. Respect for the workforce, which means sharing in gains and transitions handled with care.

**Core purpose:** Make the steel that critical things depend on.

**BHAG (20-year horizon):** Make Ironbridge's plants the lowest-cost integrated flat-rolled and plate producers in the world, earning above the cost of capital in every year of a full cycle.

**Clock-building mechanisms:**
- Plant-level profit and loss statements, plus a monthly benchmark of cost per ton against foreign and minimill costs that goes to the board.
- A capital hurdle that no project can bypass.
- Pay tied to full-cycle return.
- Productivity sharing for hourly workers.
- A majority of independent directors.
- A succession bench that includes outside candidates.
