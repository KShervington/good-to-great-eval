## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Brightwell is narrower than today's Brightwell, and its earnings swing less. It owns the everyday plastic products for organizing and cleaning a home: containers, storage, sink and bath, and trash. In those categories its design and durability are clearly better than cheaper rivals, so retailers stock it at a premium and shoppers ask for it by name. It launches fewer products, and each one either beats the SKU it replaces on profit or doesn't ship. Fill rates are the best in the category, which makes it the supplier big retailers least want to lose. Contracts and hedges share resin swings with customers, so a resin spike costs a quarter of earnings, not a year. Growth targets come out of the flywheel; the flywheel is not bent to meet a target. The company performs as well under its second post-turnaround CEO as it did under the first, and that is the endurance test it has not yet passed.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop publishing a 15% compound growth target.** Replace it with profit per pound of resin and fill-rate targets.
2. **Stop the rule of entering a new category every 12–18 months.** Enter a category only when it passes the Hedgehog test.
3. **Stop launching at a fixed pace of about one a day.** Each launch must replace or beat an existing SKU on fully costed profit `[ASSUMPTION about the mechanism]`.
4. **Stop letting launches grade themselves.** "Nine of ten meet their targets" is self-reported. Grade launches on year-three profit after cannibalization.
5. **Stop chasing volume at the largest customer without a margin floor** or resin pass-through terms.
6. **Stop acquisitions outside molded home products.** Freeze them, and review the toy and leisure units.
7. **Stop pushing the international share target** until home-market fill rate and SKU profitability are proven. Export the Hedgehog Concept, not the whole range.
