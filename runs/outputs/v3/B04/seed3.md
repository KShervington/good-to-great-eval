# Good to Great: Brightwell Housewares, Inc.

## Verdict

**Hedgehog Concept (working hypothesis):** Brightwell should be the best in the world at turning everyday household problems into durable, well-designed molded-resin products that earn the most profit per foot of retail shelf in mass-market housewares.

**Weakest flywheel link:** retailer service (order fill, on-time delivery). It is unmeasured, and SKU proliferation strains it.

**Top three stop-doing items:**
1. Stop promising 15% compounded growth.
2. Stop the "new category every 12–18 months" quota.
3. Stop counting new products as a success measure. One product a day is a cost, not a trophy.

**Most urgent brutal fact:** In Year −2, earnings rose 0.9% on 8% sales growth. Excluding the largest customer, sales grew only about 6.5% a year. The 15% promise rests on resin prices and one retailer, and Brightwell controls neither.

**Numbers that would change the answer:** order-fill and on-time rates, SKU-level profitability, resin hedging, and category share.

**Confidence:** moderate. The verdict rests on three years of public financials and stated goals. No operating data was available.

`Greatness potential: 30/100`

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

The great Brightwell makes fewer products than it does today, and each one earns its shelf space. It treats its largest retailers as partners to be served flawlessly. Order fill becomes a point of pride, not an unknown. It grows at whatever rate its hedgehog naturally supports, likely high single digits `[ASSUMPTION]`, and stops chasing a 15% number inherited from the turnaround era. New-product creativity stays. What changes is that a kill discipline sits next to it: every launch retires something, and no category is entered because the calendar says it's time. Margins hold through resin cycles because pricing power and cost discipline, not input-price luck, drive earnings. The company stays great after the turnaround CEO has left, and after the current one does too.

**Sentence:** see the verdict.

**Confirmation tests (status: working hypothesis):**
- Does this concept explain past wins? In part. The turnaround succeeded by cutting divisions and building a focused product machine. Data on how productive the shelf space is would confirm the rest.
- Does it rule things out? Yes: toys and leisure, commodity price-fighting, category quotas, and the launch-count culture.
- Is it uncomfortable to commit to? Yes. It means walking back publicly stated goals.
- **Evidence that would confirm it:** SKU profitability showing a long, low-return tail; retailer shelf-productivity data showing Brightwell above category average; margin stability once complexity is cut.

**Doom-loop check: warning signs are present.**
- *Big programs to force momentum:* growth targets that require a new category every 12–18 months.
- *Acquisitions outside the hedgehog:* a toy company, a cooler and jug maker, and a smaller manufacturer.
- *Volume as a substitute for coherence:* one new product a day, and a goal of one-third of sales from products under five years old.
- *New direction layered on top:* a big international push while domestic service is unmeasured.
- These are not yet a doom loop, but they are its typical preconditions.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop** publicly committing to 15% compounded growth in sales, earnings and EPS.
2. **Stop** the quota of entering a new product category every 12–18 months.
3. **Stop** treating "one new product a day" and the hit rate of "nine of ten meet targets" as success measures. Targets are self-set and undefined.
4. **Stop** adding SKUs without retiring others. Apply one-in, one-out at minimum.
5. **Stop** using acquisitions to close the growth gap. Run a fit review on the toy and leisure holdings.
6. **Stop** the international push as a growth plug until domestic service metrics are measured and healthy.
7. **Stop** fighting lower-priced rivals on price in commodity lines. Either win on productivity or step back from those lines.
8. **Stop** citing awards and streaks (42 years of records, Most Admired) as evidence of strategic health in internal decisions.
