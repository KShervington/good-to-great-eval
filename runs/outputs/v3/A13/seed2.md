# Kestrel Industries: Good to Great

**Verdict: Good, not yet great. A strong candidate that a full downturn has not yet tested.**

- **Hedgehog:** Make simple steel and joists at the lowest cost per ton in America by melting scrap in small rural plants, run by teams who share directly in what they produce.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** output → pay. The culture runs on high volume, and the recession plus the Texas start-up will cut volume.
- **Stop:** (1) acquisitions outside steel and joists; (2) announcing a fourth mill before Texas reaches standard output; (3) adding headquarters staff or management layers.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** the cost lead rests on culture, because anyone can buy the technology. That culture is about to take its first real pay cut.
- **Numbers that would change this:** cost per ton against integrated mills and other minimills; earnings and return on capital for the last two years.
- **Confidence:** moderate. It rests on structural and cultural evidence plus a few years of earnings growth, with no current financials.

Greatness potential: 60/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Kestrel is the lowest-cost steel producer in the country. It proves this with a published cost per ton, not a claim. It stays profitable per ton through recessions that put integrated rivals and import-dependent fabricators into losses. It grows by repeating one playbook: a scrap-fed mill in a rural region, staffed by teams paid on output, feeding its own joist plants and outside bar customers. It is the same playbook every time, and each new mill starts up faster than the one before. Headquarters stays tiny. Nobody gets laid off. Nobody dies at work. Its edge is something copycats can buy the equipment for but cannot buy: a workforce that thinks like owners. It refuses the things that would dilute that edge: conglomerate deals, products its process can't make cheaply, and depending on import protection. It moves into bigger markets only when a casting process it can run at minimill cost makes them reachable.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

> **Kestrel makes simple steel and joists at the lowest cost per ton in America, by turning scrap into product in small rural plants run by teams who share directly in what they produce.**

**Denominator:** profit per ton. Tons per employee-hour and cost per ton are its two drivers.

**Tests applied:**
- *Explains past wins?* Yes. The joist division survived the conglomerate because it was simple and low-cost. Integrating backward into steel removed an 80% import dependence. Earnings jumped once the mills ran reliably.
- *Rules things out?* Yes. It rules out conglomerate acquisitions, a flat-rolled push with today's process, specialty grades, and urban or high-overhead plants.
- *Uncomfortable?* Yes. It means turning down attractive downstream products, staying out of the import-protection campaign, and pacing expansion below what the cash flow would allow.

**Confirmation tests (evidence that would turn this into a PASS):**
1. Kestrel's cost per ton is measurably below both the integrated mills (such as Rival B) and other minimills.
2. Profit per ton stays positive through this recession.
3. The Texas mill reaches standard output much faster than the first mill, whose caster failed for more than a year.
4. Copycat minimills using the same equipment cannot match Kestrel's tons per employee-hour.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   ┌──► 1. Team output pay + egalitarian culture
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │    2. High tons per employee-hour
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │    3. Lowest cost per ton (scrap → EAF → caster → bars/joists)
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │    4. Win on price in bars and joists (own steel feeds own joists)
   │            │  → PROFIT PER TON
   │            ▼
   │    5. Big bonuses + profit sharing (best rural workers stay)
   │       AND cash to fund the next mill without heavy debt
   │            │
   │            ▼
   └─── 6. Next rural mill, same playbook, faster start-up
```

**Weakest link:** the step from 2 to 5. Pay depends on output. When the recession cuts volume and short weeks cut take-home pay, the turn that holds the culture together slows. A Texas start-up that stalls would hurt the same link: a new crew on low output means low pay and no bonus.

**Current push (next one or two turns):**
1. Get the Texas mill to standard output fast, using the people who fixed the first mill's caster.
2. Get the culture through its first serious pay squeeze intact. That means shared hours, no layoffs, an open explanation of the pay math, and no quiet softening or tightening of standards.

**Doom-loop check:**
- *Past doom loop, already escaped:* the conglomerate grew by stock-financed acquisitions in fashionable sectors. That was reaction without understanding, a new direction after each new direction.
- *Current signs:* the open debate between more mills and new downstream products is the main risk. If the recession pressure settles it, rather than the Hedgehog, the company slides back toward the old pattern.
- *Cadence:* three mills in about six years is fast, but each one follows the same model. That is coherent so far. No doom loop is present today.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **No acquisitions outside steel and joists, and no paying with stock.** This is how the company nearly died last time.
2. **No fourth-mill announcement until Texas reaches standard output.**
3. **No new headquarters staff or management layers.** Treat four layers as a ceiling, not a floor.
4. **No push into flat-rolled sheet or specialty grades with today's process.** Watch for a casting process that changes this, but don't chase the largest market with tools that can't serve it cheaply.
5. **No downstream products that fail all three tests.** A product passes only if it is made from Kestrel steel, is simple and high-volume, and can run on team incentive pay.
6. **No campaign for import protection as strategy.** Protection mainly shelters high-cost integrated rivals. Compete on cost. `[ASSUMPTION: a judgment call. Revisit if imports are shown to be priced below foreign production cost, not just below U.S. prices.]`
7. **No layoffs, and no rewriting the pay formula, to fix a bad quarter.** Short hours shared by everyone remain the downturn tool.
8. **No urban, high-overhead plant sites.**
