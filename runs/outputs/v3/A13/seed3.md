# Kestrel Industries: Good to Great

**Verdict: good, not yet great, and a strong candidate.**

- **Hedgehog (working):** Make simple steel, and the joists built from it, at the lowest cost per ton in America by giving the newest proven scrap-melting technology to rural teams paid for what they produce.
- **Weakest flywheel link:** volume → pay. In a recession, output falls and bonuses collapse. That tests the culture that creates the cost edge.
- **Top three stop-doing items:** buying businesses outside steel; adding mills faster than proven start-up teams can be grown; leaning on import protection instead of cost.
- **Most urgent brutal fact:** Mill 3 starts up into a falling market just as short weeks cut take-home pay sharply.
- **Numbers that would change this:** cost per ton against integrated mills and other minimills; the last two years' earnings and return on capital.
- **Confidence:** moderate. It rests on a deliberate culture and a strong earnings trend. The cost advantage itself is asserted in the dossier but not measured.

Greatness potential: 60/100

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

Great Kestrel makes more profit per ton than any steelmaker in America, through booms and recessions, for fifteen years or more. It stays deliberately narrow: simple steel products and the joists that consume them. Small regional mills buy proven equipment early and run it better than anyone else, because the people running it earn more when it runs well and are never laid off when it doesn't. Headquarters stays tiny. Each new mill opens only when a seasoned team is ready to run it. Its defense against imports and copycat minimills is cost and execution, not tariffs or secrets. It outlasts its current chief executive because its pay system, its plant-manager bench and its stop-doing rules work without him.

## 2. The Hedgehog Concept

**Sentence (working hypothesis):** *Kestrel makes simple steel, and the joists built from it, at the lowest cost per ton in America, by giving the newest proven scrap-melting technology to rural teams paid for what they produce.*

| Circle | Candidates considered | Narrowed to | What it is *not* best at |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Best in the world at** | Joist fabrication (about 20% U.S. share nine years ago); minimill operation; vertical integration from scrap to joist; incentive-pay plant culture | **Converting scrap into simple steel products at the lowest cost per ton**, with joists as the in-house anchor customer | Flat-rolled sheet (the largest market; still needs integrated mills); high-grade or specialty steel; anything outside steel (the conglomerate era proved this); political or trade advocacy |
| **Economic engine** | Profit per employee; profit per ton; return on capital per mill | **Profit per ton of finished steel** | — |
| **Passion** | Team output and earning by producing; egalitarianism (same hard hat for everyone, four layers); job security (no layoffs); frugality (2,000 sq ft HQ) | **Proving that an egalitarian, pay-for-output workforce can out-produce the giants** | — |

**Denominator:** profit per ton of finished steel. Profit per employee is a close second and should be tracked as a check.

**Tests:**
- *Explains past wins?* Yes. The joist division survived the conglomerate collapse. Making its own steel replaced 80% foreign supply. Earnings rose 140% and then 70% as the first mill stabilized.
- *Rules things out?* Yes. No unrelated acquisitions. No sheet until a low-cost route exists. Downstream products only if they consume Kestrel's own steel and are simple and standardized enough for low cost to win.
- *Uncomfortable to commit to?* Yes. It means refusing growth that doesn't fit, and refusing to make import protection part of the plan.

**Confirmation tests (what would make this a PASS):**
1. A cost-per-ton benchmark showing Kestrel below integrated mills *and* at or below other minimills on comparable products.
2. Profit per ton holding positive, and above peers, through the current recession.
3. Mill 3 reaching target output faster than Mill 1 did, which would show the advantage is repeatable and not a one-off.

## 3. The flywheel

```
   ┌─► 1. Egalitarian, pay-for-output teams in rural plants (no layoffs)
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │   2. High tons per worker-hour on newest proven EAF + caster tech
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │   3. Lowest cost per ton
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │   4. Win share in bars, light shapes and joists vs. integrated mills and imports;
   │      mills stay full (joist plants as anchor customer)
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │   5. High PROFIT PER TON
   │            │
   │            ▼
   │   6. Big bonuses + profit sharing (attract and keep the best rural workers)
   │      + cash to build the next regional mill with newer tech
   └────────────┘
```

**Weakest link:** 4 → 6. Pay depends on volume. In a recession, falling volume drains bonuses, which strains link 1. Link 2 is the second-weakest: the first mill's caster failed for over a year, and Mill 3 is starting up now.

**Current push (the next one or two turns):**
1. **Get Mill 3 to stable output fast.** Staff its start-up with veterans from Mill 1, who lived through the caster failures, and set caster contingencies before start-up.
2. **Protect link 1 through the downturn.** Share the pain visibly from the top down, keep the no-layoff promise, and use short-week hours for maintenance, training and cost reduction, so the recovery starts from a lower cost base.

**Doom-loop check:**
- *Past doom loop:* the "atomic age" conglomerate was a textbook case. It bought businesses with stock, chased new directions and lacked a coherent core.
- *Present signs:* the open debate over "more mills vs. new downstream products" could restart the loop if it is settled by opportunity instead of by the Hedgehog. Building Mill 3 into a recession is defensible as long as it stays a disciplined push and doesn't turn into a reach for salvation.
- *Result:* no current doom loop. There is one live risk: the expansion debate.

## 5. Stop-doing list

1. **Stop considering acquisitions outside steel.** The conglomerate past is the cautionary tale.
2. **Stop approving a new mill before a proven GM and start-up crew are ready for it.** People set the pace of expansion, not opportunity.
3. **Stop treating "new downstream products" as open-ended.** Approve one only if it (a) consumes Kestrel steel, (b) is simple and standardized enough for low cost to win, and (c) can be built by an incentive team in a rural plant.
4. **Stop chasing flat-rolled or high-grade products** until a technology exists that makes them low-cost from scrap.
5. **Stop leaving the import-protection question open in a way that invites reliance on it.** Recommended position: Kestrel's defense is cost, and the company won't build its plan around protection. `[ASSUMPTION]` The board may decide otherwise for industry reasons, but strategy shouldn't depend on it.
6. **Stop treating volume as the goal.** The goal is profit per ton. Don't fill mills with below-cost tonnage just to keep bonuses up.
7. **Stop adding headquarters staff or management layers.** Four layers is the ceiling.

## 7. Core ideology and the BHAG

**Core values (draft):**
1. **No classes:** the same hard hat, the same benefits, few layers.
2. **Pay for performance:** people earn more when the team produces more.
3. **Share the pain:** downturns mean fewer hours for everyone, starting at the top, and no layoffs.
4. **Safety comes before output.** This is newly explicit after the fire.
5. **Frugality:** small HQ, rural plants, no waste.

**Core purpose:** *Prove that American steel can be made competitively by treating the people who make it as partners.*

**BHAG (full; 20-year horizon):** *Within twenty years, earn more profit per ton than any steelmaker in America, at a scale that makes the integrated giants study us.*
