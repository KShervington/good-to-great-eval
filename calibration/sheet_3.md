## Dossier (the analyst's only input)

# Subject dossier: Varden Communications

**As of:** the start of Year 0, as full-year results for Year −1 were being reported. Everything below was known or knowable on that date. Nothing after it is included.
**Subject type:** Business (publicly listed electronics and communications company)

## Overview
Varden is a U.S. electronics company founded about 70 years ago. It began in car radios and moved into two-way radio, semiconductors and cellular telephones. It left consumer television many years ago and the car-radio business about 12 years ago. Its founding family still leads it. The company prizes continual self-renewal and a culture of open, sometimes combative, technical debate. The business press has described it as a company that likes to make its own products obsolete.

Its main businesses:
- **Wireless (about 60% of sales):** cellular phones and infrastructure, pagers, two-way radio. The company says it is No. 1 in the world in cellular phones, paging and two-way radio.
- **Semiconductors (about 29%):** including a microprocessor family developed in alliance with two large computer makers to challenge the dominant PC chip supplier.
- **Other:** automotive and industrial electronics, and computing and data products.

## Financial position
- **Net sales:** $17.0 billion in Year −3, $22.2 billion in Year −2, $27.0 billion in Year −1. That is 24% compound annual growth over five years, and the company has grown about fivefold in a decade.
- **Net earnings:** $1.02 billion, $1.56 billion and $1.78 billion over the same three years. Fully diluted EPS was $1.78, $2.65 and $2.93.
- R&D spending was $2.2 billion in the latest year, up from $1.9 billion. Patents granted rose from 613 to 1,016 a year over the past four years.
- About 63% of sales are outside the U.S., and about 142,000 people work for the company. Its investment in China is among the largest of any U.S. company.
- In the latest annual letter, management warned that wireless pricing pressure had hurt earnings and that earnings comparisons would be difficult for the next few quarters.

## Market position
- **Cell phones:** the company has held roughly 45% of the world market in recent years, and an industry analyst put its U.S. share at about 60% two years ago. Its phones are mainly analog, the standard used by most of the world's subscribers today.
- Worldwide cellular subscribers grew from 23 million to about 85 million in three years.
- **Digital:** carriers are beginning to plan digital networks, and competing digital standards are emerging.
- The company will soon launch the world's smallest cell phone, an analog clamshell. Executives are very proud of it.

## Leadership
- **CEO:** a career company executive who became CEO three years ago, when his predecessor left to run another large company.
- **President and COO:** the founder's grandson, aged 45, with a marketing rather than engineering background.
- **Chairman of the executive committee:** the founder's son, who led the company for nearly three decades. He is credited with its quality movement and with seeding many of its long-range bets.
- The company won the first national quality award, about eight years ago. It pioneered the "Six Sigma" goal of 3.4 defects per million.

## Strategy and known problems
- **Growth goal:** after the founder's son retired from the CEO role, management set a goal of doubling the company's size every five years.
- **Satellite-phone venture:** the company is the largest investor in a separately financed venture to build a global satellite telephone system of 66 low-orbit satellites, so that a handheld phone can call from anywhere on Earth. It began as a small internal experiment about ten years ago and was spun into a separate company five years ago. At year end, the company's equity investment and commitments were about $400 million, with about $160 million more committed this month. It holds contracts worth about $6.5 billion over ten years to build and run the system, and remains obligated on its subcontracts even if the venture cannot pay. The system is due to be completed within three years. The decision on whether to commit to the full, far more expensive launch phase is approaching.
- **Analog versus digital:** a senior leader of the cellular business told an internal meeting that tens of millions of analog customers cannot be wrong. Others in the company argue that carriers will soon demand digital phones.
- **Distribution:** for the new phone, the cellular group favors a premium distribution program. Under it, carriers would get the phone only if they agree to carry a high share of the company's phones and give them prominent displays.
- **Other bets:** microprocessors, wireless data, and large investments in China and other emerging markets.

## What is not in this dossier
- Market shares in digital handsets and in the competing digital standards.
- The satellite venture's market research and demand forecasts.
- Profitability by business segment.
- Carrier reaction to the planned distribution program.

## 1. The great version in one paragraph

A great Varden is the company carriers trust to take them through every wireless generation. It obsoletes its own analog phones before competitors do. It ships Six Sigma-quality digital handsets and infrastructure on each major emerging standard, and it treats carriers as partners rather than as shelf space to control. It measures itself by profit per wireless subscriber served, not by size. Capital flows first to wireless and to the semiconductors that make wireless products better. The satellite venture, the head-on PC microprocessor challenge and size-driven expansion are capped, ring-fenced or exited. Its tradition of combative technical debate is pointed at its own blind spots, with mechanisms that make dissent about the core franchise impossible to ignore. It stays great beyond the founding family because its renewal discipline is built into its processes rather than carried by individuals.
