# Subject dossier: Meridian Radio Corporation

**As of:** late summer of Year 0, just after second-quarter results were reported. Everything below was known or knowable on that date. Nothing after it is included.
**Subject type:** Business (publicly listed consumer-electronics manufacturer)

## Overview
Meridian is a Midwest maker of television sets, radios and related products. Its picture tubes come from a wholly owned tube subsidiary. For more than three decades it was led by a flamboyant, visionary founder-promoter. He pioneered portable home radios, moved the company into television early, and introduced one of the first wireless TV remote controls. He died eight years ago.

The brand stands for quality. Meridian has kept hand-wired circuits in its sets, while rivals moved to cheaper printed circuit boards, and it advertises its sets as hand-crafted. Repairmen favor the sets because they are easy to service and recommend them to customers. About one production worker in six is a quality inspector.

## Financial position
- **Year −6:** sales of $254 million and profit of $15.2 million, up from $141.5 million and $6.2 million four years earlier. The company had no debt.
- **Second quarter of Year 0:** sales rose 60% to $146.8 million, and earnings rose 85% to $7.5 million. Most of the gain came from booming demand for color television. Color-set sales alone in the quarter were larger than the company's total sales in the same quarter two years earlier.
- A dollar invested in Meridian stock at the start of Year −16 and held through the end of Year −1 grew more than a hundredfold, more than ten times the return of the overall market.

## Market position
- **Black-and-white TV:** Meridian has been the No. 1 U.S. maker since Year −7, when it passed **Rival R**, the large electronics and broadcasting company that had long led the industry. Six years ago, Meridian and Rival R each held more than 20% of the U.S. TV market. About 25 other companies shared the rest.
- **Color TV:** Rival R pioneered color television and leads the color set market. The founder held color back for years, saying it would stay in the lab until it was ready. Meridian launched its first color line five years ago, and color is now its fastest-growing business. This year its tube subsidiary bought a large plant near its home city to make color picture tubes.
- **Imports:** Japanese manufacturers have begun selling small, low-priced sets in the U.S. Meridian's leaders regard them as cheap products that are no threat to an American quality brand.

## Leadership
- After the founder's death, leadership passed to his long-time lieutenants: an elderly chairman who had been his finance chief for decades, and a president who joined the company as its lawyer and has run day-to-day operations for several years.
- The president has publicly said that integrated circuits are not yet ready for use on any significant scale in radio and TV sets.

## Strategy and known problems
- **Overtaking Rival R in color.** The company's ambition is to become No. 1 in color sets as it did in black-and-white. Reaching that goal would require more tube and set capacity.
- **Quality positioning.** Meridian's identity rests on hand-built quality and U.S. manufacturing. The industry is moving to printed circuits, transistors and, in time, integrated circuits, which favor automated, lower-cost production. Meridian has been slower than some rivals to move from vacuum tubes to transistors.
- **Pay TV.** Meridian has spent years developing a subscription television system, in which viewers pay to unscramble programs. It is running a multi-year over-the-air test in a northeastern city. Wider commercial service depends on approval from federal regulators.
- **Inventory and distribution.** Its control of inventory and dealer stock is regarded as among the best in the industry.
- **Open questions:** how fast to add color capacity, and whether to borrow to fund it; whether to keep hand-wiring as volumes grow; how seriously to take low-cost imports; and how much to keep investing in pay TV.
