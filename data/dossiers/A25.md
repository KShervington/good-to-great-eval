# Subject dossier: Dunmore-Kessler Corporation

**As of:** early in Year 0. Everything below was known or knowable on that date. Nothing after it is included.
**Subject type:** Business (publicly listed industrial and electronics manufacturer)

## Overview
Dunmore-Kessler is a Great Lakes industrial company founded nearly 80 years ago as a maker of printing presses. Through a series of mergers it became one of the country's main makers of printing equipment: offset lithographic presses, envelope presses, paper cutters, bindery equipment and hot-metal typesetting machines. It also makes corrugated-paper machinery.

Over the past 15 years it has moved into electronics:
- About 15 to 20 years ago it bought a maker of radio broadcast transmitters and microwave gear, and an electronics firm in the East.
- **Six years ago** it merged with a fast-growing aerospace electronics company based in the South. That company was founded about 20 years ago by two engineers. At the merger it had about 3,000 employees and more than $50 million in annual sales, and it had built tracking, telemetry and data systems for satellites, manned space flights and missile programs. Dunmore-Kessler paid about $56 million in stock, valuing the target's earnings at about twice its own multiple. It wanted the target's engineering talent as much as its earnings.
- About four years ago it bought a maker of point-to-point radio communications equipment in a share exchange.
- **Last year** it bought a large electrical company's television broadcast equipment line (cameras, transmitters, studio equipment, antennas) for $5.5 million in cash, and a maker of computer terminals and data-communications subsystems for $20 million in cash.

## Financial position
- Under the long-time former chief, who controlled the company for about 25 years, annual sales grew from about $10 million to almost $200 million before the aerospace merger. After the merger, combined sales passed $250 million.
- Sales in Year −3 were $380 million, and electronics made up about half of them.
- Earnings growth has been weak for about five years. The cost of integrating acquisitions and launching new product lines has weighed on profits, and investors have largely ignored the stock.

## Market position
- **Printing equipment:** a leading domestic supplier of presses and finishing equipment. Its machines are still largely mechanical.
- **Government electronics:** a significant contractor for space and defense communications and data systems.
- **Commercial communications:** broadcast transmitters and studio equipment, microwave and radio links, data terminals. Competitors include large electrical and electronics conglomerates. [Market shares are not available.]

## Leadership
- **Chairman and chief executive:** joined 17 years ago from a smaller printing-machinery company, where he had been president. He trained in mathematics and physics. He became president of Dunmore-Kessler in Year −12, chief executive in Year −5 and chairman in Year −1. He worked with the former chief on the aerospace merger and led the acquisitions since.
- **Head of electronics:** formerly president of the acquired aerospace company, now an executive vice-president in charge of the electronics businesses.
- Headquarters remains in the Great Lakes city where the company grew up. The main electronics operations are several states away.

## Strategy and known problems
- The company's stated approach since the merger is **technology transfer:** taking technology developed under government contracts and turning it into commercial products. Promotion and demotion of managers is tied to developing commercial products from defense work.
- Management has argued that future printing equipment will need electronics, and that this was the reason for the aerospace merger.
- Problems:
  - integrating several acquisitions at once;
  - building commercial markets for technologies developed for government customers;
  - reliance on government contracts, whose funding rises and falls with federal budgets;
  - weak earnings growth and an indifferent stock market.
- There is an open question about the balance of the company: how much to keep investing in the original printing-equipment business versus the newer electronics and communications businesses, and whether the two belong together.
