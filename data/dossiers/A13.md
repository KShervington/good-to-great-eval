# Subject dossier: Kestrel Industries

**As of:** spring of Year 0. Everything below was known or knowable on that date. Nothing after it is included.
**Subject type:** Business (publicly listed manufacturer)

## Overview
Kestrel Industries is a small U.S. steel company headquartered in the Southeast. Twenty years ago it was the shell of a failed automaker, turned by dissident shareholders into an "atomic age" conglomerate with businesses in nuclear instruments, chemicals, electronics and energy, bought mostly with stock.

About 13 years ago the conglomerate bought a small maker of steel joists and girders (the light structural beams used in roofs and floors of commercial buildings). That joist division was soon the only healthy part of the company.

Ten years ago the company defaulted on two major loans and was close to collapse (some accounts say it filed for bankruptcy). The board made the head of the joist division chief executive. He sold or closed about half the divisions, committed the company to joists, and moved the headquarters to a 2,000-square-foot rented office in the Southeast. The company took its current name three years ago and listed on the New York Stock Exchange the same year.

Today the company has two linked businesses:
- **Joists and girders:** five fabrication plants in small towns across the South, the Plains and the Midwest. The division held about 20% of the U.S. joist market about nine years ago. [No current share figure is available.]
- **Steel bars ("minimills"):** Six years ago, foreign producers were supplying up to 80% of the joist division's raw steel. The company decided to make its own. It does not use the traditional coke-oven and blast-furnace route. Instead it melts scrap in electric-arc furnaces, casts it continuously and rolls it into bars and light shapes. The first mill, in a rural southern town, was financed with a $6 million loan and started up six years ago. A second mill in the Plains was announced three years ago. A third, in Texas, is starting up this year. The mills supply the joist plants and sell to outside customers.

## Financial position
- When the current CEO took over, the company had about $20 million in sales and $7 million in assets, and was losing about $400,000 a year. Restructuring produced a final loss of about $2.2 million. In his first three years, sales grew from about $21 million to $35 million.
- The first mill's continuous caster broke down repeatedly for more than a year after start-up. The stock fell to a few cents a share.
- Earnings then rose 140% four years ago and another 70% three years ago. The company paid its first cash dividend two years ago.
- Industry context: U.S. steel profits reached records last year after price controls ended. Demand is now falling sharply with the recession, and imported steel is being offered well below U.S. prices. [Kestrel's own sales and earnings for last year are not available in this dossier.]

## Market position
- Kestrel competes with the large integrated steelmakers, including **Rival B**, one of the two biggest U.S. steel companies, in simple products such as bars, where minimills have a cost advantage. Kestrel is far smaller than Rival B.
- Minimills are a new and growing segment. Several other small operators are building scrap-based mills.
- Kestrel's mills are limited to simpler, lower-grade products. Flat-rolled sheet, the largest steel market (autos, appliances), still requires integrated mills.

## Leadership
- **Chief executive (CEO for ten years):** a mechanical engineer with a graduate degree, who ran the joist division before taking over. He works from the small headquarters office with a handful of staff.
- **Structure:** there are four management layers between the CEO and a laborer on the mill floor. In the restructuring ten years ago, the number of senior management positions was cut from 12 to two.
- **Pay and culture:** the workforce is nonunion. Production pay is tied to team output against standards, and bonuses can more than double a worker's annual wage. There is a profit-sharing plan. No employee has ever been laid off; in downturns everyone works fewer hours. Everyone wears the same color hard hat. After a mill fire last year killed four employees, the company guaranteed college scholarships for employees' children.
- Plants are deliberately located in rural areas.

## Strategy and known problems
- The company's stated focus is low-cost production of joists and simple steel products. Debate continues about how far to expand: more minimills in new regions, or new downstream products.
- Minimill technology is not proprietary. Electric-arc furnaces and continuous casters can be bought from equipment suppliers, and competitors are copying the model.
- Scrap is the main raw material, and its price is set in an open market that Kestrel does not control.
- The recession is cutting construction activity, which drives joist demand.
- The incentive system depends on output staying high. Short-hour weeks in a downturn cut take-home pay sharply.
- Six years ago the government persuaded Japanese and European producers to cut their U.S. shipments voluntarily by about 25%. Whether the industry should seek more protection from imports is a live debate. Kestrel has not stated a position.
