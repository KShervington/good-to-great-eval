# Subject dossier: Stanwick Mailing Systems

**As of:** late in Year 0. Everything below was known or knowable on that date. Nothing after it is included.
**Subject type:** Business (publicly listed manufacturer and lessor of office equipment)

## Overview
Stanwick was formed about 50 years ago by merging two small firms: one owned the patent on the first commercially practical postage meter, and the other made stamping machines. A postage meter prints postage directly onto mail, so businesses do not have to buy and lick stamps. After years of trials, the Post Office approved meters, and Stanwick grew with them. Thirteen years ago it had about 281,000 meters in service, and metered mail made up 43% of all U.S. postage.

Stanwick holds the great majority of the U.S. postage-meter market (company histories put its share at 85–90%). About 16 years ago, because domestic competition had almost disappeared, the government brought an antitrust action. Under the resulting consent decree, Stanwick must license its meter patents free to any company that wants to compete.

The Post Office does not let Stanwick sell meters, only lease them. So the company runs a large national field force that installs, inspects and services meters at customers' offices. That gives it a regular, recurring relationship with a very large number of business customers.

## Business lines
- **Postage meters and mailing equipment:** meters, plus related mail-handling machines. Five years ago meters were 53% of total sales.
- **Copiers:** a division started six years ago, first with a tabletop office copier. The market is dominated by a much larger copier company. Stanwick competes on lower prices and service packages.
- **Price-marking and inventory tags:** a company bought five years ago that has become the largest U.S. supplier of price-marking and inventory-control equipment for retailers.
- **Leasing finance:** a credit subsidiary formed two years ago helps customers lease Stanwick equipment.
- **Retail point-of-sale terminals:** Stanwick took a 64% stake in a joint venture with a small computer company to make electronic cash-register and checkout systems for retailers. The venture has lost money. This year Stanwick wrote off its investment, a loss of $42 million.

## Financial position
- Revenue this year: about $385 million. Thirteen years ago revenue was about $57 million, with about 20% from non-meter products.

## Market position
- Meters: dominant, with recurring leasing and service revenue. The free-license decree allows competitors in, but none has gained much share.
- Copiers: a small player against a giant that invented plain-paper copying.
- Office equipment more broadly is changing. Plain-paper copying and electronics are entering the office. A long-established maker of addressing and duplicating machines, **Rival A**, of similar size, faces the same shift.
- Three years ago Congress turned the Post Office into an independent government corporation, the U.S. Postal Service. How it will set postage rates and treat metered mail is uncertain.

## Leadership
- The long-serving chairman, who joined the company 54 years ago and was president for more than two decades, is retiring this year. He oversaw the first mass-market postage meter.
- A new chief executive is taking over. [His background and track record are not documented in this dossier.]

## Strategy and known problems
- The company's identity is built around the postage meter. The open strategic question is where growth should come from. One argument: meters are a mature, regulated business, and the future lies in broader office equipment such as copiers, retail systems and electronics. The opposing argument: the point-of-sale loss shows the company should not chase markets where larger, better-funded firms lead.
- The copier business faces a far stronger competitor, and its profitability is unclear.
- The free-license decree and the new Postal Service create regulatory uncertainty around the core business.
