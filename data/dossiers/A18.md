# Subject dossier: Duplicon Corporation

**As of:** late in Year 0. Everything below was known or knowable on that date. Nothing after it is included.
**Subject type:** Business (publicly listed manufacturer of office equipment)

## Overview
Duplicon was formed about 40 years ago by merging two old office-machine companies:
- an addressing-machine maker founded in Chicago about 80 years ago. Its machines print names and addresses from embossed metal plates kept in filing trays, so businesses, utilities and governments can address large mailings and bills. The U.S. government relied on its machines during the First World War;
- a letter-duplicating-machine maker founded in a Great Lakes industrial city about 70 years ago.

The merged company consolidated in that Great Lakes city, where its headquarters and main plant still are. After the Second World War it expanded greatly at home and abroad.

## Business lines
- **Addressing systems:** embossing machines, addressing plates and printers. These are the company's founding franchise and are very widely installed in offices.
- **Offset duplicators:** small offset printing presses for offices, schools and print shops. One model became extremely popular and made Duplicon a leading name in the "quick print" field.
- **Typesetting:** composing machines used for in-house typesetting.
- **Credit-card and data-recording products:** after the Depression the company moved into credit-card manufacturing and related equipment.
- **Engineering graphics:** reproduction equipment for engineering drawings.

## Market position
- Duplicon's core products (embossed-plate addressing and offset duplicating) are mechanical and ink-based.
- Office copying is shifting toward plain-paper photocopiers, a market dominated by **Rival X**, which invented the technology. That shift has already hurt older duplicating and copying companies.
- **Rival S**, a postage-meter company of similar size, faces a related shift in office equipment. Rival S holds a near-monopoly in its core product, has diversified into copiers and retail systems, and this year wrote off a large loss on a point-of-sale terminal venture.

- The company is run by professional managers at its long-time Midwest headquarters. [Names, tenure and backgrounds of the current chief executive and senior team are not available in this dossier.]

## Strategy and known problems
- The company has a large installed base and a well-known name with office managers, schools and printers.
- Its duplicating products face replacement by plain-paper copying, a technology it does not lead.
- The open strategic question is how to respond. One option: defend and improve the existing duplicating and addressing lines, where the company is strong. Another: invest heavily to become a leader in new electronic office technology, competing with much larger firms. A third: narrow to the niches where it leads (such as quick-print shops) and shrink the rest.
- The company's workforce and main plant are concentrated in its older Midwest headquarters city.
