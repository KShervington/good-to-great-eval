# Subject dossier: Continental Provision Company

**As of:** early in Year 0, just after the close of the fiscal year. Everything below was known or knowable on that date. Nothing after it is included.
**Subject type:** Business (publicly listed grocery retailer)

## Overview
Continental Provision is the largest grocery chain in the country, and one of the oldest. It was founded more than a century ago as an importing and retail business, and grew into a national chain of grocery stores. For decades it was the dominant American retailer: about 40 years ago it was roughly twice the size of the largest general-merchandise retailer and four times the size of **Rival L**, a Midwestern grocery chain. The two brothers who built the modern company ran it until their deaths in Year −22 and Year −16. A charitable foundation they set up received the family's shares. It held more than a third of the stock when the company went public about 15 years ago.

The company is heavily vertically integrated. It makes a large share of its own products under store brands. About eight years ago it opened a $25 million, 1.5-million-square-foot food-processing plant, described as the largest of its kind under one roof.

## Financial position
- About 15 years ago, sales passed $3 billion for the first time, with profits of about $54 million.
- About four years ago, sales were well over $5 billion, more than twice those of the closest competitor.
- The traditional profit margin has been about 1 cent per dollar of sales.
- **This year's loss:** for the fiscal year just ended, the company expects a loss of about $51 million.
- **Capital policy:** the company has long avoided debt and paid out most of its profit as dividends, which has limited investment in its stores.
- **Share price:** the stock peaked at $70 about 12 years ago and has fallen to about $17.
- **Takeover bid:** a conglomerate has just made a tender offer at $20 a share. Management opposes it.

## Market position
- **Stores:** about 4,000, many of them small and old. Two years ago, half were under 8,000 square feet, smaller than competitors' newer supermarkets.
- **Service problems:** cuts in store staffing have led to long checkout lines and empty shelves. Heavy reliance on store brands means national brands are often out of stock.
- **Labor:** costs are high because the workforce has aged and accumulated seniority.
- **Retreats:** the company left the West Coast entirely in recent years.
- **The price war:**
  - Four years ago, with sales slowing, it began converting weaker stores to a discount format.
  - Last year it converted nearly all of its stores to a deep-discount pricing program, aiming to win back shoppers. Competitors matched the price cuts, setting off an industry-wide price war.
  - Analysts say the program was not supported by adequate cost controls. It produced the year's loss.
- **Rival L**, now one of the largest chains, has been closing or selling stores in markets where it is weak. It has paired supermarkets with its own drugstores, and it is testing larger stores with specialty departments and electronic checkout scanners.

## Leadership
- **Chief executive (since Year −2):** joined the company almost 40 years ago as a store clerk.
- **Succession:** since the founding brothers' era, the top job has passed through several long-serving insiders. They include a former assistant to the brothers who led the company for about a decade. The company has never recruited a senior executive from outside.
- **Culture:** critics describe senior management as overly conservative and attribute this partly to the practice of promoting all top officers from within.

## Strategy and known problems
- **Current strategy:** win back sales volume with low prices across the chain, using the existing stores.
- **Store base:** many stores are too small or badly located to run a high-volume discount format efficiently. Replacing them would take large amounts of capital, and the company has historically paid out most of its profit as dividends.
- **The takeover bid** adds pressure to show results quickly.
- **Open questions:**
  - Should the company close large numbers of stores?
  - Should it exit more regions?
  - Should it change its dividend policy to fund new, larger stores?
  - Should it bring in outside management?

## What is not in this dossier
- Market share by region and the trend in each.
- Profitability by store size or age.
- Whether the company has tested larger store formats, and with what results.
- Detailed labor-contract terms.
