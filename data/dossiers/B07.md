# Subject dossier: Tolland Stores, Inc.

**As of:** early in Year 0, shortly after the close of fiscal Year −1. Everything below was known or knowable on that date. Nothing after it is included.
**Subject type:** Business (publicly listed discount retailer)

## Overview
Tolland is a discount department-store chain in the U.S. Northeast. It was founded nearly 30 years ago by members of one family and a partner, in a former mill building in a small New England town. Its founding idea was to bring discount retailing to rural and small-town areas that had no large competitor nearby. It was one of the first companies to stake its future on that idea.

Tolland's model rests on:
- everyday low prices on everything, rather than marked-up prices with occasional sales or a few loss-leader items;
- targeted direct-mail advertising to nearby shoppers;
- a broad mix of hard goods (about half of sales), housewares and apparel;
- a performance-driven culture: store managers review weekly scorecards early every Monday morning, and the company invests in information systems.

The company has learned to beat **Rival K**, a large national discount chain, in head-to-head competition in its markets. Its closest analogue is **Rival W**, a discounter founded a few years later on a similar rural model in the mid-South.

## Financial position
- The company has been profitable every year since its founding. Five years ago it reported eight straight years of rising sales and earnings, with sales of about $400 million.
- **Year −2:** sales rose more than 25% to $822 million, and earnings rose 43% to $28.5 million.
- **Year −1:** a large acquisition (below) roughly doubled annual sales, to about $1.7 billion. It also raised debt to about 80% of the company's net worth.
- A dollar invested in Tolland's stock at the start of Year −16 was worth more than 60 times as much by the end of Year −1. Since Year −13, its stock has roughly kept pace with Rival W's, and both have returned more than nine times the market.

## Market position
- Before last year, Tolland ran about 115 discount stores from northern New England to the mid-Atlantic, plus about 20 variety stores, mostly in Florida.
- Growth has come from new stores and a string of acquisitions, often of distressed chains bought cheaply:
  - Year −8: 13 former stores of a bankrupt national variety chain, for $1 million.
  - Roughly Year −7: a bankrupt regional chain of 32 stores in New York and Florida, for $38 million.
  - Two to three years ago: a bankrupt chain of 42 department stores, for $28.5 million. It came with about $98 million in tax credits.
  - **Year −1:** an old Pennsylvania-based variety and discount chain, for about $196.5 million. It added stores in 14 new states. Within three months Tolland closed or sold about 130 of the acquired chain's unprofitable stores, and it is converting the rest to the Tolland format.
- Management has said it plans growth of about 12% a year for the rest of the decade.

## Leadership

## Strategy and known problems
- **Growth by acquisition.** In about five years the company has moved from a regional rural chain to a multi-state operator. Each deal has brought stores with different formats, locations and customer habits, which Tolland must convert to its own pricing and merchandising model.
- **Balance sheet.** The latest acquisition was financed largely with debt, so leverage is much higher than before.
- **Integration.** Converting stores, systems and staff from several acquired chains is under way at the same time. The variety-store chains also give Tolland formats outside its core discount store.
- **Competition.** Rival W is expanding steadily outward from its home region, store by store. Rival K and regional discounters compete in Tolland's existing markets.
- **Open questions:** whether to keep growing mainly by buying distressed chains, or by opening new stores in rural markets; how quickly to integrate the latest acquisition; and how much more debt to take on.
