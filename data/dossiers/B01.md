# Subject dossier: Kestrel Stores, Inc.

**As of:** spring of Year 0, a few weeks after the fiscal year closed at the end of February. Everything below was known or knowable on that date. Nothing after it is included.
**Subject type:** Business (publicly listed specialty retailer)

## Overview
Kestrel is a U.S. consumer-electronics retailer. It began decades ago as a small TV and appliance seller and over the past two decades became a national chain of large "superstores" selling TVs, audio, computers, home office products, music, video games and major appliances. Its formula is wide selection, competitive prices, and knowledgeable salespeople ("sales counselors") who are paid on commission. Its distribution and store-operations systems are well regarded in the industry.

The company also owns a second business: a chain of used-car superstores that applies the same big-box, no-haggle retail approach to used vehicles. It started that venture seven years ago.

## Financial position
- **Year −1 (fiscal year just ended):** total net sales rose 17% to $12.61 billion (prior year $10.81 billion). Earnings from continuing operations rose 55% to $327.8 million. Net earnings were $197.6 million after charges for a discontinued venture (see below).
- **Electronics business alone:** sales rose 13% to $10.60 billion. Comparable-store sales rose 8%, and 34 superstores were added.
- Management forecasts earnings growth of 20–25% in the coming year.
- The electronics tracking stock reached an all-time high of about $54 in the past year, up from a low of about $14 the year before.
- Under the current CEO, sales have grown from about $1 billion to $12.6 billion and the store base from 69 to more than 600. The company employs more than 60,000 people.
- Long-run returns to shareholders have been far above the market for well over a decade.
- [Return on equity, profit per store and gross margin trends are not available in this dossier.]

## Market position
- 571 electronics superstores at year end. The company plans about 25 new superstores this year and has said the U.S. market can hold about 800.
- **Rival B**, a Midwest-based electronics superstore chain, overtook Kestrel in total sales about four years ago. It now leads Kestrel in sales, earnings, profit per store and national market share. About a decade ago Rival B dropped commissioned selling for a lower-pressure, self-service model. Since then it has redesigned its store format at least twice.
- Kestrel still sells through commissioned sales counselors.
- Major appliances are about 14% of store sales. Large home-improvement chains have entered the category aggressively and started price battles.

## Leadership
- **CEO:** joined 18 years ago, has been CEO for 14 years and chairman for 6. A business-magazine profile five years ago praised him for growth of more than 20% a year. He has announced he will hand the CEO role to his successor this summer and remain chairman.
- **CEO-designate:** an insider who joined 13 years ago and has been president and chief operating officer for three years.
- The executive who led the company's original transformation into a superstore chain retired from management years ago.

## Strategy and known problems
- **Growth targets.** Four years ago, management said it aimed to more than double revenue to about $15 billion by Year 0 and to reach 800 superstores. Total sales in the year just ended were $12.6 billion, including the used-car business.
- **Used-car venture.** It grew from nothing to about $510 million in revenue within four years. Three years ago the company sold 25% of the venture to the public through a tracking stock, raising about $415 million, with a goal of more than 80 stores within five years. It then lost $23.5 million in Year −2, and expansion was halted last year. Its tracking stock fell from a high of $13.50 to a low of about $1.30.
- **Disc-rental format venture.** Kestrel developed a pay-per-view DVD system: viewers bought a cheap disc and paid to unlock it for viewing. It invested about $233 million in development and promotion. Few other retailers carried it, and it was shut down last June. Charges in the year just ended were a $114 million after-tax loss on disposal plus a $16 million operating loss, on top of $68.5 million in operating losses the year before.
- **Management confidence.** In an investor interview about 18 months ago, the CEO acknowledged investor concern that the two ventures were taking management attention away from the electronics business. He answered by pointing to 44% earnings growth in the core and said the company was in great shape.
- **Store program.** For the coming year the company plans to test 30–35 remodeled stores devoted to electronics and home office, and six stand-alone appliance stores. Open questions for management include how much to invest in remodeling the existing store base, whether to stay in appliances, and whether commissioned selling still fits a market where Rival B's self-service model has been gaining share.
