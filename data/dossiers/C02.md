# Subject dossier: Meridian Surgical Corporation

**As of:** early in Year 0. Everything below was known or knowable on that date. Nothing after it is included.
**Subject type:** Business (medical-device manufacturer)

## Overview
Meridian Surgical is a Northeastern maker of surgical instruments, founded about 13 years ago. Its core product is a surgical stapler: a metal instrument loaded with disposable staple cartridges, used to close and join tissue during operations. The design lets surgeons clamp tissue before cutting it, which reduces blood loss. At the time of founding, surgeons in the U.S. closed internal tissue with thread sutures. Staplers of this kind were used mainly in Eastern Europe.

The founder saw a foreign stapler at a patent broker's office, built a wooden prototype in his basement, and spent about $75,000 of his own savings on a metal version. He incorporated the company with four employees. After two surgeons at a leading teaching hospital endorsed the device, he raised about $2 million from two outside investors. In its first three years, the company refined the stapler and developed other surgical instruments so that it could spread its marketing costs across several products.

## Financial position
- First sales came about 10 years ago and totaled about $350,000.
- About 8 years ago, sales reached about $1 million, but the company lost about $1 million that year. Many surgeons had bought the stapler but were not using it. The business model depends on repeat sales of disposable staple cartridges, not on the instrument itself.
- Since then, sales have grown rapidly. [Sales, earnings and margins for recent years are not available in this dossier.]

## Market position
- Meridian has had the surgical-staple market largely to itself since it began selling. About 8 years ago, about 20,000 patients a year had stapled operations. That number has grown rapidly since.
- Staplers are used mainly in abdominal and thoracic surgery. The company also sells skin (external) staples for wound closure.
- The main alternative is conventional suturing, which surgeons already know and which their training is built around.
- Products were first sold through independent surgical-supply wholesalers, and independent distributors still handle part of the business.
- [Patent position, market size and the plans of large suture makers are not available in this dossier.]

## Leadership
- **The founder and chief executive** has a high-school education and no medical training. Before starting the company he owned a small, unsuccessful business making dry-cleaning equipment. He is known for a strong personal interest in gadgets and biology. He has run the company since its founding.
- The company's culture is described as high-pressure.

## Strategy and known problems
- **Getting surgeons to use the product.** Adoption depends on training surgeons and operating-room staff, which the company does itself. About 10 years ago it hired 20 registered nurses as technical instructors. That worked but cost too much. It then hired medics leaving the army, who had medical backgrounds but proved to be poor salespeople.
- **The current sales model.** About 5 years ago the company began hiring experienced salespeople and training them medically: a 240-hour course plus 40 hours of surgery in an animal lab, at a cost of about $8,000 per salesperson. Salespeople are paid on commission only, and poor performers are let go quickly.
- **Channels.** The company sells through a mix of independent distributors and its own medically trained representatives.
- **Product concentration.** The product line is centered on one technology: stapling instruments and their disposable cartridges.
