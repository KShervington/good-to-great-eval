# Subject dossier: Ironbridge Steel Corporation

**As of:** spring of Year 0. Everything below was known or knowable on that date. Nothing after it is included.
**Subject type:** Business (publicly listed manufacturer)

## Overview
Ironbridge is one of the two largest U.S. steel producers, and has been for most of the century. It began about 120 years ago as an iron-rail maker in an eastern Pennsylvania river town, where its headquarters still is. A famous steel financier built it into a national producer early in the century, largely through acquisitions of other mills and shipyards. Its shipyards built more ships than any other builder during the Second World War, when it employed about 300,000 people.

Today it is a fully integrated steelmaker: it owns iron-ore mines, coal mines, ore ships and railroads, and runs coke ovens, blast furnaces, steel furnaces and rolling mills. Its main plants are:
- the original works at its headquarters town;
- a very large tidewater plant on the mid-Atlantic coast;
- older plants in western Pennsylvania and in upstate New York on the Great Lakes;
- the newest plant, on the southern shore of Lake Michigan, built in stages starting about 13 years ago. About nine years ago the company announced a $500 million expansion there to win Midwest sales.

It also makes structural shapes for bridges and buildings, plate, sheet and tin plate, and still builds ships.

## Financial position
- About 18 years ago, at the postwar peak, the company produced more than 19 million tons of steel and earned $190 million on $2.6 billion of sales. Workforce at the end of that decade: about 165,000 full-time employees.
- In the 1960s a new chairman launched a $3 billion modernization and expansion program.
- Industry profits hit records last year. Government price controls on steel ended last spring, and by year-end prices averaged about 40% higher than at the start of the year. Customers also built up inventories, which inflated demand.
- Ironbridge's first-quarter profit this year was up 86% on the year before.
- But demand is now falling fast. Auto, appliance and construction customers are cutting orders. Many U.S. mills are running at about 78–80% of capacity, and layoffs are spreading. One industry analyst expects U.S. shipments this year to fall about 25% from last year. The largest U.S. steelmaker's chairman has warned that profits will shrink for the rest of this year and possibly into next.

## Market position
- **Imports:** About 15 years ago the U.S. began importing more steel than it exported, for the first time. Japanese and European mills, rebuilt after the war with newer equipment, are offering steel to U.S. buyers well below U.S. prices. Six years ago the government persuaded Japanese and European producers to cut their U.S. shipments voluntarily by about 25%.
- **Minimills:** A new class of small producers melts scrap in electric-arc furnaces instead of making iron from ore. They make simple products such as bars at much lower cost and are taking share in the bar market from integrated producers, including Ironbridge. One of them, **Rival K**, a former conglomerate in the Southeast, now runs three such mills plus a chain of joist plants. Minimills cannot yet make flat-rolled sheet.

## Leadership
- Leadership is drawn from long-serving company insiders. The company has had four presidents in the past 15 years. The current chairman took office last year after four years as president.
- Two years ago the company finished a new 21-story headquarters tower in its home town, named after a former chairman and costing about $18.5 million. It has a cross-shaped floor plan, which creates more corner and window offices. Decorators from New York fitted it with fine wood furniture, hand-woven carpets and doorknobs bearing the company logo. Each floor houses a different department.
- The company has a long history of very high executive pay: a bonus dispute in the 1930s led it to begin disclosing executive bonuses to shareholders.

## Strategy and known problems
- **Labor:** The steelworkers' union represents the hourly workforce. Two years ago the union and ten major producers signed an agreement giving up strikes in favor of binding arbitration, in exchange for a guaranteed floor under each settlement (reported as at least 3% a year in wages). Company histories cite high wages as a drag on profits and cash reserves.
- **Costs:** Many plants are old. Integrated mills need large, lumpy capital spending to stay competitive, and new environmental rules require pollution-control spending.
- **Innovation:** Observers note that the company does little product research, and that it has relied on price increases rather than cost reduction to protect profits.
- **Mines:** Its iron mine in eastern Pennsylvania was closed two years ago after storm flooding.
- Inside the industry the debate is whether to (a) invest heavily in modernizing the big integrated plants, (b) push for stronger government limits on imports, or (c) cut capacity. Ironbridge has not announced a choice.
