# Subject dossier: Brennard Steel Corporation

**As of:** early in Year 0, a few weeks after the close of the last fiscal year. Everything below was known or knowable on that date. Nothing after it is included.
**Subject type:** Business (publicly listed steel producer)

## Overview
Brennard makes steel in electric-arc "minimills" that melt scrap metal, rather than in blast furnaces fed with iron ore and coke. About 30 years ago the company was a struggling holding company close to insolvency. The current chairman took charge then and moved the head office into a small rented space. Shortly afterwards the company built its first mill to supply its own steel-joist business, and that business is still part of the group.

Today Brennard runs bar mills, a structural-beam mill (a joint venture with a Japanese steelmaker), and flat-rolled sheet mills. It also makes joists and deck, cold-finished bars, grinding balls, fasteners, bearings and metal buildings, all from steel. About six years ago it became the first company to make flat-rolled sheet in a minimill using thin-slab casting, a technology the large integrated steelmakers had chosen not to adopt. A second thin-slab sheet mill opened about three and a half years ago. A third sheet mill is being built for roughly $300 million and is scheduled to start early next year.

## Financial position
- Net sales rose from $1.47 billion four years ago to $3.46 billion last year. Net earnings rose from $65 million to a record $275 million over the same period.
- Return on average equity was 21.9% last year (22.4% the year before). Four years ago it was 9.5%.
- Long-term debt is $107 million, against stockholders' equity of $1.38 billion.
- Steel sold to outside customers reached 6.7 million tons last year, 13% more than the year before and more than double the figure four years ago.
- Capital spending was $263 million last year. More than $500 million is planned for this year.
- The share price rose above $70 two years ago, fell to the low $40s during last year, and stood at about $58 at the end of last month.

## Market position
- Brennard competes with integrated steelmakers, other minimills and imports. Analysts and the company describe competition as heavy.
- Other companies have announced about ten new minimills due within roughly three years. Together they would raise industry capacity by about 40%.
- During last year, orders for sheet fell and Brennard cut its sheet prices.
- Scrap is the main raw material. Scrap prices jumped 55% two years ago. The average cost was about $155 per gross ton last year, against about $145 the year before. As more minimills open, demand for high-quality scrap will rise.
- In fasteners, the market is dominated by foreign suppliers.

## Leadership
- **Chairman:** has run the company for about 30 years. This month he gave up the chief executive role and remains chairman.
- **New chief executive:** a long-time insider who was president and chief operating officer. He has said publicly that his aim is to keep the company running the way it has been run.
- **Board:** made up mainly of current and former company executives. The former president, who retired about five years ago, is a director and one of the largest individual shareholders.
- **Organization:** fewer than 25 people work at headquarters. There are four levels of management from the chief executive to the shop floor, and plant general managers run their divisions with wide autonomy.
- **Pay and employment:** 6,200 employees. Production teams earn weekly bonuses tied to output, which can more than double base pay. The company has never laid off a worker for lack of work. In downturns it shortens hours instead.

## Strategy and known problems
- **Long-standing policies:** the company grows by building new plants from scratch with new technology, not by buying older mills. It stays in steel and steel products, and operates only in its home country.
- **Iron-making venture:** two years ago Brennard finished a $65 million plant on a Caribbean island to make iron carbide, a scrap substitute made from iron ore and natural gas, with capacity of about 320,000 tons a year. It is the first commercial use of a process previously proven only in pilot plants. It was finished a year late and about 30% over budget, and it has been shut twice for repairs since start-up, mainly for mechanical problems with auxiliary equipment. Management says such problems are to be expected. The rationale is to reduce dependence on scrap.
- **Safety:** a ladle accident killed a worker at the first sheet mill six years ago, and the company was fined by the safety regulator.
- **Questions the company faces this year:**
  - how much new capacity to build while rivals add about 40%;
  - whether, and how far, to expand into making its own iron;
  - how to keep its historic growth rate as its model is copied;
  - whether its culture will hold up under the first change of chief executive in three decades.
