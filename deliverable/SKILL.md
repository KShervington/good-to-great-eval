# Good to Great: Anything

This skill turns a subject that is already *good* into a concrete plan for making it *great*. It uses the framework from Jim Collins's *Good to Great*, plus two of his follow-ups: the *Social Sectors* monograph, which adapts the ideas for non-business subjects, and *How the Mighty Fall*, which covers decline risks.

The subject passes through a fixed series of gates. Each gate produces a written artifact and a verdict. The final output is the great version of the subject: its Hedgehog Concept, its flywheel, who it needs, what it stops doing, and a scorecard to measure progress.

Principle: good is the enemy of great. Most subjects stay good because good is comfortable. Every gate should leave the subject sharper and narrower, not just add more to it. The exception is a subject that is already great: there the job is to protect what works, not to invent change.

---

## How to run it

1. **Gather evidence first.** Before Gate 0, search memory and past chats for the subject, including any earlier good-to-great run on it. If one exists, use its scorecard as the new baseline. Then ask **one batch of at most five questions** covering what the gates need most: the hard numbers for Gate 1, the key people or commitments for Gate 3, and the uncomfortable fact the user already suspects for Gate 4. Don't ask questions gate by gate. A long chain of questions stalls the run, especially on a phone.
2. **Pick a mode.**
   - **Interview (default when the user is present):** after the evidence batch, pause only at Gate 5, to test the Hedgehog Concept candidates with the user before building on them.
   - **Autonomous (when the user says "just run it", is away, or leaves the evidence batch unanswered):** infer from what you have, research where useful, and mark every inference `[ASSUMPTION]` so the user can correct it.
3. **Run the gates in order, at two depths.** Collins's sequence matters: people come before strategy, and facts come before the Hedgehog Concept. Don't skip ahead to the Hedgehog Concept just because it's the interesting part. The **core gates** (0, 1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8) get full treatment every time, because that's where the narrowing happens. The **light gates** (2, 7, 9, 10) get a short paragraph plus their artifact, unless the subject makes one of them central.
4. **Give each gate a verdict:**
   - `PASS`: proceed.
   - `CONDITIONAL`: proceed, but log the open issue in the final Risk Register. Every CONDITIONAL must name its specific condition and the evidence that would turn it into a PASS. If more than half the gates come out CONDITIONAL, say so plainly. The cause is usually one or two shared root problems, so name them.
   - `FAIL`: stop and resolve the issue, or loop back to an earlier gate. Fails at Gates 0 and 1 end the run with an explanation.
5. **Translate the vocabulary** to fit the subject type (see the table below). Don't force business language onto a career or a band.
6. **Synthesize** the final deliverable (see Output). When the session offers a Docs artifact type, deliver it as a doc. Otherwise, use a well-structured markdown file.

### Vocabulary map

| Concept | Business | Product / app | Nonprofit / social | Team / org unit | Personal (career, craft) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Leader | CEO / founder | Product owner | Executive director | Team lead | You |
| The bus | Employees, execs | Contributors, key partners | Staff, board, volunteers | Members | Collaborators, mentors, network, *your own commitments* |
| Economic engine | Profit per X | Revenue or retention per X | **Resource engine**: time, money and brand | Budget and headcount leverage | Income, reputation or opportunity per unit of effort |
| Great means | Sustained outperformance of the market | Category-defining, durable usage | Distinctive impact, relative to resources | Indispensable output | Best-in-world at a narrow thing that sustains you |

## The gates

### Gate 0: Fit (is this a subject the framework can work on?)
Check that the subject:
- is an **ongoing endeavor**, not a one-off artifact. ("My novel" fits poorly. "My writing career" fits well.)
- has a **steward**: someone who can make decisions about it.
- has **people or resources** that can be rearranged.
- has a **time horizon of years**, so compounding can happen.
- has some way to **measure outcomes**.

A reusable tool, skill, template or process counts as an ongoing endeavor when it is used repeatedly and someone maintains it. Its outcomes are the decisions and results it produces for its users.

If the subject is a one-off artifact, suggest reframing it as the endeavor behind it. If that isn't possible, `FAIL` and recommend a better-suited method (for example, a normal editing or critique pass).

**Artifact:** a subject card covering the name, type (from the map above), steward, time horizon and current stage.

### Gate 1: Baseline (is it actually good, and what would great mean?)
The framework assumes a working foundation. Establish:
- **Evidence of good:** what already works, backed by numbers or concrete outcomes.
- **Definition of great:** three tests, adapted from the *Social Sectors* monograph:
  1. **Superior performance** against its mission or market.
  2. **Distinctive impact:** if it vanished, it would leave a hole that couldn't easily be filled.
  3. **Lasting endurance:** it would stay great beyond one person, one product or one lucky break.
- **Two or three measurable outputs** that will make up the scorecard.

Then place the subject in one of three states, and say which:
- **Not yet good:** the core doesn't work yet without the steward's personal effort, network or manual workarounds (for example: most users are friends, retention is unproven outside that circle, the key feature is hand-operated). `FAIL` gently. Don't soften this to `CONDITIONAL`: Gates 2–10 would be built on sand. Say what "good" would look like and the two or three milestones that would prove it. This state is for a core that has never worked on its own.
- **Good, not yet great:** the normal case. Run the gates as written. A subject that was once good and is now in crisis (mounting losses, lost share, a funding squeeze) belongs here, not in "not yet good": run the gates as a turnaround, with the brutal facts and survival actions first.
- **Already great:** the evidence shows all three tests of great have held for years. Say so plainly, then switch to **stewardship mode**. Gate 5 confirms the Hedgehog Concept the subject already lives by instead of inventing a new one. Gate 6's stop-doing list names only the real temptations to refuse, with no minimum count. Recommendations are few and protect the flywheel. Gate 10 becomes the center of the analysis. Don't manufacture weaknesses to justify a long plan.

**Artifact:** a baseline sheet and a draft definition of great.

### Gate 2: Level 5 Leadership
Assess the steward against Collins's Level 5 profile: personal humility combined with fierce resolve for the cause.
- **Window and mirror:** when things go well, do they credit others and luck? When things go badly, do they look at themselves first?
- Is their ambition directed at the **subject**, or at their **own status**?
- Would the subject survive their absence? Have they set up successors to succeed?
- **For personal subjects:** the user is the leader, so frame this as self-audit questions, not judgment.

Verdict tips: ego-driven leadership is `CONDITIONAL`, logged as a risk. This gate never fails outright, because leaders can grow.

**Artifact:** a leadership read covering strengths, gaps and one concrete behavior change.

### Gate 3: First Who, Then What
The right people come before direction.
- **Right people:** they share the core values, need no tight management, and own outcomes instead of tasks.
- **On the bus / off the bus / wrong seat:** classify the key people, partners or commitments. For personal subjects, this includes collaborators, advisors, clients, and recurring obligations that eat capacity.
- **Rigor, not ruthlessness:** when in doubt, don't hire. When a change is needed, act. Put the best people on the biggest opportunities, not the biggest problems.
- Identify the **missing seats** the great version will need.

`FAIL` if a key seat is filled by someone fundamentally misaligned and the user won't address it. Strategy built on top of that will not hold.

**Artifact:** a bus roster (keep / move seat / off / missing).

### Gate 4: Confront the Brutal Facts (the Stockdale Paradox)
Keep two things at once: unwavering faith that you will prevail in the end, and the discipline to face the harshest facts of current reality.
- List the **brutal facts**: competitive threats, weak metrics, uncomfortable truths, things everyone knows but doesn't say. Push past the first polite answers. Do web research for market or competitor facts where relevant.
- Check the **truth climate**: lead with questions, not answers; debate, don't coerce; conduct blameless autopsies; set up red-flag mechanisms that make ignored information impossible to ignore.
- **Charisma check:** is the steward's force of personality suppressing bad news?
- Write the **faith statement**: why the subject will prevail anyway.

`FAIL` if a fact surfaced here contradicts Gate 1. Loop back and redo the baseline.

**Artifact:** a brutal-facts register (fact / evidence / severity / owner) plus a one-line faith statement.

### Gate 5: The Hedgehog Concept
Find the intersection of three circles. This gate usually takes the most iteration.
1. **What can it be best in the world at?** Equally important: what can it *not* be best at? Being good at something, or even core competence, is not enough. It must be genuinely best-in-world potential, in a narrow enough world.
2. **What drives its economic or resource engine?** Find the single denominator: "profit per ___" (or impact, income, or retention per ___). For non-business subjects, use the resource engine: time, money and brand.
3. **What are its people deeply passionate about?**

Process:
- Draft several candidates for each circle, then narrow them.
- Test each intersection candidate: Does it explain past wins? Does it rule things out? Would it be uncomfortable to commit to?
- Write the Hedgehog Concept as **one simple sentence**. Simple doesn't mean simplistic; it should come from deep understanding.
- Collins's companies often took years to arrive at their Hedgehog Concept. Mark this one as a working hypothesis, along with what evidence would confirm it.

`FAIL` if there's no real intersection, for example if "best at" is only good enough or the engine doesn't connect. Loop back to Gate 3 or Gate 4, or decide this subject can only be good.

**Artifact:** a three-circle table, the denominator, the Hedgehog Concept sentence, and confirmation tests.

### Gate 6: Culture of Discipline
Disciplined people + disciplined thought + disciplined action = freedom within a framework.
- **Stop-doing list:** everything outside the Hedgehog Concept, including attractive opportunities. This list matters more than the to-do list. Be specific, and aim for at least five items (in stewardship mode, list only the real temptations).
- **Freedom and responsibility:** what's the framework, and where do people have autonomy inside it?
- **Bureaucracy check:** which rules exist only to compensate for having the wrong people on the bus?
- **Fanatical consistency:** what does the subject do relentlessly, every time?

**Artifact:** a stop-doing list, a list of non-negotiable disciplines, and the framework boundaries.

### Gate 7: Technology Accelerators
Technology accelerates momentum; it does not create it.
- For each current or proposed technology or tool, classify it as: **accelerates the Hedgehog Concept**, **neutral or parity** (just keep up), or **irrelevant** (drop).
- Flag any **fear- or hype-driven adoption**.
- Name one or two technologies where the subject could become a **pioneer in applying** them.

**Artifact:** a tech triage table.

### Gate 8: The Flywheel (and the Doom Loop)
Greatness comes from consistent pushes on a coherent loop, not from one big launch.
- Map the flywheel as **4–7 components** in a causal loop. Each one drives the next, and the last one feeds back into the first. Include the Hedgehog Concept's denominator.
- Identify the **current push**: the next one or two turns that matter most.
- **Doom-loop check:** look for signs of reaction without understanding, new direction after new direction, big programs or acquisitions to jump-start momentum, or inconsistency.
- Where possible, draw the flywheel as a diagram.

`FAIL` if the loop doesn't close. That usually means the Hedgehog Concept is wrong, so loop back to Gate 5.

**Artifact:** a flywheel diagram, a description of the current push, and the results of the doom-loop check.

### Gate 9: Built to Last (making the greatness endure)
From Collins's *Built to Last*: preserve the core, stimulate progress.
- **Core values:** three to five values that won't change.
- **Core purpose:** why it exists, beyond making money.
- **BHAG:** a big, hairy, audacious goal with a 10–30 year horizon that comes out of the Hedgehog Concept. For personal subjects, or subjects with a short horizon, keep the BHAG to one line or skip it, and say which you did.
- **Clock building, not time telling:** which mechanisms make this work without the current steward?

**Artifact:** core ideology (values and purpose), the BHAG, and a list of mechanisms.

### Gate 10: How the Mighty Fall (stress test)
Check the great version against Collins's five stages of decline:
1. Hubris born of success.
2. Undisciplined pursuit of more.
3. Denial of risk and peril.
4. Grasping for salvation.
5. Capitulation to irrelevance or death.

For each stage, name the specific way this subject could slide into it, and the early warning sign to watch for.

**Artifact:** a decline-risk register.

## Output: the great version

Lead with the result, not the process. **Open the reply with a one-screen verdict** that reads well on a phone. It should give the Hedgehog Concept sentence, the weakest link in the flywheel, the top three stop-doing items, the single most urgent brutal fact, and which missing numbers would change the answer, plus one line on how confident the verdict is and what it rests on. Keep `[ASSUMPTION]` tags on any figure in the verdict that isn't in the evidence. In stewardship mode, open with the already-great verdict and replace the stop-doing items with the temptations to refuse. Keep the verdict under about 150 words. Leave out score rationales, gate tallies and method notes: they belong in the gate log. The full deliverable has these sections:

1. **The great version in one paragraph:** what the subject looks like when it's great.
2. **The Hedgehog Concept:** the sentence, the three circles and the denominator.
3. **The flywheel:** a diagram plus the current push.
4. **The bus:** people moves (keep / move seat / off / missing).
5. **Stop-doing list.**
6. **Brutal facts and the faith statement.**
7. **Core ideology and the BHAG.**
8. **Scorecard:** two or three outputs from Gate 1, with current value, 12-month target and BHAG trajectory.
9. **Next 90 days:** three to five actions, each with an owner, all pushing the flywheel.
10. **Re-check:** offer once to set up a scheduled task about 90 days out. It should re-score the scorecard with real numbers and re-run Gates 1, 5 and 8. Without that loop, the skill never finds out whether its verdicts were right.
11. **Risk register:** every `CONDITIONAL` item plus the decline risks from Gate 10.
12. **Gate log:** one line per gate with its verdict. Keep this short. Count the verdicts from the table itself before you write any summary of them.

## Guardrails

- **Be honest, not flattering.** The framework works only when it narrows things down. If the user's favorite idea falls outside the Hedgehog Concept, say so.
- **Don't invent metrics.** Ask, research, or mark the figure `[ASSUMPTION]`.
- **Paraphrase Collins in your own words.** Don't reproduce passages from the books.
- **Collins's caveats apply.** His method was retrospective, and some of the companies he named as great later declined. Present the output as disciplined hypotheses to test, not guarantees.
- **Evidence beats coverage.** A run with real numbers on the core gates is worth more than a complete run built on assumptions.
- **Keep people decisions humane.** "Off the bus" means the person or commitment is in the wrong role for this endeavor. It is not a judgment of their worth.
